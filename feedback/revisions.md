## Chapter Three: Under Canvas

ORIGINAL:
> Cheap, and it still took very nearly every silver I could put a hand to. That is a different thing altogether from everything I had, as I have learned since. Penworth spent the winter turning my affairs over in his careful hands to see what they would bear, and what they bore was a great deal on paper and almost nothing in coin. The river house was the most of my worth, and the river house would not move quickly; a grand house in a great city is a slow animal, and that one stood on ground I had never owned and carried a debt I had signed for in a better year, so that when it went at last it would come to me in two lump sums, one the buyer's deposit to secure the sale and one to come at a later date out of his lender. Rather than sit and wait on it, Penworth borrowed against it: a short loan out of a bank that knew the street the house stood in and cared nothing whatever for the country I was going to, the whole of it to fall due when the sale cleared, and a sale of that size clears at the pace of its lawyers, which Penworth priced, as he priced everything, at very nearly a year. Set beside that were the old patents, which paid their steady quarterly pittance whether I attended to them or not, and my father's firm, which went on paying its partner's share into an account I never opened. My father's house did not appear in the columns at all. Penworth knew better than to put it there, and I would not have signed the page it was on. He ruled it out for me in a letter, in neat columns, and at the foot of the columns stood a sum aimed like a spent arrow at a hill nobody wanted, and the loosing of it left me holding five thousand acres and next to no money, a condition I had no name for then and have never since been entirely out of. That was the mercy in the thing, and the whole of the trap. So nothing at all fell due until the place had been given a full turn of the seasons to find its feet, which was not generosity but conveyancing, and it meant that in a year I would need this hill to be earning, one way or another. The farmland in the bottoms would have to be got into the hands of tenants who would work it and pay for the working. The trade would have to come. A wiser man would have looked at that year the way you look at a lit fuse, and been frightened. I looked at it and felt, for the first time in three years, interested. I have learned to trust that in myself further than I trust my caution, though it has cost me nearly as often as it has paid.

## Chapter Four: Ask for Passage

ORIGINAL:
> I want to be plain about what I did, because it matters to everything after. I drafted it the way I had drafted a hundred workings before it, the way any competent man in my trade would have drafted it, which is to say I drafted it to win. A reshaping ward is a large and expensive thing, and it does what its name says: it takes hold of the deep layer of a place, the slow shifting stone under the soil that no spade ever reaches, and it tells that layer what shape to keep. You set your anchors at the corners and you feel down for the deep grain and you bind it, and thereafter the ground holds the footing you have given it because it no longer has any say in the matter. It is a fair-mason's working, if a mason could reach that far down. It is exactly what Cael's father did with his four square walls and his stubbornness, only I could reach the layer his mortar never could, and I did not doubt for a moment that I could make it obey.

## Chapter Six: First Light

ORIGINAL:
> The collar was Tomas's, and the man deserves more than a word here. Most smithies are a racket, and a good part of the racket is put on for the customer, the way a market-seller cries his wares; a smith who lets you watch him work is half performing, and the work suffers for it. Tomas did not perform. When I brought him the drawing of the collar he looked at it a long while and said nothing, and then he looked at me the same way, and asked the one real question of the whole commission. "What's it to hold?" I told him a light that did not burn. He took that in with no more surprise than if I had said a candle, and he made the thing, and it took him the better part of a week for a piece of brass a tinker would have knocked out in an afternoon, and when it was done it was so exactly the shape the glass wanted and no other that I understood he had made it not to my drawing but to the thing the drawing was reaching for, which is a gift I have met only a handful of times, and never in a man who spoke so little. He named a fair price and I paid it, and as I gathered it up he said one more thing, or the start of one. "That hill," he said, banking his fire and not looking at me. And then he stopped, the way a man stops at the head of a stair he has decided not to go down, and said only, "Comes on to winter early, up there. Mind it does," which was true, and was not, I thought, quite what he had begun to say. I said I would, and carried the collar home.
