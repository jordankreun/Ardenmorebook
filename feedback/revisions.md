## Chapter One: Ardenmoor

ORIGINAL:
> I had never meant to stay in it long, for that matter. Coming home to my father's house was to be a stopgap, a place to keep myself and my boots while my own house went up across the river; and my own was to be a wonder, or a warning, depending on which of us you asked about it afterward: grander than anything my father would have put his name to, taller, flashier, altogether more foolish, the same clean lines he had given my mother swollen to twice the scale and three times the cost, a great bright thing built to stand as a gift and a grand gesture ahead of a wedding. I had drawn a good part of it myself, in the good years, when I still supposed I knew what I was building toward. There was no wedding, in the end, and so no need of the house. I never lived in it. For three years it had stood across the water full of a life that had not happened, costing me a small fortune a year in the bare keeping of a grand thing that nobody used, and I could no more sell it than cross its threshold. Penworth had spent the better part of that winter trying, with a delicacy that cost him something to keep up, to talk me round to letting it go, for the sake of my purse and, though he was too careful a man to say it, for the sake of the rest of me; and I had spent the winter not answering him.

> The first and easier part of it I had come by rail: three days out of Vethmark in a carriage that rattled the teeth in a man's head and let the cold in at every seam. The railways were new then, and it showed. The engine that hauled us was a crude, soot-breathing thing, all iron and steam and appetite, and there was next to nothing of the arcane in it, which surprised the sort of people who supposed a wizard must approve of a train on principle. I did not, particularly. You may put a working into a machine, and I have put a good many into a good many; but a working has to be fed and tended and coaxed and renewed, and one big enough to shoulder a train up a grade would have eaten more in the keeping than the coal it saved. That is the arithmetic people get backwards about my trade. There is no shortage of magic in this world; there has never been a shortage of magic. What the world is short of is people who can work it. The talent is born, not made. The Conclave sharpens it where it appears, and it appears seldom, and more seldom than it once did. Coal the world has by the mountain, iron by the shire, stokers by the thousand for the hiring; of those born able to set a lasting working into iron it has a scattered handful, every one of them wanted in six places at once. Put the whole of that little company to nursing locomotives and you might keep one railway arcane, and nothing else in the world would ever be warded or mended or lit again. So no sane man spends a wizard where a stoker will serve, and the railways ran on fire and water and brute iron cleverness and almost no magic at all, and I spent a fair part of those three days watching the engine hiss and labor at the watering-stops and thinking, in the idle, itching way of a man who cannot let a made thing alone, how much smaller and quieter and cleverer the whole of it could have been built, if the world had only had the hands to spare for it. It did not. It never has. That is the first hard fact of my trade, and the trains were a great smoking monument to it: a mountain of crude iron doing loudly and dearly what a very little good artifice could have done well, had there only been enough good artifice to go around, which there was not, and is not, and I have stopped expecting there ever to be.

## Prologue

ORIGINAL:
> Not that the real one was any great prize. I will not sit here in my old age and pretend the man I was in those days was wiser or gentler or one whit less of a fool than the figure in the story. God knows I had a great deal more to learn than I yet had the sense to know I was missing, and I learned the most of it the slow way, and late, and at a cost to better people than myself. But he was a man, at least, and not a monument. And if I am to leave one true thing behind me when I go, I would sooner it were him than the bright bronze figure the years have cast up in his place.
