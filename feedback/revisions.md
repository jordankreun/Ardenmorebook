## Chapter Seven: Wednesdays

ORIGINAL:
> Then the kettle. Then the work. My days that autumn were full of the small honest labor of a practice getting itself onto its feet, and I found, to a surprise that shames me a little to remember, that I was glad of the work in a way I had not been glad of anything in years. Where the wall had brought the villagers up to watch a thing fail, the light brought them up to ask for something. It could not be had, not yet, though it would be, and I told them so honestly, and in the telling I found myself doing the plain work of my trade for people who needed it: a ward mended on a barn that took the damp, a set of drawing-instruments trued for the surveyor two valleys over who had heard there was a man at Ardenmoor who understood ground, a cold-spot in Kellard's storeroom talked out of the corner it had settled in. It was not grand work. It was the work of a country wizard in a small valley, and I did it well, and I was paid fairly for it, and every job of it was also, without exception, the slow business of a village deciding a man was a neighbor and not a rumor. I have done work that clever men still argue over. I am prouder of the cold-spot in Kellard's storeroom.

## Chapter One: Ardenmoor

ORIGINAL:
> I had never meant to stay long. My father's house was a stopgap, somewhere to keep myself and my boots while my own went up across the river; and my own was to be a wonder, or a warning, depending on when you asked. It was grander than anything my father would have put his name to, taller, flashier, altogether more foolish: his clean lines swollen to thrice the scale and as many times the cost, a great bright gesture ahead of a wedding. I drew much of it myself in the good years, when I still supposed I knew what I was building toward. There was no wedding. I never lived in the house. For three years it stood across the water as a reminder of a life that had not happened, costing a small fortune to keep empty, and I could no more sell it than cross its threshold. Penworth spent that winter trying, with a delicacy that cost him something, to talk me round to letting it go, for the sake of my purse and, though he was too careful to say it, the rest of me. I did not answer.

> The first and easier part I came by rail: three days out of Vethmark in a carriage that rattled the teeth in a man's head and let the cold in at every seam. The railways were new, and it showed. The engine was a crude, soot-breathing thing, all iron and steam and appetite. Many called the train magical, though there was nothing arcane in it. You may put a working into a machine—I have put a good many into a good many—but a working must be fed, tended, and coaxed into existence. One large enough to shoulder a train up a grade would then have eaten more in the making and keeping than a lifetime of coal. That is the arithmetic people get backwards about my trade. There is no shortage of magic in the world; it is short of people who can work it. Coal the world has by the mountain, iron by the shire, stokers by the thousand. Of those born able to set a lasting working into iron it has a scattered handful, every one wanted in six places at once. Put them all to nursing locomotives and you might keep one railway arcane, while nothing else was warded, mended, or lit. So no sane man spends a wizard where a stoker will serve. The railways ran on fire, water, and brute iron cleverness. I spent much of those three days watching the engine labor at the watering-stops and thinking how much smaller and quieter it might have been. That was the first hard fact of my trade, and the trains were a great smoking monument to it: crude iron doing loudly what a little good artifice could have done well, had there been enough good artifice to go around.

## Chapter Four: Ask for Passage

ORIGINAL:
> What I was after, under all the walking and the lying still, was a plain enough thing, and it was this: I meant to build, and you cannot build on ground that will not hold. That was the whole of Cael's father's trouble, and the whole of the valley's forty years of certainty. It was not that the hill was steep, for the crown is flat as a table and always has been. It was that nothing set on it stayed set. Foundations that went down true came up crooked in a season. Mortar that cured hard everywhere else in the country stayed soft here for a month and then let go. Walls came down in the night with no wind to blame. I had heard it from Cael on the crown in the gold light, and I had felt, in the weeks since, exactly the quality of ground he meant. It was not dead ground. Dead ground is honest; it takes what you give it and keeps it. This ground was awake, and it had been throwing off everything set upon it for longer than the village had a name, shrugging off a blanket it had never asked for.
