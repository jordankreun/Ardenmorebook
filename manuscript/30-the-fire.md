# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Thirty: A Dry Month

I woke, on a night at the front of that winter, not to smoke and not to light. To Snow, screaming, which is a sound a horse makes perhaps twice in her life and a man does not mistake for anything else in this world, and I was out of the bed and into my boots with my heart already at a gallop before my head held one thought, and the thought, when it came, came in through the window with the light: an orange window, in a room with no lamp lit, at the black bottom of a winter night.

The barn was going up like a struck match.

I will not make the fire beautiful. Men who were not there have said to me since, meaning kindness, that it must have been a terrible grand sight. It was not grand. It was fast, and it was loud, which nobody tells you; a fed fire in dry timber does not crackle, it roars, one long freight-wagon roar that stands your hair up off your arms, and it was eating the only hay and the only cart and the only horse I had, and I went down that slope in my nightshirt and boots with no plan in me at all beyond the bar of her box.

I got the door. The heat off the loft end was already a wall, but the box stood at the near end, and the bar was iron, Tomas's iron, and it came free clean the way his work always came, and I had one arm over her neck and my nightshirt over her eyes, and Snow, who would not settle in that barn through two years of ordinary nights, came out of it now stiff-legged and shuddering but came, because whatever else was between us she had decided long ago on the road from Vethmark that my arm over her neck meant the thing it meant. We were two lengths clear when the loft came down into the stalls behind us with a sound like a shelf of books going over, if the books were made of light.

I got her to the paddock rail on the east slope and tied her, badly, my hands not being entirely mine, and turned around, and understood the rest of it in one look.

The wind was southwest, moderate, gusting. The house stood northeast of the barn, up the shoulder. Thirty steps of frosted grass between them, and the air over those thirty steps was not air any longer, it was a current of sparks, a river running uphill, breaking on the honey-colored boards Maret had planed and I had warded, and I will be exact: the warding held. Rot and wet and the boring beetle, those were the wards I had asked of that timber, and not one plank of that house ever rotted, and the wards took the first sparks and the second and shed them as they had shed two years of rain, and then the fire, which does not negotiate, put a coal the size of a fist under the eave where the bath-alcove met the main wall, and held it there on the wind, and heat is not a working, heat is only heat, and there is a number at which dry oak stops discussing the matter, and the house reached the number while I stood in the paddock with my hands full of a shaking horse, doing the one working I could reach at that distance, which was nothing.

I am going to say the next part once and plainly.

I did not save the house, and I could not have, and I know that the way a man of my trade knows a load will not hold, by arithmetic, not by forgiveness. What I saved, in the quarter hour the fire gave me before the roof was one flag of light, was this: I went in the north door with my coat over my mouth, and I came out three times. Once with the strongbox and the deed-papers and my working notes off the shelf. Once with the shelf of oft-read books, the whole shelf, carried flat like a stretcher. And once, the last time, with the fire already in the roof-space over my head and talking to itself in that language it has, with Soren's drawing from its place by the hearth, and the half-loaf from the articles in its cloth, and Lira's two blankets off the bed, because a man in a burning house does not choose what matters, he discovers it, and that is what my hands discovered, and I have never once been ashamed of the inventory.

Essa's bed I could not take. It does not lift in a quarter hour. I stood one breath in the doorway of the little room looking at it, the good waxed frame with the years of polish in it, the first thing I had built a life around in that valley, and the smoke came down the ceiling at me like a hand turned over, and I said something to the room that I will keep, and went out the north door the last time, and the house let me go the way the hill had always carried me, and fell in behind me before I had counted a hundred.

It was the light the village came to. There is no bell on my hill. There did not need to be.

They came up the way. I will give that exactly as it happened, because it is the fact from that night I hold above every other: they came up the dark in the hard cold, the whole strong heart of that village, and the stone way took them at a run, dry-shod, sure-footed, lamplit, the way I had laid it flag by flag for a purpose I had never once been able to name to myself, and I stood in the paddock with my horse and my armload of rescued oddments and watched a chain of lanterns come bobbing up my hill like the valley's own blood coming to a wound.

Jorin was first, Ren on his shoulder. Then Tomas, who I am told simply walked out of his house toward the glow without one word to anyone, at a pace nobody could match. Kellard with his yard-cart and every bucket that yard possessed. The Wrays, all of them, Tam from his own cottage on his own acres. Bess with bread, because Bess Wray has triage in her bones and knew before anyone that this fire would be fed and not fought. Brenna. Lira, who left the inn unlocked, which those who knew her counted the wildest act of the night. Cael came up last with Soren, an old man on a stick on a stone way in the cold dark of a winter's night, and no soul alive would have said one word to stop him.

There was no saving to do by then, and every builder in the crowd saw it in the first ten steps. So they did the other work, and they did it the way that valley did everything, without a wasted word. Buckets from the spring in a chain, not on the fires, which were past buckets, but on the grass and the fences downwind, on the Study's sills, on the margins, penning the thing into the two graves it had dug for itself. Jorin walked the line of it with a torch reading the wind like a drawing. Ren and Tam turned the paddock rails out of the sparks' road. Tomas stood between the burning house and the Study, up on the crown, watching the air the way he listened to iron, and where he pointed men wetted. And when there was nothing left to pen, they stood with me in the cold, the whole of them in a rough half-ring, and we watched my barn and my house burn themselves down into two red hearts on a black hill, and nobody said anything for a long time, because that valley knows when a thing is past talk, and then Bess Wray put bread in my hands and said, "Eat it while it's warm," and I found that I was sitting on the paddock rail with no memory of getting there, and that the bread was the best thing I had ever eaten, and that my house was gone, in the specific order a man finds such things out.

The cause was never settled. I want that on the record, plainly, because the valley in its kindness spent that whole winter settling it in six different directions on my behalf, and because I have gone over the facts of that night more times than any accounting in this valley, and they assemble into no more than they are. It had been a dry month, for early winter. The wind was from the southwest, moderate, gusting. I had banked the main-room fire at the usual hour and shut the stove as I had shut it a thousand nights, and I would swear to the shutting before any bench in the Commonwealth, and no man has ever asked me to. The barn held hay in the loft, where the storehouse's filling had left it, and straw in the stalls, and the cart, and the winter stores that had not yet gone down to the new cold store, and every stick of it was timber, quick honest carpentry run up cheap in a fortnight under the note, two winters dry. And Snow would never settle in that barn. From the first night, she had never once stood easy in it, and I had put it down to the hill's hours, and I had been, in the way a man is about the things he has decided not to look at, content with that. So: the flue, said some; a flue will hold a spark the way a sinner holds a grudge, and a dry month, and a gusting wind. A tramp's fire in the barn's lee, said others, though no tramp had been seen on the roads since the frost. Hay put up too green in a wet week and mellowing toward its own heat, said the older farmers, who had seen barns go that way, and this was the theory I gave Penworth in my letter because it asked the least of everyone. My own lamps do not burn; that much the whole valley said for me before I could say it, in the way of a character witness, and it mattered to me out of all proportion. The hill was blameless; I will say that too, flatly: whatever was under my ground wanted, it had never once in three years wanted heat, and the fire began at the barn's loft end, the farthest timber on the hill from the crown, and the hill that night did what it always did at the dusk and the dark's bottom, no more and no less, its long pulse rolling under the burning like a whale passing under a boat, indifferent, ancient, and cold. The morning never quite closed the question. Some mornings don't. I have learned to leave them open, the way you leave a barn door that has already done its worst.

What was left, when the light came up grey over the frost, was this. The Study, untouched on the crown, grey and square, my instruments and books and desk and the cellar under it, whole. The glasshouse, untouched down the slope, its panes running with melted frost on the fire side, Brenna and the keeper plant and the winter stock all safe behind their steel. The storehouse in its shelf, the lab, the lamp still burning at the bench, the harvest safe in the cold store, which is the fact that let the winter be survivable at all. The way, stone, fireproof, and now the most-trodden road on the hill. And on the southwest shoulder, two black rectangles in the white frost, one where my stores had been, one where my life had been, going up in two thin lines of smoke as straight as the smoke of the village below, as though the hill had merely acquired two more hearths.

I wintered in the Study. It had been my whole house once and it consented to be again, the lumen and the hearth and a borrowed cot, my books stacked along the wall in the beginning of an order, Lira's blankets, and under the floor, down the stair, the cellar, so that I slept that whole winter twelve feet over the corner where the hill says its word. I notice that now. I was busy then.

Of the money I will say the least the truth allows, in the manner I promised myself when the tenants taught me how. The arithmetic had gone hard again, that is all. The barn and the house were two springs of building, burned in an hour, uninsured, there being no man within a week's road who writes such paper. The glasshouse's first surplus, which had stood clean at the bottom of Penworth's column for one quarter, exactly one, went spoken-for again before the ash was cold: stores to re-buy, a horse to shelter, at Kellard's, at a neighbor's rate, meaning nearly nothing, meaning everything. Penworth wrote, in the letter where a lesser man would have said the sensible things, four sentences, and the second was *You will rebuild in the spring, in stone, and the difference in cost between stone and sorrow is one I am no longer willing to discuss*, and I read it by the Study fire and laughed for the first time since the night, one short bark I did not plan, because he had contrived to make a scolding of it, and the scolding was the tenderest paper that man ever sent me. In the spring, in stone. That was all I said of it to anyone, and it was enough, and the valley nodded and went on bringing me things.

Because that was the other half of that winter, and I will close on it rather than on the ash, because it is the truer weight. They would not stop bringing me things. Eggs at the Study door. A chair, up the way on a boy's back, no note. Firewood I had not ordered, corded against my wall by hands I never caught. Mira's bolster, again, mended where the sparks had caught it in the paddock. A hat of Cael's own, brought by Soren, with the message, verbatim, that a man who has lost his roof has no business also losing his ears. I had come to that valley three winters before with everything I owned in two wagons, wanting nothing from anyone, arranged never to need to. I ended that third winter owning, by my own count, rather less than I had come with, and I could not walk to my own door without stepping over the proof that I had never in my life been richer, and the ash smell hung in everything I owned until the thaw, and some mornings I hardly minded it, and that is the whole truth of that winter, both halves of it, and I have set them down side by side because that is how they were lived.
