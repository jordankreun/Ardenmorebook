# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Thirty: A Dry Month

I woke, on a night at the front of that winter, not to smoke and not to light. To Snow, screaming, which is a sound a horse makes perhaps twice in her life and a man does not mistake for anything else in this world, and I was out of the bed and into my boots with my heart already at a gallop before my head held one thought, and the thought, when it came, came in through the window with the light: an orange window, in a room with no lamp lit, at the black bottom of a winter night.

The barn was going up like a struck match.

I will not make the fire beautiful. Men who were not there have said to me since, meaning kindness, that it must have been a terrible grand sight. It was not grand. It was fast, and it was loud, which nobody tells you; a fed fire in dry timber does not crackle, it roars, one long freight-wagon roar that stands your hair up off your arms, and it was eating the only hay and the only cart and the only horse I had, and I went down that slope in my nightshirt and boots with no plan in me at all beyond the bar of her box.

I got the door. The heat off the loft end was already a wall, but the box stood at the near end, and the bar was iron, Tomas's iron, and it came free clean the way his work always came, and I had one arm over her neck and my nightshirt over her eyes, and Snow, who would not settle in that barn through two years of ordinary nights, came out of it now stiff-legged and shuddering but came, because whatever else was between us she had decided long ago on the road from Vethmark that my arm over her neck meant the thing it meant. We were two lengths clear when the loft came down into the stalls behind us with a sound like a shelf of books going over, if the books were made of light.

I got her to the paddock rail on the east slope and tied her, badly, my hands not being entirely mine, and turned around, and understood the rest of it in one look.

The wind was southwest, moderate, gusting. The house stood northeast of the barn, up the shoulder. Thirty steps of frosted grass between them, and the air over those thirty steps was not air any longer, it was a current of sparks, a river running uphill, breaking on the honey-colored boards Maret had planed and I had warded, and I will be exact: the warding held. Rot and wet and the boring beetle, those were the wards I had asked of that timber, and not one plank of that house ever rotted, and the wards took the first sparks and the second and shed them as they had shed two years of rain, and then the fire, which does not negotiate, put a coal the size of a fist under the eave where the bath-alcove met the main wall, and held it there on the wind, and heat is not a working, heat is only heat, and there is a number at which dry oak stops discussing the matter, and the house reached the number while I stood in the paddock with my hands full of a shaking horse, doing the one working I could reach at that distance, which was nothing.

I am going to say the next part once and plainly.

I did not save the house, and I could not have, and I know that the way a man of my trade knows a load will not hold, by arithmetic, not by forgiveness. What I saved, in the quarter hour the fire gave me before the roof was one flag of light, was this: I went in the north door with my coat over my mouth, and I came out three times. Once with the strongbox and the deed-papers and my working notes off the shelf. Once with the shelf of oft-read books, the whole shelf, carried flat like a stretcher. And once, the last time, with the fire already in the roof-space over my head and talking to itself in that language it has, with Soren's drawing from its place by the hearth, and the half-loaf from the articles in its cloth, and Lira's two blankets off the bed, because a man in a burning house does not choose what matters, he discovers it, and that is what my hands discovered. I have never once been ashamed of the inventory.

Essa's bed I could not take. It does not lift in a quarter hour. I stood one breath in the doorway of the little room looking at it, the good waxed frame with the years of polish in it, the first thing I had built a life around in that valley, and the smoke came down the ceiling at me like a hand turned over, and I said something to the room that I will keep, and went out the north door the last time, and the house let me go the way the hill had always carried me, and fell in behind me before I had counted a hundred.

It was the light the village came to. There is no bell on my hill. There did not need to be.

They came up the way. I will give that exactly as it happened, because it is the fact from that night I hold above every other: they came up the dark in the hard cold, the whole strong heart of that village, and the stone way took them at a run, dry-shod, sure-footed, lamplit, the way I had laid it flag by flag for a purpose I had never once been able to name to myself, and I stood in the paddock with my horse and my armload of rescued oddments and watched a chain of lanterns come bobbing up my hill like the valley's own blood coming to a wound.

Jorin was first, Ren on his shoulder. He looked at the barn for about as long as it takes to read a word, and at the house for longer, and then at me.

"Anyone in?"

"No."

"Horse?"

"Out. Paddock."

"Right," he said, and turned round and began giving the hill its orders, and I did not give another one all night, and was glad of it. Then Tomas, who I am told simply walked out of his house toward the glow without one word to anyone, at a pace nobody could match. Kellard with his yard-cart and every bucket that yard possessed. The Wrays, all of them, Tam from his own cottage on his own acres. Bess with bread, because Bess Wray has triage in her bones and knew before anyone that this fire would be fed and not fought. She said one thing on her way past me, without stopping, in the tone of a woman moving down a list. "Study's got a hearth and a door. We'll want it warm by three." Brenna. Lira, who left the inn unlocked, which those who knew her counted the wildest act of the night. Cael came up last with Soren, an old man on a stick on a stone way in the cold dark of a winter's night, and no soul alive would have said one word to stop him.

There was no saving to do by then, and every builder in the crowd saw it in the first ten steps. So they did the other work, and they did it the way that valley did everything, without a wasted word. What talk there was that night was work, and I have kept most of it, and I set it down here in the order it came.

Jorin gave the first of it going past me at a walk, without stopping and without turning his head.

"House is gone. Study."

That was the whole plan, and every man on the hill had it inside a minute. Buckets from the spring in a chain, not on the fires, which were past buckets, but on the grass and the fences downwind, on the Study's sills, on the margins, penning the thing into the two graves it had dug for itself.

Kellard set the chain himself, spring to crown, and counted it out twice before he was satisfied. "Nine to the sill. Hold nine." Then, down the length of it, in the voice a yard-master keeps for a yard: "Full ones up. Empties back on the outside. Nobody runs."

Hal Wray took the spring end and said nothing for a long while, and then said, "Ice on this step," and grit went down on it, and after that nobody slipped.

Jorin walked the line of it with a torch, reading the wind like a drawing, and called it aloud as he read.

"Southwest. Holding southwest."

A gust laid the torch flat and he stood and watched it lie down and come up again before he gave the correction.

"West side of the Study. Not the north face. West."

Twice more in that hour he called the wind, and both times the buckets had moved before the sparks got there.

Ren and Tam turned the paddock rails out of the sparks' road. "Lift your end," Tam said. "Don't drag it. It's shedding." "On three." "Three." They took them out in pairs at a walk, no faster, and stacked them on the wet grass well down the slope, and Ren went back along the empty line with a bucket and wetted the post-holes where the rails had sat. At the finish Tam counted the stack aloud, the way a farmer counts anything he owns or is minding for somebody. "Fourteen. That's all of them."

Tomas stood between the burning house and the Study, up on the crown, watching the air the way he listened to iron, and where he pointed men wetted. He spent perhaps six words on the whole night. One of them was "There," with his arm out straight at the Study's west sill, and a bucket went onto that sill before he had his arm down. One of them was "Wait," with his hand up, and the chain stopped where it stood while a sheet of sparks came over on the gust, and then the hand came down and the buckets went on. Kellard, who had run a yard since before I came to that valley, took Tomas's word the way a man takes a foreman's.

And when there was nothing left to pen, they stood with me in the cold, the whole of them in a rough half-ring, and we watched my barn and my house burn themselves down into two red hearts on a black hill, and nobody said anything for a long time, because that valley knows when a thing is past talk, and then Bess Wray put bread in my hands and said, "Eat it while it's warm," and I found that I was sitting on the paddock rail with no memory of getting there, and that the bread was the best thing I had ever eaten, and that my house was gone, in the specific order a man finds such things out.

The cause was never settled. I want that on the record, plainly, because the valley in its kindness spent that whole winter settling it in six different directions on my behalf, and because I have gone over the facts of that night more times than any accounting in this valley, and they assemble into no more than they are. It had been a dry month, for early winter. The wind was from the southwest, moderate, gusting. I had banked the main-room fire at the usual hour and shut the stove as I had shut it a thousand nights, and I would swear to the shutting before any bench in the Commonwealth, and no man has ever asked me to. The barn held hay in the loft, where the storehouse's filling had left it, and straw in the stalls, and the cart, and the winter stores that had not yet gone down to the new cold store, and every stick of it was timber, quick honest carpentry run up cheap in a fortnight under the note, two winters dry. And Snow would never settle in that barn. From the first night, she had never once stood easy in it, and I had put it down to the hill's hours, and I had been content with that, in the way of a man about the things he has decided not to look at.

So the valley set about explaining it for me. The flue, said some; a flue will hold a spark the way a sinner holds a grudge, and a dry month, and a gusting wind. A tramp's fire in the barn's lee, said others, though no tramp had been seen on the roads since the frost. Hay put up too green in a wet week and mellowing toward its own heat, said the older farmers, who had seen barns go that way, and this was the theory I gave Penworth in my letter because it asked the least of everyone.

The first of them came at me on the second afternoon, across the counter in Kellard's yard, and I have gone back to it more often since than to any of the others.

I had taken Snow down. There was nothing standing on my hill any longer that a horse could be put into, and nothing in that winter's money to build one with, and she had spent the night at the paddock rail under blankets somebody had put on her without telling me, and stood there in the morning looking down the shoulder at where the barn had been, with her ears going back and forward and back. So I led her down the flags at a walk. She went with her head low and her feet careful, and she did not care for the smell she was carrying in her mane, and neither did I.

I found out on that walk what a valley does with a man it has nothing to say to. There were people about, it being the middle of a working afternoon. Not one of them stopped me and not one of them turned away either, which takes more managing than either of the other two. A woman scouring a step went on scouring it and said good afternoon to the horse. Two men shifting sacks at the top of the street stopped shifting them and stood, and then went back to it after I had gone by, and I heard the sacks start again behind me and was glad to hear them. Nobody asked how I was, and I have thought since that a village that would ask a question like that of a man in my condition on his own street would be a poorer place to live in than the one I had.

Kellard's yard in early winter smells of sawdust and paraffin and the cold underside of tarpaulins, and none of that had changed, and I was gladder of it than the fact deserved. He came out of the store before I was through the gate, wiping his hands down his front, and he looked at the horse first and at me second. I have liked him for that ever since.

"Far stall," he said. "Out of the wind. Nothing over her but slate."

He took the rope off me and led her in himself and stood a while at her head with his hand flat on her neck, saying nothing at all, and then came out and shut the half-door and dropped the pin in it and shot the bolt across and tried the bolt. I understood that the trying of the bolt was for me.

Then he went in and got the book. It is a long book, ruled in three columns, kept on the counter under a weight, and he writes in it in the careful hand a man of that trade keeps for other people's business and never for his own. He turned to the page and wrote the date and wrote the horse in, and then held the pen over the third column for rather longer than a figure takes.

"Flues," he said, to the page.

"Flues," I said.

"A flue'll hold a spark a week." He was still writing. "My father's held one. Held it behind the brick in the back wall through a whole dry October and let it go on a Tuesday morning with the lot of us sat at our breakfast, and took the back of that house off, and nobody in it so much as scorched. And there was nothing wrong with the flue. Man came and looked at it after and said so. And there was nothing wrong with my father either, and I'd have taken the head off anyone that said different."

I stood there with my hat in my hands doing the thing I have done all my life at the wrong moments, arithmetic. The barn went first. The barn stood thirty steps down the shoulder from the house and downwind of every chimney on that hill, and the fire began at the loft end, and there is no version of a spark out of my own flue that reaches the far end of my hay before it reaches my roof.

I did not say any of that. I have never said it, to him or to anybody, and I am not going to sit here at this distance and take a good man's chimney apart for him, because he was not offering me a theory. A theory is a thing you argue with. He was reaching over his own counter and putting a floor under my feet, and the floor he had to hand was his father's back wall, and he had carried that wall about inside him for forty years for exactly this, and he laid it down in front of me and went on writing.

"It might have been," I said.

"It'll have been that," he said, and blotted the line, and shut the book on it.

There was a figure at the end of what he had written. He did not read it out and I did not lean over and read it for myself. No word passed between us about it that afternoon or for a fortnight after, and I will come to what it was.

I walked back up in the last of the light with my hat still in my fist where it had been since the gate. From the elbow you can see down into the yard over the wall, and he was out in it with two buckets, going across to the far stall, and there was no reason on this earth for a man who had run that yard since before I came to the valley to be carrying water himself at that hour of an afternoon.

My own lamps do not burn; that much the whole valley said for me before I could say it, in the way of a character witness, and it mattered to me out of all proportion. The hill was blameless; I will say that too, flatly: whatever was under my ground wanted, it had never once in three years wanted heat, and the fire began at the barn's loft end, the farthest timber on the hill from the crown, and the hill that night did what it always did at the dusk and the dark's bottom, no more and no less, its long pulse rolling under the burning like a whale passing under a boat, indifferent, ancient, and cold. The morning never quite closed the question. Some mornings don't. I have learned to leave them open, the way you leave a barn door that has already done its worst.

Go back to the night, though, because I have run ahead of it, and the night had an end.

The two burnings sank somewhere in the small hours from an emergency into a thing to stand near. Kellard broke the chain up when there was nothing left worth wetting, and the buckets came back down the line the same road they had gone up, empties on the outside, and were counted into his cart, and the cart was run in under the lee of the Study out of the sparks. Jorin walked the black ground once with a torch, all round both of them at ten paces, and came back and put the torch out in the wet grass with his boot and said nothing whatever to anybody about what he had walked, then or since. Tomas went down without a word to a soul, at the same pace he had come up. After that they went in ones and twos, as men go who have been up all night and have their own beasts to see to at first light, and the lanterns came off the crown and along the shoulder and down the flags and strung themselves out along the lane, and I stood at the paddock rail with my hand under Snow's jaw and watched them go back down into the village. Cael went last, and it took him a good while, and Soren walked at his elbow the whole way and did not once put a hand out to him. It is a courtesy I have not always been clever enough to manage myself. Before they went, most of them stood a minute at the Study door, because the Study had been warm since three by Bess Wray's arrangement and there was something hot in a kettle in there all night that I had no part in the making of, and men came out of that doorway with their hands round a cup and their faces orange down one side, and drank it, and set the cup on the sill, and picked their lantern up off the flags and went home. Then the sky over the north wood went the colour of dishwater, and it was morning.

What was left, when the light came up grey over the frost, was this. The Study, untouched on the crown, grey and square, my instruments and books and desk and the cellar under it, whole. The glasshouse, untouched down the slope, its panes running with melted frost on the fire side, Brenna and the keeper plant and the winter stock all safe behind their steel. The storehouse in its shelf, the lab, the lamp still burning at the bench, the harvest safe in the cold store, which is the fact that let the winter be survivable at all. The way, stone, fireproof, and now the most-trodden road on the hill. And on the southwest shoulder, two black rectangles in the white frost, one where my stores had been, one where my life had been, going up in two thin lines of smoke as straight as the smoke of the village below, as though the hill had merely acquired two more hearths.

I walked all of it before I did anything else, in the frost, in yesterday's clothes, my boots going through the crust at every step and the grass under the crust still wet from the buckets. The barn's ash was warm enough at the middle to stand near, and I stood near it a while for the reason you would think. Tomas's bar lay in it, black, straight, not so much as bowed, and I raked it out and stood it against the paddock rail, and it has been leaning against something of mine ever since. The rails Ren and Tam had turned out lay in their stack down the wet slope, all fourteen of them, and went back up in the spring, and are up now. Where the house had been there was less than you expect. The stove, on its side, with its door open. The iron of Essa's bedstead, and I did not look at that for long. The chimney standing up out of the middle of everything, sound, unbothered, insolent. And the doorstone lying flat and level and swept, with nothing behind it at all, and I stood on it a while because I could not think of a reason not to.

I wintered in the Study. It had been my whole house once and it consented to be again, the lumen and the hearth and a borrowed cot, my books stacked along the wall in the beginning of an order, Lira's blankets, and under the floor, down the stair, the cellar, so that I slept that whole winter twelve feet over the corner where the hill says its word. I notice that now. I was busy then.

The first night in it I lay on the borrowed cot in the dark and listened for the barn. I had kept a horse forty steps from my bed for two years, and there is a sound a stabled animal makes in the small hours, a shift and a knock and a long blow down the nose, and the ear goes on putting its hand out for that sound after the thing itself has gone. Stone holds no noise but its own. I did not sleep much, and I got up at first light and went down to the glasshouse, because the glasshouse still wanted doing and was there to be done.

Of the money I will say the least the truth allows, in the manner I promised myself when the tenants taught me how. The arithmetic had gone hard again, that is all. The barn and the house were two springs of building, burned in an hour, uninsured, there being no man within a week's road who writes such paper. The glasshouse's first surplus, which had stood clean at the bottom of Penworth's column for one quarter, exactly one, went spoken-for again before the ash was cold: stores to re-buy, a horse to shelter, at Kellard's, at a neighbor's rate, meaning nearly nothing, meaning everything. That was the figure at the end of Kellard's line, and I saw it a fortnight after, when I went down to settle what of it I could, and it was not a rate any yard charges for a stall and hay and a man's hands to a horse twice a day. I looked at the book and he looked at the wall over my shoulder, and I paid it, and neither of us has mentioned it from that day to this, and I put it here with the rest of that winter's arithmetic because that is where it belongs. Penworth wrote, in the letter where a lesser man would have said the sensible things, four sentences, and the second was *You will rebuild in the spring, in stone, and the difference in cost between stone and sorrow is one I am no longer willing to discuss*, and I read it by the Study fire and laughed for the first time since the night, one short bark I did not plan, because he had contrived to make a scolding of it, and the scolding was the tenderest paper that man ever sent me. In the spring, in stone. That was all I said of it to anyone, and it was enough, and the valley nodded and went on bringing me things.

Because that was the other half of that winter, and I will close on it rather than on the ash, because it is the truer weight. They would not stop bringing me things. Eggs at the Study door. A chair, up the way on a boy's back, no note. Firewood I had not ordered, corded against my wall by hands I never caught. Bess's bolster, again, mended where the sparks had caught it in the paddock. A hat of Cael's own, brought by Soren, with the message, verbatim, that a man who has lost his roof has no business also losing his ears.

Two of those I want to give properly, because a list is a poor way to be handed anything.

The firewood began inside the first week. I went down to the glasshouse after breakfast and came back at midday and there was a stack of split ash against the Study's south wall, ends out, the two ends crossed course over course, laid up by somebody who intended it to stand through a winter and not fall over in the first thaw. There was no note on it and no cart tracks near it, the ground being frozen iron-hard, and no boot mark I could read. The next day there was more. I made an effort to catch them, which I am not proud of; I went down late one morning and came back inside the hour, and stood at the north window in the dark another evening for longer than a grown man should stand at a window, and I never once caught anybody at it. The stack went along the wall from the corner to the window and past the window, and stopped when it had run the length of the wall, the wall being the only instruction anybody had been given and evidently instruction enough. It was not one man's wood either. There was ash in it and oak in it and a course of larch that could only have come off the woodland strip, and some of it was split beautifully and some of it was split by somebody in a hurry with a bad wedge, and I burned every stick of it between then and the thaw. I have my opinions about the hands. I have kept them to myself for a good many years now, and I will keep them, because a man who goes about naming his benefactors has taken something off them that they went to a deal of trouble not to give him.

And the hat. Soren came up the way every morning of that winter, with the bread under his coat and whatever the post had for me in his fist, and he had got into the habit of setting them both down on the corner of my desk in a particular order, bread nearer, letters squared to the desk edge, in a way I am fairly sure he had learned off a builder. One morning near the middle of December he set them down in their order and then did not go.

"There's another thing," he said.

He opened his coat and brought it out from under his arm, held flat, the crown pushed in. It was Cael's own hat, the good felt one, brim gone soft as cloth at the front where forty years of a hand had taken it off and put it on, the band inside dark with a life's weather.

"Grandda says to give you this."

"Your grandda wears that hat."

"He's got the other one," Soren said, in the voice of a boy giving evidence, and then he stopped, and I watched him go and find the rest of it, and bring it back whole. "He said to say it in his words and not mine. He said a man who has lost his roof has no business also losing his ears." He thought about it. "He made me say it back to him twice."

There was no arguing with a thing delivered like that, and it had not been sent up in a form that could be sent back, and Cael knew it when he sent it, and Soren knew it, and I knew it, and none of the three of us said so.

I put it on.

Soren looked at it from the side and then square on, taking his time, the whole of his attention on the job as it always was.

"It's too big," he said.

"It is."

"You'll grow into it." Somebody had said that to him about a coat, I have no doubt, and he had kept it by him against a use, and here was the use, spent on a man past forty standing in a stone room with no house. He picked up the empty cloth off the desk and folded it into his coat and went, and I heard his boots go off down the flags at the trot they always went at.

I wore it all that winter and I have it yet. It smelled of woodsmoke and old felt and another man's head, and it kept my ears warm, and I never once said thank you for it to Cael, who would not have known where to look, and who has never in the whole course of our acquaintance explained a kindness of his to me or let me explain one of mine to him.

I had come to that valley three winters before with everything I owned in two wagons, wanting nothing from anyone, arranged never to need to. I ended that third winter owning, by my own count, rather less than I had come with, and I could not walk to my own door without stepping over the proof that I had never in my life been richer, and the ash smell hung in everything I owned until the thaw, and some mornings I hardly minded it, and that is the whole truth of that winter, both halves of it, and I have set them down side by side because that is how they were lived.
