# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Seven: The Delicate Conversation

The lamp was lit in Jorin's kitchen before I was off the lane. It was a warm evening at the front of that autumn, and I had come down the way to ask a man for his son.

I had known it was coming for a year. Longer than that, if I am honest, since a summer evening on a half-built wall when a boy's corners came out true four times running and his father and I both pretended, for the boy's sake and for our own, that we had not seen what we had seen. The paper in the cellar had only put a date on it. A talent like Ren's does not keep. It grows whether it is tended or not, and it goes on growing past the years in which a boy can still choose what to do with it, and every man who loved him knew that, and only one of us had the standing to speak first, and it was not me.

It was Jorin, of course, who solved that, the way he solved most things, by being at work on it while the rest of us were arranging our feelings.

I came to his gate to ask for an evening of his time, and he was in the yard with the storehouse door on trestles, fitting its hinges, and he did not let me get the first sentence to my mouth.

That yard was the best-kept working ground in the valley and the worst-looking. Timber stacked and stickered the whole length of the south wall in three ages of seasoning, the oldest at the dry end and covered. A saw pit with its lid on. The trestles out in the middle of the yard where the last of the light was, because Jorin moved his work round that yard through the day the way a cat moves round a room. And shavings an inch deep over all of it, oak and pine together, so that a man walking across it made no sound at all and the air smelled like the inside of a good barrel.

Two of the younger ones were on the gate when I came to it, on the outside of it, riding it round on its hinge in a way that would have cost anyone else in that family a word. They got down and let me through and got back up on it, and neither of them said good evening to me.

I had four openings ready. I had made them coming down, improved two of them at the river, and settled on the third, which began with the weather and turned on its second sentence into the matter at hand without a seam showing anywhere. I never got to use it.

The first opened on the storehouse, on the grounds that a builder will listen to you about his own work when he will listen to you about nothing else, and it came round by the roof and the doorway and the set of the front wall to the boy who had laid its lines. I threw that one out at my own gate. It was a builder's compliment with a hook in it, and Jorin has been taking builders' compliments off clients for thirty years.

The second was the direct one. It was six words long and I said it aloud on the road, past the elbow, to hear how it stood up in open air. I want to take your son. The river went on running. Nothing whatever in that valley took the least notice of it, and neither did I.

The third was the one I had worked hardest at. I took an adverb out of it at the river and put a better one back, and it was in my mouth and ready when I came up the lane.

The fourth was a letter. I composed it standing still in the middle of the road with the evening flies going over the hedge, and gave it up before its second paragraph, on the grounds that you do not write to a man whose gate is a quarter of an hour off and whose lamp is already lit.

I got the whole way across that yard without being heard. I had always liked that about the place and did not like it at all that evening, having wanted, I think, to be heard coming. He had a hinge in one hand and the screw-iron in the other, and he did not look up when my shadow came over the door.

"You've come about the boy," he said, to the hinge.

"I have."

"Took you a while."

"I have," I said, and then, my third opening being of no further use to anybody, "How long have you known I'd come?"

"Year." He turned the hinge over on the door's edge and started the screw. "Give or take."

"Does the valley know?"

"Valley's known longer."

I took that standing in his own shavings and I had earned it. I had gone about the whole of that summer under the impression that I was a discreet man handling a delicate matter with unusual care, and Ardenmoor had settled the question at the inn some months before and moved on to the business of whether I would put it to the boy before harvest or after, on which, I heard long afterward and from somebody who ought to have known better, money had changed hands.

I had taken pains, too. I had said nothing of it to Brenna, who would have had an opinion about my timing and would have given it to me entire. I had said nothing of it to Cael, who would have said nothing back and let me hear that instead. And in a whole summer of standing in that yard and on that scaffold with the boy an arm's length off me, I had not once let a word of it near him. All of which had bought me nothing at all. They had the summer to watch me being careful, and they watched.

He set down the screw-iron then, and straightened, and looked at me across the trestles in the last of the warm light, and I have faced examiners with less in their faces. It was not hostility. It has never once, in all the years I have known that man, been hostility. It was weight. A man was about to discuss the whole life of his eldest son, and he intended every word of it to be load-bearing, and he wanted me standing square before we started.

"Say your piece, then," he said. "The whole of it. I'll not interrupt."

There is a quiet a builder can make by folding his arms and standing still in his own yard, and he made it, and I began.

So I said my piece, in his yard, with the swallows going over. I told him what I believed Ren was, as plainly as I could say it without a word of the cellar: not clever hands, which any fool could see, and not a good eye, which the valley already priced into his day-wage, but the other thing, the born thing, the thing my whole trade is a school for and cannot put into a man. I told him what I proposed: a formal apprenticeship, the old kind, article and term, in my craft. I told him what it would cost his firm, which was the truth: the best pair of young hands in three valleys, and half of them mine, at the exact moment those hands were coming worth paying for. And I told him what I feared, which I had told no grown man until that evening: that a talent that strong, unschooled, does not stay a gift. It goes inward and sours, or it goes outward and is noticed, and when it is noticed, the noticing is done by men who see good oak in another man's yard and have it marked for a roof of their own before the day is out, and the boy is not asked, and the father is not asked, and I had seen where that road goes and I would build a wall across it with my own hands before I let it open under Ren.

It took a good deal longer to say than it takes to read. I said it standing, sitting not having been offered and my not being about to ask for it, and my hands were at the top button of my coat the whole time, and twice I lost my line entirely and had to go back and take it up again from further off. The light went off the top of his wall while I talked, and then off the trestles, and then off the yard. The swallows finished and gave the air over to the bats. The hinge stayed where he had left it, half driven.

The first time I lost it was on the word apprentice. I heard it come out of my own mouth in the shape it has on paper, a word out of an indenture and a term of years and a clerk's copperplate, and it did not belong in those shavings. I stopped. I said, "That is not how I meant to say it," and went back, and took it up again from a summer evening on a half-built wall and four corners come out true, that being the one piece of the case I could set in front of him without a word of the cellar and be certain he had seen it himself, with his own eyes.

He did not help me. He had told me he would not interrupt and he did not interrupt, not once, not with a word and not with the small encouraging noises a kinder man makes to get you over a stile. All of his attention, and no help in it.

The second time I lost it was worse, and it was at the end, on the part I had said to no grown man. I got as far as the word noticed and stopped. The light was off the trestles by then. I stood in another man's yard in the smell of cut oak and could not get the next clause up off the ground. I said, "Wait." Jorin waited. Two bats went over. Then I took the sentence back to where it started and came at it again from further off and got it out whole, and my hands finished with the top button of my coat and started on the second.

What I did not tell him was how I came to know the shape of that danger, nor why a man past forty in a small valley should have had that wall in him ready-built against a road nobody had yet come up. That part was mine and it stayed mine, and he did not go looking for it.

The house went on being a house behind me while I talked. There was a pot set down on iron; a chair pushed back over flags; once, a voice pitched at the low level a mother keeps for moving children through a room without raising it; and then a door at the back of all that going to, and staying gone to. Nobody came out into the yard.

Once, a child came to the house door, saw the two of us standing over that door in the dark, and was gone again before either of us had moved a hand. What I saw of it was a strip of firelight put out across the yard, and a head in the middle of the strip at about the height of a latch, and then the strip narrowing, and then nothing. Jorin never turned round. The house had been told to leave the yard alone that evening, and told some hours before I came through his gate, and the man had known what was walking down the lane at him better than I had known it walking down.

I finished. He said nothing for a long moment.

A bat came through the yard twice at about the height of my shoulder, hunting the same line both times. Under the timber stack something small was working away at a shaving and did not stop. I stood in the dark and let him have the silence as long as he wanted it, having nothing left to spend and no wish to spend it.

"Three things," he said.

"Name them."

"First." He held up one thick finger. "The boy finishes what he builds. He's my son and he's given his word on half the work in this valley for the next year, the storehouse in it, and no craft, yours or any man's, teaches a boy that a given word has a bottom to it. He finishes what he starts. Every job. If your trade can't wait its turn behind a promised wall, it's no trade for a son of mine."

"Agreed," I said. "And I'd have thought less of both of us if you hadn't said it."

"There's the storehouse," he said, and counted it off against the door's edge with the screw-iron. "That's yours and it's his. He set those lines and he'll close that roof. There's Maret's cart-shed after it. There's a stair been promised two year that's shamed me twice at the inn to my face. And there's a pair of doors for Lira that she's paid half on, that I'd not have taken had I known this was coming down the lane at me, and that I'll not be giving back, because she'd have it for a slight and she'd be in the right of it."

He laid the screw-iron flat on the trestle.

"That's to the spring. Might run past it. I'll not tell a man a joiner's date and call it a date."

"Second." The next finger. "You'll not take him out of wood and stone. I've watched your kind of work, close, for two years now. It goes up into the air. Papers and readings and things a man feels and can't show his neighbor. That's your ground and you stand on it well enough. But my boy was built by timber and rock, and they built him true, and a man wants ground under him all his life, whatever else he gets. However far you take him, he keeps his tools sharp and he keeps his hand in. The day he can't square a frame is the day I've lost a son, and I'll not lose a son to an improvement."

I looked at the storehouse door on its trestles, mortised and beaded and hung-ready, the plainest and soundest work in the county, and thought about my own father's drawing board, and the years I had paid my way with a pencil while I learned the stranger trade, and how I had never once put the pencil down, and I said, "Second condition's not a condition, Jorin. It's how it works when it works. But you'll have it as a condition too. Agreed."

"Third." He put the finger down, and the weight in his face went, if anything, heavier, and his voice came out flatter than I had ever heard it, which in Jorin meant nearer the bone. "He chooses it himself. Not led to it. Not eased into it the way you'd ease a beam down, a little a week till it's carrying before it knows. You'll ask him plainly, in plain words, with me standing there, and whatever he says stands. He's a good boy. He'd say yes to please you, and he'd say yes to please me, and I'd sooner he said no on his own legs than yes on ours." He paused, and looked away down the valley for a moment, and when he looked back he finished it. "He's the best I'll ever make, that boy. Whatever he's for, he's not for being handed about between men who admire him. Ask him. Plainly."

"Tomorrow," I said. "At your table, if I may. Plainly."

He did not answer that at once. He took the door up off the trestles instead, alone, which is a two-man lift, and stood it on edge against the wall out of the dew. Then he stayed there looking at it in the dark with his hands hanging.

"He was four when he first came out here to me," he said. The yard was black by then and I could hear him a good deal better than I could see him. "Stood about where you're standing. Wanted a job of work. So I gave him the shavings to sweep, which is what you give them, and he swept them, and then he came out the next day and wanted another." A pause. "He came out every day after that for fourteen year. I never once had to call that boy out of the house. Not in the wet, not the winter I'd nothing on the books and was out here making up work to be at. He'd be stood in this yard at seven in the morning with his hands gone blue, waiting on me to come out."

He turned round.

"Here's a thing I've said to nobody, not his mother and not him. There were mornings I'd have been glad to lie in. I got up because the boy was already out in the yard."

He let that stand in the dark a moment.

"I'm not giving him to you," he said. "I'd have that understood before we go further. I'm letting him choose, and that's a different article altogether, and it costs a good deal more."

There was nothing to say to that which would have improved it. I did not try.

"Aye," said Jorin, and picked up the screw-iron. That was the treaty.

I did not use the third opening on the road home either. I found it in my mouth somewhere about the river, whole and useless, and let it go over the water with the rest of the evening's traffic. The lane was warm still off the stones and the bats were working it end to end and the lamp was lit in Jorin's kitchen behind me the entire way to the turn.

I did not sleep much on it. I got up twice and wrote the question out, once at the table and once later on standing at the window because sitting had stopped being any help, and both drafts were long and both of them were, I thought, rather fine, and I burned the pair of them in the grate at something past two on the grounds that a question a boy needs a lamp and a dictionary to follow is not a plain question and has no business calling itself one. What I kept was the frame and nothing else. What I was asking. How long for. His father's three conditions in his father's own order and his father's own words. And that he could say no.

The first draft I wrote at the table with the lamp pulled close, and it ran to a page and a half, and it opened with the words It has been represented to me. There was a short account in it of what a term of articles is. There was a sentence on the responsibilities of a master. There was an entire paragraph in which I explained, to an eighteen-year-old joiner in his mother's kitchen, the present state of my own conscience. I read it back twice and thought it careful.

The second I wrote standing at the window with the paper flat against the frame, and it was shorter and it was worse. It had a figure of speech in it about a road with two ends. It had the word gift in it three times. It had, at the foot, a line offering the boy a year to think, and under that a line setting out why a year would be ample. That is the sort of thing a tired man writes at one in the morning and would not say to a dog.

I laid the two of them side by side under the lamp and saw that I had written two long letters to myself and signed neither of them. Then I put them in the grate, the first and then the second, and the second one lifted off the coals before it caught and came down again and burned, and I stood there in my shirt and watched it down to ash.

I said it aloud to the room a number of times to hear where it caught.

It caught in two places. It caught on the term, because five years said out loud in an empty room at that hour is a long time and sounds like one, and I left it standing, since it is a long time and he had the right to hear it at its full length. And it caught on the last piece, on his being able to say no, which came out of me the first four times sounding like a man apologising in advance for a refusal he was expecting. I said that one over until it came out flat.

I came back the next evening, washed, in my good coat, which Jorin noted with one flick of an eyebrow.

The yard had been swept. That was the first thing I saw coming through the gate and it went through me like cold water, because a builder's yard is not swept for a neighbour and never has been, and somebody in that house had been up and down it with a birch broom the better part of an hour on my account.

You could see where the broom had gone. The shavings stood in two long ridges against the timber stack, not thrown out, only moved, and under where they had lain was bare beaten dirt, pale in the last light, holding the print of every trestle foot and barrow wheel of that summer. I had been coming into that yard for two years and had never once seen the ground of it. The lid of the saw pit had been swept clean and the broom had gone round both gate posts, and the storehouse door stood against the wall where he had left it the night before, its hinge driven home.

I did not say one word about it. There was nothing to be said about it that would not have made whoever did it sorry they had.

The kitchen ran the whole width of the house, fire at one end, the long table down the middle. There was bread out on the board. There was a ham gone into with the knife left standing in it. There was a pot on the crane with its lid lifting and settling, and the room smelled of onions and thyme and scrubbed wood, and under all of it a hot iron somewhere out of sight, which is the smell of a house that has been getting ready since noon and does not propose to admit it.

Ren's mother met me at the door with her hands still wet and dried them on her apron before she would take mine. She was a small woman and she had built that evening from the ground up. I had eaten at that table a dozen times across two years and had never once had a question out of her about my trade. I took it at first for shyness. It was not. She had settled long before I ever came to that valley that a man's work is his own business, and she extended me that as a courtesy and expected the same back, and got it.

She said, "You'll sit the fire end, it's warmer," and put me there, and set a cup by my hand, and did not sit down herself, and did not once in the next quarter of an hour let her eyes rest on her eldest son.

The younger ones were still in the room then, on the settle and under the table and in everybody's road, and there were a great many of them. They knew something was on. Children always know something is on, and they were making what they could of it. Ren sat down at the far end with his sleeves rolled down for the occasion and his hair wet at the front where he had put his head under the yard pump.

It was Jorin who cleared them out, and he did it without saying anything. He looked down the room at the settle, and then at the door, once. There was a movement of children toward the yard with a certain amount of protest in it and no disobedience anywhere, and the smallest one went out backwards, watching me.

Ren sat across from me. His father took the head of the table. His mother did not sit at the table at all. She took the mending up because her hands wanted a job and nobody in that room was going to give her one, and sat in the doorway, where she could see the whole of it and could say afterward, if anybody had been rude enough to ask her, that she had only been near. The basket stayed on her knee the entire time. Not one stitch went into anything in it.

Jorin looked at me down the length of his own table. That was the whole of the ceremony there was going to be, and it was enough of one, and I put my cup away from me out of reach and set my hands flat on his boards.

I said: "Ren. I am asking you, in front of your father and mother, whether you wish to be articled to me as apprentice in my craft, for a term of five years, keeping your father's three conditions, which are that you finish what you build, that you are never taken out of wood and stone, and that this choosing is yours alone. Nobody at this table needs your yes. There is no wrong answer, and the offer does not lapse if you want a year to think. That is the whole of the question."

The room was very quiet. Out in the yard the younger ones were fighting over a hoop.

Ren looked at his hands a moment, flat on the table, big already, scarred already, his father's hands one generation younger. And then he looked up, not at me first but at his father, one long look passing between them, and then at me, and he answered.

"Yes," he said. "I've been answering it for a year. I'd like to know what I'm saying."

And his mother in the doorway made one small sound and turned it into a cough, and Jorin put both hands flat on his table and bowed his head over them a moment, one breath, and I sat very still in a joiner's warm kitchen and did not trust myself to speak first.

It was Jorin who came up out of it first. He lifted his head and looked down the table at his son a long moment and said one word.

"Right."

That was the entire remark that man made upon the largest thing that ever happened in his family, and Ren took it in exactly the sense it was given, and something went out of the boy's shoulders that I had not known until then was being carried in them.

Then his mother said from the doorway, in her ordinary voice, "It'll be dry," and stood up, and the evening turned over and became a supper. It was the most graceful piece of work done in that kitchen that night, and it was done by the person at that table who had said the least.

Terms could wait for Penworth's paper, and the old ceremony could wait for the storehouse, because the boy finishes what he builds.

The younger ones came back in off the yard the instant the pot went on the table, and brought the hoop in with them, and the hoop was confiscated, and they were sat down, and the noise in that room doubled and then doubled again. There was the ham, and a great dish of potatoes done in the fat under it, and beans, and her own bread, and a jug off Jorin's back wall.

The two of us he poured for without thinking about it at all. Then he came round to his son with the jug in his hand and stopped, and stood over that cup a moment longer than pouring takes. He poured it half. Then he poured it full. Not one person at that table remarked on it, and in a family that size, on that evening, that took some doing.

Nobody said one word about apprenticeships. The talk was of a wheel at the mill, and of the price of nails, which had been scandalous for two years and would be discussed as a fresh outrage every evening until it came down. Ren ate the way an eighteen-year-old eats and said perhaps nine words the whole meal.

The wheel at the mill had been running out of true since the spring and the whole street had a theory about it. Jorin's theory concerned the bearing at the wall end and was delivered with a fork in his hand, and it took in, before it was done, the man who set that bearing, the man who set the one before it, and the general condition of the trade. The head of the shaft, he said, would be three foot six across if it was an inch.

"Three foot four," said Ren, to his plate.

His father took it, and did not look up, and went on to the nails.

The nails were dearer than they had been in the spring, and had been dearer in the spring than in the autumn before that, and this was, by the settled opinion of that table, an outrage, an imposition, and in some manner nobody could describe the fault of somebody in Wainford. I said that steel was steel and had to come from somewhere and be carried. This was received as the remark of somebody who does not buy nails.

The smallest one got round to me at the end. She had been working up to it since the potatoes.

"Are you going to teach him magic?"

"Some of it."

"Can he float?"

"No."

"Can you?"

"No."

She sat back and thought about it.

"Da can lift a door," she said.

The table went up, and Jorin laughed out loud, the once, which I had heard him do perhaps three times in three years. Ren's mother fed me until I could not stand, and would not hear a word about it.
