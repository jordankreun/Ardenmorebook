# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Seven: The Delicate Conversation

There are negotiations a man prepares for with figures, and negotiations he prepares for with sleep, and the one I opened at Jorin's gate on a warm evening at the front of that autumn was the second kind, and I had not prepared well.

I had known it was coming for a year. Longer, if I am honest, since a summer evening on a half-built wall when a boy's corners came out true four times running and his father and I both pretended, for the boy's sake and our own, that we had not both seen what we had seen. The paper in the cellar had only put a date on it. A talent like Ren's does not keep. It grows, tended or not, the way water rises in a lock, and there is a window in which the choosing is still the boy's, and the window does not stand open forever, and every man who loved him knew it, and only one of us had the standing to speak first, and it was not me.

It was Jorin, of course, who solved that, the way he solved most things, by being at work on it while the rest of us were arranging our feelings.

I came to his gate to ask for an evening of his time, and he was in the yard with the storehouse door on trestles, fitting its hinges, and he did not let me get the first sentence to my mouth.

"You've come about the boy," he said, to the hinge.

"I have."

"Took you a while."

He set down the screw-iron then, and straightened, and looked at me across the trestles in the last of the warm light, and I have faced examiners with less in their faces. It was not hostility. It has never once, in all the years I have known that man, been hostility. It was weight. A man was about to discuss the whole life of his eldest son, and he intended every word of it to be load-bearing, and he wanted me standing square before we started.

"Say your piece, then," he said. "The whole of it. I'll not interrupt."

So I said my piece, in his yard, with the swallows going over. I told him what I believed Ren was, as plainly as I could say it without a word of the cellar: not clever hands, which any fool could see, and not a good eye, which the valley already priced into his day-wage, but the other thing, the born thing, the thing my whole trade is a school for and cannot manufacture. I told him what I proposed: a formal apprenticeship, the old kind, article and term, in my craft. I told him what it would cost his firm, which was the truth: the best pair of young hands in three valleys, half-lost to wood and stone at the exact moment those hands were coming worth paying for. And I told him what I feared, which I had told no one whole until that evening: that a talent that strong, unschooled, does not stay a gift. It goes inward and sours, or it goes outward and is noticed, and when it is noticed, the noticing is done by people who reach for a gifted boy the way a man reaches for a dropped tool, and the boy is not asked, and the father is not asked, and I had seen where that road goes and I would build a wall across it with my own hands before I let it open under Ren.

Jorin heard the whole of it the way he had promised, without a word, his arms folded, the door lying between us on its trestles like the agenda.

When I had finished he was quiet a long moment.

"Three things," he said.

"Name them."

"First." He held up one thick finger. "The boy finishes what he builds. He's my son and he's given his word on half the work in this valley for the next year, the storehouse in it, and no craft, yours or any man's, teaches a boy that a given word has a bottom to it. He finishes what he starts. Every job. If your trade can't wait its turn behind a promised wall, it's no trade for a son of mine."

"Agreed," I said. "And I'd have thought less of both of us if you hadn't said it."

"Second." The next finger. "You'll not take him out of wood and stone. I've watched your kind of work, close, for two years now. It goes up into the air. Papers and readings and things a man feels and can't show his neighbor. That's your ground and you stand on it well enough. But my boy was built by timber and rock, and they built him true, and a man wants ground under him all his life, whatever else he gets. However far you take him, he keeps his tools sharp and he keeps his hand in. The day he can't square a frame is the day I've lost a son, and I'll not lose a son to an improvement."

I looked at the storehouse door on its trestles, mortised and beaded and hung-ready, the plainest and soundest work in the county, and thought about my own father's drawing board, and the years I had paid my way with a pencil while I learned the stranger trade, and how the pencil had never once left my hand entire, and I said, "Second condition's not a condition, Jorin. It's how it works when it works. But you'll have it as a condition too. Agreed."

"Third." He put the finger down, and the weight in his face went, if anything, heavier, and his voice came out flatter than I had ever heard it, which in Jorin meant nearer the bone. "He chooses it himself. Not led to it. Not eased into it the way you'd ease a beam down, a little a week till it's carrying before it knows. You'll ask him plainly, in plain words, with me standing there, and whatever he says stands. He's a good boy. He'd say yes to please you, and he'd say yes to please me, and I'd sooner he said no on his own legs than yes on ours." He paused, and looked away down the valley for a moment, and when he looked back he finished it. "He's the best I'll ever make, that boy. Whatever he's for, he's not for being handed about between men who admire him. Ask him. Plainly."

"Tomorrow," I said. "At your table, if I may. Plainly."

"Aye," said Jorin, and picked up the screw-iron, and that was the treaty concluded; and you will know by now that the three conditions, laid end to end, were the entire law and testament of a man's fatherhood, given in under a hundred words across a door he had built for me, and that I walked down the way that evening in more awe of him than I have been of archmagi.

I came back the next evening, washed, in my good coat, which Jorin noted with one flick of an eyebrow and approved of.

We sat at the family table, the younger children shooed out to make riot in the yard, and Ren sat across from me with his father at the table's head and his mother at the door with her mending untouched in her lap, and I asked him plainly.

I said: "Ren. I am asking you, in front of your father and mother, whether you wish to be articled to me as apprentice in my craft, for a term of five years, keeping your father's three conditions, which are that you finish what you build, that you are never taken out of wood and stone, and that this choosing is yours alone. Nobody at this table needs your yes. There is no wrong answer, and the offer does not lapse if you want a year to think. That is the whole of the question."

The room was very quiet. Outside the younger ones were murdering each other cheerfully over a hoop.

Ren looked at his hands a moment, flat on the table, big already, scarred already, his father's hands one generation younger. And then he looked up, not at me first but at his father, one long look passing between them of the kind that built the Study and the glasshouse and the way, and then at me, and he answered as level as a sill.

"Yes," he said. "I've been answering it for a year. I'd like to know what I'm saying."

And his mother in the doorway made one small sound and turned it into a cough, and Jorin put both hands flat on his table and bowed his head over them a moment, one breath, the way a man does at grace before a meal it took his whole life to grow, and I sat in a joiner's warm kitchen and understood that I had just been trusted with more than the Conclave had ever held of me, and that the trade I had come to that valley to lay down had picked itself back up at that table and reached out its hand, and that nothing on the hill, not the tower to come nor the word under the grass, would ever ask more of my craft than the sixteen words this boy had just answered a life with.

Terms could wait for Penworth's paper, and the old ceremony could wait for the storehouse, because the boy finishes what he builds. That was all as it should be. His mother fed me until I could not stand, and would not hear one word about it.
