# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Eight: The Lamp Business

A man who makes a thing that people want will find, whether he goes looking for it or not, that he is in a business, and I was in a business some weeks before I admitted it to myself. The lumen was the cause. Word of it had gone down the hill the way water goes down a hill, which is to say by every channel at once and faster than you would credit, and where the first months had brought people up to my crown to look at a wall and wait for it to fall, the light brought them up to ask a question, one and two at a time, always the same question in a hundred coats. They would admire the amber glow in my window with the particular sideways admiration of a valley that does not like to seem impressed, and they would turn the talk to their own houses, a daughter who read late and ruined her eyes by tallow, a cow-byre black as a pocket at the four-o'clock milking in the dark of the year, and then they would arrive, by the slow country road that never comes straight at a thing, at the asking. Could such a light be had for a house that was not on a hill full of song.

And I had to tell them no. That was the trouble, and it sat worse with me each time I said it, because a man does not like to stand in a warm lit room and tell a neighbor with a cold dark one that the warmth is not for sale. The lumen drank the hill. That was the whole of what it was and the whole of what was wrong with it as a thing to sell: it fed on the hum, on that deep two-times-a-day voice that ran under my particular acre and, so far as I had ever been able to learn, under no other, and a lamp built to drink the hill was a lamp that would sit dark and sullen anywhere but the crown of Ardenmoor. I had made, without meaning to, the least portable useful object in the valley. I could no more sell a man a lumen for his cow-byre than I could sell him a piece of my hill to stand it on.

The one that stayed with me, and I think in honesty drove me harder at the problem than any pride of my own, was the joiner. She kept a shop off the square, a woman past fifty with a joiner's forearms and a way of looking at a board's end-grain the way Jorin looked at ground, and she did the fine work of the valley, the inlay and the true-fitted drawers and the small boxes with lids that shut on a breath of air, work you cannot do by feel because it lives or dies by a hair you have to see. I had gone down for a set of shelf-brackets and found her at her bench in the short afternoon with a lamp of three tallow dips burning at her elbow and her nose near enough the wood to touch it, and she had straightened when I came in and rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand in the particular way of a person whose eyes have begun, in the last few years, to fail them at exactly the work that is their whole life. She did not ask me straight. Nobody in that valley asked anything straight. She turned my brackets in her hands and passed a word about the weather and the early dark, and then she said, not looking at me, looking at the mean little huddle of flame at her elbow, that she had heard I burned a light up on the hill that a body could see by properly, and that she supposed it was a wizard's business and not to be had by an ordinary joiner, and that she only wondered. And I had to stand in her shop, with my new brackets in my hand and the good clean smell of shaved oak all round us and the winter already stealing the afternoon out from under her, and tell her that she supposed right, that it could not be had, not yet, that the light I burned drank something that was only under my particular hill and would sit dark in her shop as a dead coal. She took it well. She said of course, and that she'd thought as much, and turned back to her bench and her three poor flames and her failing eyes and the work she loved and was losing the light to do, and I carried my brackets up the hill in a black mood that had nothing to do with the cold, because there is a particular helplessness in owning a warmth you cannot hand across a bench to a good workman who needs it, and I had felt it now once too often to keep feeling it and do nothing.

I turned it over for a good while, the way you turn over a problem you have decided to be annoyed by rather than beaten by. And the turning point, when it came, was not a stroke of cleverness but the plain noticing of a thing I had known all along and never looked at straight, which is where most of my better ideas have come from and I have long since stopped being ashamed of it. The hill's song was alive. That was why the lamp had to ask for it, why a greedy draught got a dark glass and a patient one got amber: you cannot take from a living thing, you can only be given by it. But light itself, ordinary light, the flat grey gift of a winter morning through a north window, was not alive. It was just there, spilling in and going to waste against the walls all day, asked of nothing and answering to nothing, as free for the taking as the puddles in the lane. A lamp did not have to court the daylight. It only had to catch it, and hold it, and give it back after dark.

That was the whole idea, and like most whole ideas it was simple to say and a month of evenings to do. I will not pretend the first ones worked. My first attempt caught the light well enough and could not keep it, and gave the whole day's gathering back in one useless flare the moment the sun went off the sill, so that I had built, at considerable trouble, a lamp that was bright exactly when you did not need it and dark all the long evening when you did. My second held the light but soured it, the way a cellar sours milk, and gave back at dusk a thin bluish glow with no comfort in it, a corpse-light, and I put it out with my thumb and sat in the dark and was poor company to myself. The trouble was never the catching. The trouble was the holding, the long patient keeping of a bright thing through the dark hours without letting it spoil or bolt, and I solved it, in the end, the way I have solved most things on that hill, by asking what the hill had already taught me and finding it had taught me this too. The lumen kept its amber by never grasping. So I built the Series lamp to hold the day the way a good host holds a room, lightly, attending to it, letting it be what it was and only asking it, at dusk, if it would care to stay a while longer. It stayed. God help me, it stayed, and I have never entirely got over the fact that the same manners work on daylight as on grief.

The first one that truly held, I woke on a bright cold morning at the very edge of winter, the kind of morning that has no warmth in the light at all, only clarity, a hard scrubbed brilliance off a hoar-frost that made the whole valley ring like struck glass. I set the lamp on the north sill among Brenna's cuttings, where the soldier's-heart had put out, against all likelihood and exactly as she had promised, one small dark flower in the dead of the year. And I let it drink the morning. There was nothing to see in the drinking, which is true of most real workings and a great disappointment to people who have heard the tales; it only sat there in the cold light looking like a lamp that was not lit, which is what it was. But that evening, when the early dark came down blue over the crown and the true lumen woke to its amber at the dusk pulse, the Series lamp beside it warmed too, a little slower, a little cooler in its color, more the plain gold of a good beeswax candle than the deep honey of the hill's own light, and it held that glow steady and uncomplaining for the whole of the evening on nothing but a morning it had kept in its glass. I sat between the two of them a long time, the hill's light and the day's light burning side by side on my windowsill, and I thought that I had just made the first thing in my life that a great many people were going to be able to use, and that it was a lamp, and that there are worse things for a man to have made.

I wrote to Penworth that night, because a thing you mean to sell wants a man of affairs the way a thing you mean to build wants a mason, and Penworth was mine. I set out the whole of it plainly: what the Series lamp was, how it differed from the one on my hill, that it would burn anywhere a window let in a few hours of daylight and go on burning after dark, that it ate nothing, spilled nothing, could not catch a barn afire or gutter out in a draught, and would, so far as I could see, outlast the man who bought it and very possibly the man's grandson. I told him I meant to make them, not in great numbers, I was one man with two hands and a hill to build, but steadily, and to sell them fairly, and that I wanted the thing set up honestly from the start, with whatever protections and papers a made thing needs in the wide world so that some sharper man could not take the shape of it out of my hands the moment it proved worth taking. I have made a great many things I gave away for nothing. This one I meant to keep the making of, and I said so.

His answer came back within the fortnight, which for Penworth by winter roads was very nearly running. Penworth's letters were small essays in the art of saying a great deal while appearing to say the minimum a man could get away with, and I had learned over our decade together to read the freight under the plain words, the way you learn to hear the weight in a quiet man's few. He approved. He approved the way Penworth approved of things, which is to say he raised, in order, every objection I had not thought of and answered most of them himself in the same sentence, and by the end of the page had built, out of my one lamp on a windowsill, an entire small orderly future with the corners squared and the accounts already ruled. There would be registrations to file, and a mark to set on the housings so a man would know a true one from a copy, and an arrangement with Kellard for the credit and the carriage, and a first small run to trusted hands before the wider world was told anything, and I read it all and felt the peculiar comfort of being managed by someone who was better at it than I was and did not need to be thanked for it, which he never did, and would have been embarrassed if I had tried.

It was the last part of the letter that I read twice, and then a third time, and then set down and looked at the fire for a while.

He had, he wrote, taken the liberty of renewing the registrations on my published work while the matter of the lamps had the lawyers' attention anyway, since the two would travel together, and he wished only to remind me, as he had reminded me before and expected to remind me again, that those registrations protected what I had actually set down and no more. The papers I had published in my old life, in the years when I still went out into the world and let it read me, covered perhaps a third of what I knew, the safe third, the third a man could extend and improve and not come to grief on. The rest I had never published, for reasons Penworth knew and did not name, and it was therefore protected by nothing but the inside of my own head and the one place outside it where any full account of the work had ever been written down. And that account, Penworth reminded me, in the driest and most careful sentence he had ever sent me, was not in my keeping. It was in the city, among the books I had not sent for, in the rooms I had not opened, and he would say no more about it than that, save that the arrangement could not last forever and that some spring or other I would have to decide what to do about the things I had left behind me there, and that he hoped, when I did, I would let him help.

I sat with that a long time. The two lamps burned on the sill, the hill's and the day's, and neither of them cared what was in the city, and I envied them the not caring. I had built myself a room and a light and a trade and a life that was becoming, whether I had leave from myself or not, a going concern, a thing with weight and future in it. And under all of it, quiet as the predawn pulse and about as easy to ignore once you had felt it, ran the plain fact that a part of my work, the truest part, the part I had never trusted the world with, sat unread on a shelf in a set of rooms I could not make myself walk into, in a city four days off, guarded by nothing but my own inability to go there. Penworth had a gift, among his other gifts, for naming a thing without naming it, and he had just named the largest closed door of my life by pretending to talk about lamps.

I got up, after a while, and went to my own shelves, because a man who has had a thing like that said to him wants to put his hands on the honest weight of what he does have. I had built the shelves myself, plain oak from Maret's mill, and filled them over that first autumn with the books that had come up on the wagons, my whole working library ranged spine by spine in the amber light, and I ran my eye along them the way you count a thing to be sure of it. And my eye stopped, as it always stopped, as I had known before I ever crossed the room that it would stop, at the one gap. There is a place on the second shelf, between a fat green compendium of grounds and a slim black book of anchorings, where a third book should stand and does not. It is not a large gap. A stranger would take it for nothing, a book lent out, a space not yet filled. I know better. There is exactly one book that belongs in that gap, and it is the full account Penworth had just declined to name, and it is in the city, in her rooms, on a shelf I did not build and have not seen in a great deal longer than I have been on this hill. I had left it there on purpose, the way you leave a light burning in a window you do not intend to come home to, because to send for it would have meant deciding something, and I was not able to decide it, and the gap on my shelf was the shape of the not-deciding, standing there in the amber light between two books that had made the journey. I looked at it for as long as I could stand to, which was not long, and then I put a different book into the gap, a middling atlas I did not much want, so that the shelf would look full to me and I would not have to see the shape of the thing every time I crossed my own room. It is a poor trick and I knew it for a poor trick even as I did it. It is the only kind of courage I had that winter, and I used what I had.

But I have let the account go quiet and inward, and the lamp business was not a quiet inward thing; it was, if anything, the most cheerful public business I ever set my hand to, and I would not have you close this chapter on a shelf and a gap. So let me tell you instead about the first Series lamp I gave away, because I did give the first one away, before I sold a single one, and the giving of it was the truest bit of commerce in the whole enterprise though not a coin changed hands.

I gave it to Brenna. It seemed only right. She had climbed my hill unasked with a basket of living things because a room with a light like mine, she had said, wanted something living to look at, and I had thought at the time that it was the most exactly considered kindness anyone had done me in three years, and it had gone on being right, the rosemary and the grey sage and the improbable soldier's-heart flowering black-red on my sill in the dead of the year. So when I had a lamp that would burn off an ordinary window, the first house I thought of was the low stone one at the end of the lane by the birches, where a woman sat up late over her grandmother's forty years of notebooks by a tallow dip that could not have been kind to her eyes. I carried it down myself on a hard bright afternoon, which was itself a piece of news in the valley, the wizard coming down off his hill to a villager's door instead of the other way about, and I gave it to her on her own doorstone without ceremony, and told her only that it wanted a north sill and a few hours of morning and would ask nothing of her but that, and that she was to think of it as the return of a courtesy and not a debt, because I had learned by then that Brenna weighed debts and would try to pay one.

She took it in both hands and looked at it a long moment in the way she had, considering the whole of what it was before she said anything at all, and then she looked at me, and she did not thank me, which I had half hoped she would not, because thanks would have made it smaller. She said, "My grandmother wrote that the hill gives most to the ones who ask it for least." She turned the lamp so the low sun caught the glass. "She'd have liked that you made a lamp that only asks the morning for what it was going to throw away anyhow." And that was all, and it was a great deal more than thanks, and I went back up my hill in the failing light feeling I had been paid in full and then some, in a currency I had not known I was owed. That evening, from my crown, I watched the valley put out its windows one by one into the dark, and at the far end of the lane by the birches a light came up gold and steady that had not been there before and did not gutter, and stayed lit long after the tallow houses had gone dark, a small warm gold star burning on nothing but a morning a woman had let a lamp keep for her, and I took it, rightly this time and with no doubt in it at all, for company.

The second lamp I did not give away. I sold it, for a fair price and no favor, to the joiner off the square, whose name was Essa, and whom I had come by then to know a little, enough to know that a gift would have sat worse with her than a bill. I carried it down and set it on her bench myself and watched her light her fine work by it for the first time, an inlay of pale and dark woods she had been picking at by tallow for a month and could not get true, and I saw her lean in close over it the old way and then, catching herself, sit back, because for the first time in a winter she did not need to put her nose to the wood to see the join. She did not thank me either. She turned the box in the good gold light and found the one place the inlay was a hair proud, the place she had not been able to see to fix, and she looked at it a while, and she said, "Well. There's the winter's excuse gone," and reached for a file, and I left her to it, which is the only thanks a workman really wants, to be left to do the thing right at last.

The business grew from there the way such things grow, which is slowly and then not slowly, and I will not bore you with the ledger of it, the runs and the marks and Kellard's careful carriage arrangements and the first lamps going out over the winter roads to houses I never saw. It became, in time, a good part of what Ardenmoor is known for in the wider world, more even than the tower, and there are people who know the name of my hill only because a lamp with its mark on the collar burns in their front room and has for forty years and shows no sign of stopping. That is a strange kind of fame to have, to be a warmth in the corner of a stranger's evening and a name they never think about, and it is very nearly the only kind I have ever wanted. But all that was ahead. That winter it was one lamp on Brenna's sill and a few more on my own bench half-made, and a letter from a dry man in a far city folded in my desk, reminding me under its talk of registrations that a made thing outlives the making, and that there were things I had made, and left, and not yet found the courage to go back for. I banked the fire, and put out the Series lamp with a thought rather than a thumb now that I had the trick of it, and left the true lumen burning its amber alone against the dark, keeping the hill's hours as it always would, whether I was there to see it or not.
