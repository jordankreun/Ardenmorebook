# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Eight: The Lamp Business

There was a man at my door on a raw afternoon that winter, turning his hat in his hands and looking past me at the amber in the window, and not saying that he was looking at it. He had climbed the hill on an invented errand, as they all did, and worked his slow way round to the true one by the country road that never comes straight at a thing.

I had him in out of the wind and gave him the chair and set the kettle on, because there is no telling of that conversation that goes well with both men standing in a doorway in December.

He began with the track. It had taken a bad frost at the elbow of it, he said, where the water comes off the field and stands, and he had a barrow of small stone at his place doing nothing and no earthly use to him. I was welcome to the lot. I said I would be glad of it. He said it was no trouble, and it was two miles uphill with a barrow, and we both let that stand.

Then the weather. He allowed it had turned hard early. His father had always held that a wet autumn made a mean winter, and his father had been wrong about most things but not that one. I said two other men had told me the same in that same month. He said that would be because it was true.

Then my wall. He came at it sideways and from behind, having walked two miles uphill in a cold wind to look at it. It was standing, he observed. I agreed that it was. He said there had been talk it would not. I said I had heard some of the talk. He said there had been money laid on it at the inn over the summer, and that he had not been among the losers. I asked which way he had bet. He had not bet at all, he said, being a careful man. A careful man never made anything in his life and never lost anything either. I was welcome to that, he said, along with the stone.

Then the slates, and where I had got them, and what Edran had charged, and whether the hauling had come to more than the stone. It had. That was no more than he had been saying about quarrymen the whole of his life. Then the door, and the hinges, and whether Tomas had made them, and my saying he had, and his saying that Tomas was a good enough smith if you had a year to wait on him.

The kettle went. I made the tea. He held the cup in both hands and drank very little of it, and turned the hat between his knees, and looked at the window, and looked at the fire, and looked at the window again.

He was a long while coming round. That is not a fault. In a place where everybody has to go on living next to everybody, the country road exists so that a man may ask a thing and still meet you at the oven in the morning if the answer is no.

His girl read late, he said, and ruined her eyes by tallow. He had heard I burned a light up here a body could see by. He wondered, only wondered, whether it was the sort of thing a man might have for his own house.

And I had to tell him no.

I made a poor job of it. I said the light was not a thing I could sell yet, and he said he had not been asking to buy it exactly, and I said I knew that, and we stood a moment in the wreck of the pretence with nowhere to put it. So I gave him the short of it. The lamp would not burn off my hill, I said, and I did not yet know why, and until I did there was nothing to be had.

"Ah," he said. "Well." He got the hat back onto his head. It took him two goes. "It's a grand light, that."

"It is."

"She'll manage. They do." He looked at it once more on his way past, with the frank sideways longing of a man at another man's good horse over a gate. At the doorstone he turned round. "I'd not have come up if I'd known it was trouble."

"It isn't trouble."

"No," he said, in the voice of a man being let off, and put his head down into the wind and went.

I stood in the lit doorway and watched him all the way down to the elbow of the track where the frost had got at the stone, and then a while after that, until the grey had him.

I had told a dozen of them no by then, one and two at a time, always the same question in a different coat, and it sat worse with me every time: this man's girl and her tallow-worn eyes, the next man's shed black as a pocket at the four-o'clock milking. You do not like to stand in a warm lit room and tell a neighbor with a cold dark one that the warmth is not for him. That was how I learned I was in a business, by having to turn it away at my own door. I had been in it some weeks before I admitted it, and the lumen was the cause.

The lumen drank the hill. That was the whole of what it was, and the whole of what was wrong with it as a thing to sell. It fed on the hum, on that deep twice-a-day voice that ran under my acre and, so far as I had ever been able to learn, under no other, and a lamp built to drink the hill sat dark and sullen anywhere but the crown of Ardenmoor. I had made, without meaning to, the least portable useful object in the valley. I could no more sell a man a lumen for his cow-shed than sell him a piece of my hill to stand it on.

The one that stayed with me, and I think in honesty drove me harder at the problem than any pride of my own, was the joiner. She kept a shop off the square, a woman past fifty with a joiner's forearms and a way of looking at a board's end-grain the way Jorin looked at ground, and she did the fine work of the valley, the inlay and the true-fitted drawers and the small boxes with lids that shut on a breath of air, work you cannot do by feel because it lives or dies by a hair you have to see. I had gone down for a set of shelf-brackets and found her at her bench in the short afternoon with a lamp of three tallow dips burning at her elbow and her nose near enough the wood to touch it, and she had straightened when I came in and rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. Her eyes had begun to fail her, in the last few years, at exactly the work that was her whole life.

Her shop was two rooms and a yard, and the front room smelled of three things at once: shaved oak, hot hide glue, and the sharp clean sting of the shellac she kept for the small boxes. Shavings stood in drifts against the wall where she had swept them, blond and curling, and somebody's boy came for them by the sack for kindling and paid in eggs. There were four benches in it. Three held the work that keeps a joiner alive, doors and window-frames and a coffin-lid on trestles at the back under a sheet, and the fourth stood under the north light and held the work she was alive for.

My brackets were done and set aside on the corner of it, six of them, made off a drawing I had brought her a fortnight before. I had asked for a plain scroll. What I had was a scroll with a bead run down the edge of it, fine as a pencil line, that I had not asked for and had not paid for and would not have thought of. Two silver the set. She had named the price at the ordering and did not name it again at the handing over.

I turned one over. The bead was carried right round the return, where a bracket sits against a wall and nobody living will ever see it.

She did not ask me straight. Nobody in that valley asked anything straight. She turned my brackets in her hands and passed a word about the weather and the early dark, and then she said, not looking at me, looking at the mean little huddle of flame at her elbow, "I hear you burn a light up on that hill a body could see by properly."

"I do," I said.

"Wizard's business, I expect. Not the sort of thing an ordinary joiner could have at her bench." She said it to the flame, not to me. She did not make it a question. That was as near as she would come to asking one.

And I had to stand in her shop, with my new brackets in my hand and the good clean smell of shaved oak all round us and the winter already stealing the afternoon out from under her, and give her the truth. Some of the truth. "You expect right," I said. "It can't be had. Not yet."

She waited for the rest, in the way of a tradeswoman handed half an answer, and I did not give her the rest. "It won't leave my hill," I said. "I'm at work on why. When I have it, you'll not need to come up and ask."

She took it well. "Of course," she said, and turned back to her bench and her three poor flames and the work she loved and was losing the light to do.

I carried my brackets up the hill. I had a warmth in my window and no way to hand it across a bench to a good workman who wanted it, and I had stood in the middle of that once too often now to go on doing nothing about it.

I turned it over for a good while, having decided to be annoyed by the problem rather than beaten by it. And the turning point, when it came, was not a stroke of cleverness. It was the plain noticing of a thing I had known all along and never looked at straight. The hill's song was alive. That was why the lamp had to ask for it, why a greedy draught got a dark glass and a patient one got amber: you cannot take from a living thing, you can only be given by it. But light itself, ordinary light, the flat grey gift of a winter morning through a north window, was not alive. It was just there, spilling in and going to waste against the walls all day, asked of nothing and answering to nothing, as free for the taking as the puddles in the lane. A lamp did not have to court the daylight. It only had to catch it, and hold it, and give it back after dark.

That was the whole idea, and like most whole ideas it was simple to say and a month of evenings to do.

A month of evenings looks like this, and nobody ever tells you, and it is the hardest part of the work to give any account of to a man who asks what a wizard does all day.

It starts with the blank. A lamp of that kind is not built into glass the way the lumen was; the glass is only the coat it wears. The working goes into a piece of grown crystal about the size of a plum stone, clear as still water and cold to the hand in any weather, and I had fourteen of them in a wooden box lined with flannel, come up on the last wagon before the roads went. They cost what they cost. I thought about the price of them every time I ruined one, and I ruined a fair few, and each one was a half-gold I had already spent twice over in my head before it went into the tin.

Into that goes the working, and the working goes in three layers laid one over another, and the whole art of it is that they must go in the right order and none of them may disturb the one beneath. The first layer is the eye: the part that opens on the light and takes it in. The second decides what is worth taking, because the world is full of light and most of it is no good to you, the red off a fire and the blue off snow and the wrong sort of grey. The third is the binding, and the binding is the whole of the argument, and I will come to it.

The doing of it is bench work. Filthy, close, patient bench work, done with the jeweller's glass screwed into my eye socket until it ached, with a set of files so fine that three strokes was a heavy hand, and with drawn wire I made myself because nobody within four days of that valley drew wire true to a thousandth of anything. A small brass scale that would weigh a hair. Calipers. A cloth laid down so that a dropped file went onto wool and not into the gap between two boards and out of the world. My hands were cold the whole month. You cannot do that work in gloves, and you cannot keep a fire hot enough at your back to warm a bench under a north window in a hill winter without smoking yourself out of the room.

There was a difficulty I had not foreseen. I was doing all the fitting after dark, by the light of the lumen, and the lumen is amber, and you cannot judge the colour of a light by the colour of another light. So I gave the fine judging to the hours either side of noon, being the only hours of that winter worth anything for the purpose, and did the filing and the seating at night by the honey glow with my back complaining, and lost the better part of a week to that arrangement before I stopped fighting it and began getting up earlier.

In all that month I did not do one single thing that would have looked like magic to a man standing in the doorway. I sat at a bench with my nose two inches from a small cold stone and made ten thousand decisions the size of a hair.

The first ones did not work. My first attempt caught the light well enough and could not keep it, and gave the whole day's gathering back in one useless flare the moment the sun went off the sill, and I was leaning over it at the time with my nose a hand's breadth from the glass. What I did in the first second of it was go over backward off my stool with both feet in the air, catch the bench with my elbow on the way down, and fetch the lamp off it onto the boards after me, where it broke into more pieces than I would have said it had in it. I sat in the wreck with my eyes streaming and nothing hurt but the lamp and my opinion of myself, and after a while, once I no longer saw a green sun wherever I looked, I got the broom. So I had built, at considerable trouble, a lamp that was bright exactly when you did not need it and dark all the long evening when you did.

The boy came up the following Wednesday and found the glass still swept into the corner, because I had not been able to make myself put it out with the ashes. He stopped in front of it with his cap in his hand.

"What was that?"

"A lamp. It was going to be a lamp."

He crouched and picked a piece up and turned it in the window light and set it back exactly where it had been. "What did it do?"

"Everything I asked it, in the wrong order." He waited. He always waited. "It took the light in all day, and gave it all back at once, at sundown, in about a second and a half."

"All of it?"

"All of it."

He worked on that a moment. "You were looking at it."

"I was looking straight into it from about so far." I showed him with my hands.

"Did you hit your head?"

"I hit the bench with my elbow and the floor with everything else. My head came out of it better than my dignity."

He nodded, taking that at its full weight as he took everything, and did not laugh, and I watched him decide not to, and thought better of him for it than if he had.

Then he sat down on the boards by the hearth with his slate and let me get on. Somewhere in the middle of the afternoon, without looking up, he said, "How long has it got to hold it for?"

I stopped filing. It was the right question and it was not the one I had been asking. I had been asking how to make a thing keep light at all, as you ask how to stop a bucket leaking. He had gone straight past that to the only figure that mattered: how much bucket a person actually needs.

"Say a woman lights it when the dark comes," I said. "And puts it out when she goes to bed."

"Winter or summer?"

"Winter. Winter's the hard one."

He thought. His lips did not move. His eyes went a little out of focus, and that was all there was to see of it. "Five hours. Nearer six at the turn of the year, if she sits up." A pause. "How long does it get to drink?"

"Call it seven hours of daylight in December. Grey ones."

"Then it wants to give back less in an hour than it takes in an hour," he said. "That's the easier way round."

"Is it."

"It's easier than the other way round," he said, in the voice of a boy stating that water runs downhill, and went back to his slate.

He was right, and I had known it without ever once troubling to say it to myself, and having it said out loud changed the shape of the problem on my bench for the rest of that month. I have taken instruction from some very expensive men in my time. That afternoon I took it off a boy of ten sitting on my floor, and it cost me nothing but the admitting.

My second held the light but soured it, the way a cellar sours milk, and gave back at dusk a thin bluish glow with no comfort in it, a corpse-light, and I put it out with my thumb and sat in the dark and was poor company to myself.

There were nine others besides those two, and not one of them worth a name. One held the light two hours and let the rest go out through the binding in the night, so that I woke at three to a dark lamp that was warm, warm as a stone that has lain in the sun, and that told me exactly where the leak was and did me no good at all. One took in the morning and gave back the morning, grey for grey, faithful as a mirror and about as useful. Two cracked in the setting. One would not take anything whatever, and sat on the sill for four days doing nothing at all, and I put that one in the tin with real ill will.

The tin was the arithmetic of it. It stood at the end of the bench and it filled up over that month with pieces of ruined crystal, and every time I dropped one in, the small dry sound it made was a half-gold going. I had counted the box in the flannel down by better than half before the thing came right, and had written to the city for more, and paid the winter carriage on them. Winter carriage on that road is very nearly the price of the goods.

The trouble was never the catching.

The trouble was the holding, the long patient keeping of a bright thing through the dark hours without letting it spoil or bolt, and I solved it, in the end, by copying myself. I had already built one lamp that held what it was given without souring it, and I had built it without ever quite understanding why that part had come out right.

So I cleared the bench of everything but my own failures, and set the last and best of them in the middle of it on a folded cloth, and sat and looked at it for the better part of an evening the way you look at a letter in a hand you ought to know.

Then I got out the board.

It was a bare board that had come up on the wagons with everything else and had not been out of its wrapping since, and a straightedge, and a hard pencil sharpened to a chisel, and that was the whole of the kit. Taking a working to pieces on paper is not drawing in the sense that a house is drawn. There is no asking in it at all, only the long cold business of setting down what is actually there, layer by layer, at four times the size, until the thing has nowhere left to hide.

It took me three nights. I ruled the sheets and pinned the corners with whatever came to hand, a river stone, the tea tin, the brass scale. The first night I set down the eye of my own lamp, and then the layer that chooses, and both of those went onto the paper easily enough, because both of those I had built with my own hands and understood while I was building them. The second night I drew the binding, the whole of the argument, at four times the size, and sat back and looked at what I had drawn until the small hours, and put that sheet in the fire and drew it again, and put the second one in after the first. There was nothing whatever wrong with either of them. That was the trouble. It was a good binding by every measure I had, correct in every particular, drawn true and drawn twice, and the lamp it belonged to still let the light out of itself before midnight, and the paper would not tell me why.

On the third night I turned my own sheets face down at the far end of the bench, and got up, and went and took the other lamp down off its shelf.

So I went back to the lumen and took it apart on paper, layer by layer, looking not at what it drank but at how it kept, and what I found was that the whole of its keeping lived in one habit of the binding: it never grasped. It held the way a good host holds a room, lightly, attending to it, letting the thing be what it was and only asking it, at dusk, if it would care to stay a while longer. I built that habit into the Series lamp because it had worked once and I could see no reason it should not work twice. It stayed. I have never entirely got over the fact that the same manners work on daylight as on grief.

The first one that truly held, I woke on a bright cold morning at the very edge of winter, the kind that has no warmth in the light at all, only clarity, and a hoar-frost that made the whole valley ring like struck glass. I set the lamp on the north sill among Brenna's cuttings, where the soldier's-heart had put out, against all likelihood and exactly as she had promised, one small dark flower in the dead of the year. And I let it drink the morning.

There was nothing to see in the drinking. There is nothing to see in most real workings, and that is a great disappointment to people who have heard the tales. It only sat there in the cold light looking like a lamp that was not lit, and that is what it was. But that evening, when the early dark came down blue over the crown and the true lumen woke to its amber at the dusk pulse, the Series lamp beside it warmed too, a little slower, a little cooler in its color, more the plain gold of a good beeswax candle than the deep honey of the hill's own light, and it held that glow steady and uncomplaining for the whole of the evening on nothing but a morning it had kept in its glass. I sat between the two of them a long time, the hill's light and the day's light burning side by side on my windowsill, and I thought that I had just made the first thing in my life that a great many people were going to be able to use, and that it was a lamp, and that there are worse things for a man to have made.

I wrote to Penworth that night. A thing you mean to sell wants a man of affairs as a thing you mean to build wants a mason, and Penworth was mine. I set out the whole of it plainly: what the Series lamp was, how it differed from the one on my hill, that it would burn anywhere a window let in a few hours of daylight and go on burning after dark, that it ate nothing, spilled nothing, could not catch a barn afire or gutter out in a draught, and would, so far as I could see, outlast the man who bought it. I told him I meant to make them, not in great numbers, I was one man with two hands and a hill to build, but steadily, and to sell them fairly, and that I wanted the thing set up honestly from the start, with whatever protections and papers a made thing needs in the wide world so that some sharper man could not take the shape of it out of my hands the moment it proved worth taking. This one I meant to keep the making of, and I said so.

A month is a long time to wait on a letter and I did not spend it waiting. There was a lamp standing on my sill in nothing but its own glass, bare as an egg, and a second half-made on the bench beside it, and glass wants brass round it if it is ever to go further than a table. So I went down for iron.

I went down on foot. Snow was wintering in the inn's spare stall at a price I paid by the quarter, there being no shelter on that hill fit to keep a horse through a hard season and no prospect of one until I built it. And in any case a track a man can barely get down in ice is not a track you take a mare down for the pleasure of it. I looked in on her all the same. She had a deep bed and hay to her knees and a rug on her. She put her ears flat when she saw me, took the apple, and then turned her whole grey backside to the door and stood facing the wall until I went away. She had been doing that since the first week and would go on doing it all winter. A horse cannot say *you left me here*. She had found the next best thing and she used it.

Lira crossed the yard with a bucket as I came out. She did not ask after the hill, or the lamp, or my health. She said, "Her board runs to the quarter-day." I said I would settle at the quarter-day. She said, "You will," in exactly the tone she would have used to read the figure off a page, and went on with her bucket.

The smithy stands at the top of the street, where the ground begins to think about being a hill again, and on a cold day you can find it with your eyes shut. Tomas had the door half open with snow on the sill. That tells you what it is like in there in August. I stood inside it and let the heat come into my coat and waited on the piece he had in the fire, because you do not talk to a smith over an open fire, and because I liked watching him work and would have waited anyway. He was drawing down a length of bar into the tine of somebody's muck-fork, and he did it in about eleven blows, and the last three of them were only tidying.

When he had it quenched he came over and wiped his hands and looked at the drawing I had brought.

It was a collar and foot in brass, near enough to the lumen's but not the same: a shallower seat, and a ring let into the waist of the collar that a thumb could turn.

He looked at it as long as he had looked at the first one.

"Thumb-ring," he said.

"It has to be turned by a woman with her hands full and no notion how it works."

He put a finger on the seat. "Wants deeper."

"It'll be standing on a shelf in somebody's kitchen. Deeper is heavier."

"Deeper is still on the shelf."

I thought about that for about four seconds. "Deeper, then," I said. He took a pencil out of the bench and altered my drawing without asking me. From Tomas that is a speech, and I took it as one.

Then he said, "Seam."

I had drawn it soldered. He shook his head, once. He did not tell me why and I did not ask him, and about six weeks later, when the frost got in behind a soldered joint on a bracket I had made myself, I found out.

"How many," he said.

"Two, to start. More if they go."

"They'll go." He said it as he would have said that iron was hard. "Eight silver."

"For the pair?"

"For the one." He put the drawing down on the bench and squared it to the edge with two fingers. "First one's mine."

There is no arguing with a man who has already put a thing away in his own accounts, so I did not try. We did not settle the pattern that day, either. It took us the back end of that winter and a good part of the spring to settle it, in eight and ten words at a time. But when the second collar was done he cut a strip of soft iron off the end of a rod and bent it round the finished piece and knocked it true, and hung it on a nail low down at the end of a wall that was already crowded with nails, and said nothing whatever about it, and neither did I.

But that was all in front of us that first afternoon. I climbed the track home in the last of a short day with the altered drawing buttoned inside my coat, and after that there was nothing further to be done about any of it by me or by anybody until the post came up the valley.

His answer came back before the month was out, which for Penworth by winter roads was very nearly running. Penworth's letters were small essays in the art of saying a great deal while appearing to say the least a man could get away with, and ten years of them had taught me to read the freight under the plain words, as you learn to hear the weight in a quiet man's few. He approved. He approved as Penworth approved of things, by raising, in order, every objection I had not thought of and answering most of them himself in the same sentence, and by the end of the page he had built, out of one lamp on a windowsill, a small orderly future with the corners squared and the accounts already ruled. There would be registrations to file, and a mark to set on the housings so a man would know a true one from a copy, and an arrangement with Kellard for the credit and the carriage, and a first small run to trusted hands before the wider world was told anything. I read it through and understood that all of it would be done, and done better than I would have done it, and that I was not required. He did not want thanking. He would have been embarrassed by it.

At the foot of it he had ruled the lamps against the year in two short columns, the way he ruled everything, and he had been careful to make the lamps small. A run of the size one man could actually make between other work, he wrote, was worth having and was not worth mistaking for a rescue: it would buy timber, or a guest cabin, or a season of the small unglamorous materials a building year eats without anybody noticing, and it would not touch the transfer taxes, which fell where they had always been going to fall, nor the plain running cost of keeping a hill and a mare and a crew fed. Cash would still be wanted in the spring. The bottoms would have to be in other people's hands and under a crop by seed-time, he wrote, or the arithmetic would want a harder conversation than either of us cared to have by post.

It was the last part of the letter that I read twice, and then a third time, and then set down and looked at the fire for a while.

He had, he wrote, taken the liberty of renewing the registrations on my published work while the matter of the lamps had the lawyers' attention anyway, since the two would travel together, and he wished only to remind me, as he had reminded me before and expected to remind me again, that those registrations protected what I had actually set down and no more. The papers I had published in my old life, in the years when I still went out into the world and let it read me, covered perhaps a third of what I knew, the safe third, the third a man could extend and improve and not come to grief on. The rest I had never published, for reasons Penworth knew and did not name, and it was therefore protected by nothing but the inside of my own head and the one place outside it where any full account of the work had ever been written down. And that account, Penworth reminded me, in the driest and most careful sentence he had ever sent me, was not in my keeping. It was in the city, among the books I had not sent for, in the rooms I had not opened, and he would say no more about it than that, save that the arrangement could not last forever and that some spring or other I would have to decide what to do about the things I had left behind me there, and that he hoped, when I did, I would let him help.

I sat with that a long time. The two lamps burned on the sill, the hill's and the day's, and neither of them cared what was in the city. I had built a room and a light and a trade, and all of it was becoming, whether I had leave from myself or not, a going concern. And under it ran the plain fact. A part of my work, the truest part, the part I had never trusted the world with, sat unread on a shelf in a set of rooms I could not make myself walk into, in a city a week's road and rail away. Penworth had a gift for naming a thing without naming it. He had just named the largest closed door of my life by talking about lamps.

I got up, after a while, and went to my own shelves. I wanted my hands on the honest weight of what I did have. I had built the shelves myself, oak from Maret's mill, and filled them over that first autumn with the books that had come up on the wagons, my whole working library ranged spine by spine in the amber light, and I ran my eye along them as you count a thing to be sure of it.

And my eye stopped, as it always stopped, as I had known before I ever crossed the room that it would stop, at the one gap. There is a place on the second shelf, between a fat green compendium of grounds and a slim black book of anchorings, where a third book should stand and does not. It is not a large gap. A stranger would take it for nothing, a book lent out, a space not yet filled. I know better. There is exactly one book that belongs in that gap. It is the full account Penworth had just declined to name, and it is in the city, in her rooms, on a shelf I did not build and have not seen.

I had left it there on purpose. To send for it would have meant deciding something, and I was not able to decide it, and the gap was the shape of the not-deciding, standing in the amber light between two books that had made the journey. I looked at it for as long as I could stand to, which was not long. Then I put a different book into the gap, a middling atlas I did not much want, so that the shelf would look full and I would not have to see the shape of the thing every time I crossed my own room. It is a poor trick. I knew it for a poor trick while I was doing it. It is the only kind of courage I had that winter, and I used what I had.

But the lamp business was not a quiet inward thing. It was the most cheerful public business I ever set my hand to, and that winter did not end on a shelf and a gap. I gave the first Series lamp away before I sold a single one, and the giving of it was the truest bit of commerce in the whole enterprise, though not a coin changed hands.

I gave it to Brenna. It seemed only right. She had climbed my hill unasked with a basket of living things because a room with a light like mine, she had said, wanted something living to look at, and I had thought at the time that it was the most exactly considered kindness anyone had done me in three years, and it had gone on being right, the rosemary and the grey sage and the improbable soldier's-heart flowering black-red on my sill in the dead of the year. So when I had a lamp that would burn off an ordinary window, the first house I thought of was the low stone one at the end of the lane by the birches, where a woman sat up late over her grandmother's forty years of notebooks by a tallow dip that could not have been kind to her eyes. I carried it down myself on a hard bright afternoon, itself a piece of news in the valley, the wizard coming down off his hill to a villager's door instead of the other way about, and I gave it to her on her own doorstone without ceremony, and told her only that it wanted a north sill and a few hours of morning and would ask nothing of her but that, and that she was to think of it as the return of a courtesy and not a debt, because I had learned by then that Brenna weighed debts and would try to pay one.

Her cottage is the last house on the lane, low grey stone with the slate gone green on the north pitch, standing among birches that in that season were nothing but white sticks and a great deal of sky. You can hear the river from her doorstone. I had been down that lane half a dozen times across the year for small honest things and had never yet been asked past the counter, and did not expect it that day and did not mind.

The front window is her drying-green, and in winter it was full: bunches hung head down off a wire, grey and brown and one still green, and the whole room smelled of the inside of a hedge in August. She had the door open before I was off the lane. I never worked out how she managed that and never asked her.

I bought a salve first. There was no craft in that on my part. My hands were split across the knuckles from a winter of cold bench work and I had been meaning to come down about it for a fortnight. She looked at the hands before she looked at me, turned one of them over, made a small sound at the state of it, and went to the shelf without asking me a single thing. The jar was brown glass with a waxed cloth over the top and a string round the neck of it. Six copper. I paid the six copper and she put them in her apron and wrote nothing down. She kept her accounts as her grandmother had kept hers, in her head and correctly.

Then I took the lamp out of my coat. It had been wrapped in a shirt, that being the only clean soft thing in my house that morning, and I unwound it there in her doorway with the shirt over my arm, and I will admit that I had rehearsed a sentence on the way down and did not use one word of it.

She took it in both hands and looked at it a long moment in the way she had, considering the whole of what it was before she said anything at all, and then she looked at me, and she did not thank me, which I had half hoped she would not, because thanks would have made it smaller. She said, "My grandmother wrote that the hill gives most to the ones who ask it for least." She turned the lamp so the low sun caught the glass. "She'd have liked that you made a lamp that only asks the morning for what it was going to throw away anyhow." And that was all, and it was more than thanks, and I went back up my hill in the failing light knowing I had been paid in full.

That evening, from my crown, I watched the valley put out its windows one by one into the dark, and at the far end of the lane by the birches a light came up gold and steady that had not been there before and did not gutter, and stayed lit long after the tallow houses had gone dark, a small warm star burning on nothing but a morning a woman had let a lamp keep for her. I took it, rightly this time, for company.

The second lamp I did not give away. I sold it, for a fair price and no favor, to the joiner off the square, whose name was Essa, and whom I had come by then to know a little, enough to know that a gift would have sat worse with her than a bill. I carried it down and set it on her bench myself and watched her light her fine work by it for the first time, an inlay of pale and dark woods she had been picking at by tallow for a month and could not get true, and I saw her lean in close over it the old way and then, catching herself, sit back, because for the first time in a winter she did not need to put her nose to the wood to see the join.

When she had looked her fill she said, "What is it."

"Two gold."

She had not asked the price and we both knew it. She took the answer anyway.

"That's not enough."

"It's what it costs and a bit over."

"Show me."

So I showed her, on the back of an ash offcut, in pencil: the crystal, the glass, Tomas's brass, the carriage on the crystal up from the city, and a figure at the foot for the evenings. She put her thumbnail on that last figure straight away.

"That's low."

"It's the first one I've sold."

"That's no reason to teach yourself a bad habit on it." She got a tin down off the shelf over the glue pot and counted the price onto the bench, some of it in very old coin, and then set a piece more beside it and left her hand over that a moment before she took it back. Neither of us said anything about it at all.

Then I got out the bill, Penworth having been clear that every one of them was to be written down from the first, and I had the date on it before I understood that I did not know what to put underneath.

"Who am I making it to?"

She did not look up from the box. "Essa."

"Essa what?"

"Essa," she said. "There's only the one of me."

She did not thank me either. She turned the box in the good gold light and found the one place the inlay was a hair proud, the place she had not been able to see to fix, and she looked at it a while, and she said, "Well. There's the winter's excuse gone," and reached for a file, and I left her to it.

Two lamps is not a trade. What made it a trade was not the working at all. It was a man in a yard with a book.

I went to Kellard on a Tuesday with Penworth's letter in my coat, and I made the approach badly, because I came in with the good news first. That is a city habit. In the city you open with the size of the thing. Kellard heard me out with his hands flat on the counter and his face doing nothing whatever, and when I had finished telling him what the lamps were and how many I thought I might make in a year, he said, "How do they break?"

Not what do they do. Not what will they fetch. How do they break.

"The working doesn't. The glass does."

"On my cart."

"On anybody's cart."

He took me through into the back where the flour and the salt and the good candles lived, and where the cold had sat in the corner until I took it out of there for him, and he set his lamp on a barrel and got the book. Kellard's book was the whole of Ardenmoor's commerce and about two thirds of its memory, and he never made an entry in it without first turning back a page or two to see what he had written before.

"Straw's no good," he said. "Straw settles. It'll be packed hard as a floor by Wainford and your glass sitting on the hard of it." He licked his thumb and turned a page. "Wool waste. I can get wool waste."

"I'll pay for the wool waste."

"You will." He wrote something short. "Crates?"

"I can make crates."

"You can't." He said it without any unkindness in it at all, as a man reads off a weight. "You've a hill to build. There's a fellow down the valley owes me for a winter's flour and would sooner work it off than pay it."

The terms took the better part of an hour, and I liked it more than I would have owned to at the time. He wanted a tenth. I said that a tenth of what a thing sells for is a different animal from a tenth of what it costs to make, and he said he had been aware of that since before I was born. We settled on a tenth in the end, on the price at the far door, and then he took the carriage risk himself as far as Wainford, and I had not asked him to.

"They'll break," he said. "Some of them will. If they break on my cart it's my loss, because I'll not have you standing in my yard arguing over a crate you can't see the inside of." He wrote. "You'll make them good, and I'll not be told after that they were made bad."

"That's a fair bargain for me and a poor one for you."

"That's my book and my cart," he said. "And I close at six."

So I shook his hand and was out of the yard before six, and neither of us ever looked at that page again.

The business grew from there as such things grow, slowly and then not slowly: the runs, the marks, Kellard's careful carriage arrangements, the first lamps going out over the winter roads to houses I never saw.

I will give you one morning of it.

It came later than the winter I am telling you about, at the turn of it into the spring, when the low road had firmed enough to carry a loaded cart. Kellard's carter came up as far as the foot of the track. Nothing on wheels had ever come further. I brought the crate down to him on a hand-barrow with the wheel skidding in the frozen ruts, and we loaded it together off the tailgate.

Eleven lamps. That was the whole of the first run, and it does not sound like much, and it was two months of evenings and the better part of a year of thinking.

They went packed in wool waste in a crate a man down the valley had made off a drawing, each one wrapped by itself, glass to brass to cloth, and the crate lidded and roped and marked on the end in Kellard's own hand. I had counted them into it the night before by the lumen. I counted them again in the lane, out loud, with the carter watching me and his breath going up white, and he said nothing whatever about it, having seen a good many men count a thing twice.

"Eleven," he said, when I had done.

"Eleven."

He put a mark on a bit of card. "Warm going," he said. Then he set the tailgate up, and swore at the pin, and I put my hand on the pin and swore at it too, and between us we got it home.

He clicked to the horse and went off down the low road, and I stood in the lane with my hands in my pockets and watched a crate of my own work go round the bend below the village and out of the valley.

It is a peculiar thing to watch. I had made many things before that and let most of them go, and every one of them had gone to somebody I knew, or into a house I had drawn, or across a bench into a hand I could see. This went into the country. In a fortnight there would be a light burning in a room I had never stood in, in a house I could not have found on a map, and the woman who put her thumb to the collar would know nothing about me and would not need to.

I went back up the hill and made two more.

It became, in time, a good part of what Ardenmoor is known for beyond its own roads. There are people who know the name of my hill only because a lamp with its mark on the collar burns in their front room and shows no sign of ever stopping.

But all that was ahead, years of it, and none of it had begun that winter. That winter it was one lamp on Brenna's sill and one on Essa's bench and three more on my own, half-made and waiting on collars I had not yet asked Tomas for, and a letter from a dry man in a far city folded in my desk, reminding me under its talk of registrations that a made thing outlives the making, and that there were things I had made, and left, and not yet found the courage to go back for. I banked the fire, and put out the Series lamp with a thought rather than a thumb now that I had the trick of it, and left the true lumen burning its amber alone against the dark, keeping the hill's hours as it always would, whether I was there to see it or not.
