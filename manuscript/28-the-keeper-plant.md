# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Eight: The Keeper Plant

I came home from Jorin's table late that night and stopped at my own gate before I went in, and looked down the slope at the glasshouse standing dark. There was one bed under that roof with nothing in it. There had been for a year.

I had asked her at the turn of the year, as instructed.

She had been standing in the north corner of the glasshouse when I asked, in the bare bay she had claimed with five words a season before. She looked at the empty ground of it a while. Then she gave me her ruling, complete, as she gave everything: "Not yet. It's held a winter. I'll want it to have held a summer too. A house isn't proved till it's been tried both ways."

That was the whole of the audience. The corner stayed bare through the spring salads and the summer's first real crops, one empty bed in a glasshouse working harder by the month, and not one soul who worked under that roof so much as set a tool down in it. Brenna had spoken. In that house her word ran as mine ran on the hill.

I had a year, then, to look at a piece of bare ground, and so did everybody else who worked in that house.

It is a strange thing to work around. A glasshouse coming into its first full season is the busiest quiet building a man can stand in. There were mornings that spring with four of us under that roof and hardly a word said in an hour: Brenna at the propagation bench pricking out with her thumbs, Tam Wray down among the lettuces he had made his own study, whoever else was up from the village going along the forcing bays with a can, and myself on the vent cranks reading the glass. The sounds of it were water, and a knife, and the crank, and now and then the long soft complaint of a shade-cloth being drawn.

And at the north end, past all of it, the corner.

Nothing marked it. Four paces of dressed bed, the grit raked level, nothing growing. The light came into it the same as anywhere and went out of it the same. In a house where every inch of soil was asked to earn its keep twice a season it sat there earning nothing, and nobody touched it. I watched that go on for a year and never once heard it discussed. A man crossing with a crate would go round it with the crate rather than set the crate down in it for the ten seconds it takes to change your grip. Tam, who had the least cause of anyone under that roof to hold a herbalist's corner sacred and the best cause to want the room, grew his lettuces to within a foot of the line and stopped.

I asked him once whether he could not do with the space.

"That's spoken for," he said, and went back to it. No boundary in my life has ever been explained to me more completely.

The winter tried the house first.

It was one of those still black nights at the end of the frost, the sort where the cold does not blow but settles. I woke at two and could not swear to the stove, and there is no sleeping again after that. The track had ice in the low places and the stars were out hard and small, and when I got the glasshouse door open the warmth came out past me into the dark with wet earth and green in it, and I stood in the doorway a moment breathing it like a man let off.

The stove was in. I fed it anyway, and swept the ash, and went the length of the house with a lamp looking at panes. The frost had drawn its ferns on the outside of the glass and got no further than the glass. In the propagation bays the seedlings stood up out of their trays in ranks with no notion of February about them at all. Coming back down I stopped at the north end and held the lamp up over the empty corner, for no reason I could have given anybody, and the grit had frozen at the top and not below, and I laid my hand flat on it, and it was cold, and there was nothing else in it to find.

The summer tried it the other way, and harder.

We had three weeks in July with no cloud in them. A glasshouse in that weather is not a warm building, it is an engine. The vents went up their whole travel by eight in the morning and the shade-cloths across by nine, and by noon you could not lay your bare hand on the south glass, and the house drank water faster than four people could carry it. Brenna went along the beds the whole of one such day with her sleeves down against the burn and her hat on, doing nothing whatever but reading leaves. Twice she had us throw wet sacking over the forcing bays. Once she stopped in the middle of the walk and stood quite still for the better part of a minute with her eyes shut.

"Far end wants the cloth down a foot," she said, with her eyes still shut.

"You can't see the far end from here."

"No." She opened them. "Go and put it down a foot."

I went and put it down a foot.

We lost a little lettuce at the south end and nothing else. Coming out of it at six in the evening, wrung out and streaked to the elbow, I went up the walk to shut the last vent and passed the corner, and it was the coolest ground in the building, four paces of raked grit at the blind north end with the day's heat gone over the top of it, and I stood in it a moment longer than the vent required before I went on and shut the vent.

By the front of that autumn the house had been tried both ways. The stove had held the frost out of the propagation bays through the last of winter; the vents and the shade-cloths had held the summer down to what growing things could bear; and we had sold, by then, enough out of those beds for the deferred half of Jorin's wages to be paid out entire and a first small figure to stand at the bottom of Penworth's quarterly column with no bracket around it.

I paid Jorin at my own table on a bright cold morning. It is the only piece of business in three years I have wanted to do twice.

The money came up from Kellard's yard the day before, in a leather bag with the yard's mark burnt into the flap, and Kellard walked it up the hill himself. He stood in my doorway holding it and did not offer it at once.

"Fine morning," he said. "For the time of year."

"It is."

"Been dry."

"It has."

He put the bag into my hands then, and did not let go of it until he was satisfied my hands were under it, and looked at the doorpost while he said the only other thing he said.

"Terms as they were."

"Terms as they were."

Then he went down the way with his hat on and his hands behind him. He never once said what he was carrying.

Jorin came up at seven with his hat in one hand and his book under his arm and would not have the fire built up. I had the sum on the table already. I had spent a good part of the evening before getting it into columns, and then taking the columns apart and building them again in a different order. I had never had this to do before and I wanted it to look well on the table.

He put his book down, squared it to the table's edge, and sat, and let me count.

I counted it out in front of him aloud, in stacks, and pushed each stack over as I finished it, and he did not touch one of them until the last was down. It took a while. It is a curious thing to hear your own voice going on and on with figures in a quiet room, and the man opposite saying nothing whatever, and a winter and a summer of a builder's patience standing on the boards between you in little towers of metal.

When I had done he did not count it back. He put two fingers on the near stack and moved it a hair until it stood square to the table's edge, and went down the row doing the same to each, and then went back along the row and did it again. He does that to a rank of chisels, a stack of shims, a plate of bread, and a pair of sleeping children's boots.

"The paper," he said.

I had it ready: the sheet the two of us had signed at the black end of last autumn, the winter rate on it and the deferred half under that and the interest written in below, in my hand and with both our names at the foot. He read it through, though he had it by heart and so did I. He took his time with it. He read it once and did not go back to the top. Then he laid one finger on the line about the interest.

"That's more than the work was."

"It's what's written."

"It's what you wrote."

"And what you signed," I said. "You carried a crew through a winter on a promise. You paid them every week out of your own book for four months and you never came up this hill once to ask me how the beds were doing. There is a price for that on the paper in your hand, and I am not going to be argued down on it at my own table."

He looked at the line a moment longer. Then he folded the sheet along its old fold and put it inside his book, and swept the stacks off the edge into the bag with no care at all, the way a man puts away a thing he has finished thinking about.

"Right," he said.

And then he sat there. He had somewhere to be by eight, and he sat in my chair with his hat on his knee for twenty minutes and drank a cup of tea he had twice refused, and talked about nothing whatever: the price of lime, the state of the low road, a chimney at the mill he did not care for and had not been asked about. Those twenty minutes have stayed with me longer than the counting did.

Then he stood up and put his hat on and stopped in my doorway with the bag in his hand.

"Men were paid at the time," he said. "That part was mine to carry."

"I know it."

"Aye." He looked past me and down the slope, where the long roof of the glasshouse was taking the first of the sun and the beds stood green behind the glass. "It came in, then."

"It came in."

He went down the way with the bag in his off hand and his book under his arm and did not look back at the house or at me.

I will not call it triumph. It was thinner than the dreams had been. But the house was proved, a working glasshouse at the end of a stone way on a frontier hill, and the season was turning, and on the first evening with a real edge on the air Brenna appeared at my door with a handcart, and on the handcart, wrapped against the cold like an invalid, was the crock.

I knew the crock. Anyone who had ever stood in her cellar knew the crock, though nobody in the valley had seen it above ground in thirty years. Old glazed earthenware the colour of a winter pond, wide as an embrace, and standing in it, unkillable and unthriving, the plant.

I have called it the keeper plant because that is what she called it, on the one occasion she called it anything. What it was, no book of mine or hers has ever said: woody, low, dark-leaved, something in the way of a rosemary and nothing like one, older than Brenna, older than her grandmother's keeping of it, which had begun, by the notebooks, when her grandmother was young and had accepted it as a charge from a keeping I never learned the start of. It had lived in that cellar for thirty years because it would live nowhere else. Her grandmother had tried it in every ground and light she owned and it had sulked toward death in all of them and recovered only in the dark, in the crock, sustained on nothing anyone could name, refusing both to grow and to die, holding itself, the way a man holds his breath.

And the week I took the hill, it had put out three leaves.

She did not say, that evening at my door, that it was time. She took up the handles of the cart, and I walked beside her down the slope to the glasshouse through the first real cold of the year, saying nothing, because there was an order to this even if neither of us would have called it that, and my part of it was to open doors and hold my peace.

It is a quarter of a mile from my door to the glasshouse door and it took us a long time to go it.

The cart was a light one and the load was not heavy, and neither fact had anything to do with the pace. She had the handles and would not give them up, and the slope wanted to take the thing away from her at the elbow of the path, and I put one hand on the rail at my side of it and took what weight I could without appearing to, and she let me, and that was its own sort of permission. The wheel went over the flags with the flat knock a wheel makes on set stone, one flag, the next, the next. I had laid those flags myself, a few at a time, behind a horse, and I heard every one of them go under the wheel.

The cold had come up out of the ground at about four in the afternoon and it had the smell in it that means the summer has stopped arguing. Our breath went ahead of us. Away west the sky was still that hard clean green it goes for ten minutes at the back of a fine autumn day, and the valley had gone blue underneath it.

Neither of us said one word the whole way. I opened the gate at the head of the slope and shut it behind her. I opened the glasshouse door and held it while she brought the cart through, and shut that too.

A glasshouse at evening is a thing worth stopping in the door for. The day's heat rises off the beds slow and green-smelling in the dark, and the panes go from gold to grey to a black shine, and the whole long house ticks and settles around you like a ship at anchor. We came up the center walk between the full beds, past Tam's careful lettuces and the forcing-bays and the propagation benches with their winter stock coming on, all the way north to the bare corner, where the ground had been waiting for a year with nothing asked of it.

She stopped the cart at the head of the last bay and we went the rest of it slow. The one Series lamp at that end had been left burning, as it was left burning most nights that autumn, and it made a single amber pool against the north wall and a long stripe of itself down the wet flags of the walk, and everything outside the pool was the deep green dark of a house full of leaves after sundown. The vents were shut. At the cold end the stove was in, and you could hear it if you stopped and gave it your attention.

I had stood in that corner a hundred times. It looked altogether different with a cart drawn up in front of it.

She had brought her grandmother's trowel. I saw it come out of the cart-basket and I understood the size of the evening entire.

We got the wrappings off the crock first. There were three of them: a horse blanket, a piece of oilcloth, and under that a shawl that had been somebody's before ever it was a plant's, and she took them off in that order and folded each one square and laid it in the basket, and did not hurry any part of it.

And there it stood on the boards of a handcart at the north end of my hill. Wider than it was tall, chipped white at the rim in two places where thirty years of cellar had knocked against it, and standing up out of the dark soil in it the low woody dark-leaved thing that had been in her family longer than her family could account for.

I had seen it perhaps five times, and every time I was surprised by how little there was of it.

"You'll want to say something over it," I said. "Most people would."

"It's a plant." She turned the trowel once in her hand, checking the haft the way she checked everything, by use. "It doesn't hear me."

"Your grandmother said nothing either?"

"My gran said the date. Out loud, once, to nobody. Then she wrote it down twice, in the book and on the crock. She held that a word said and not written was a word wasted." She looked at the bare ground a moment, and then at me. "You can stand out of my light. I'm not as young as the last time I dug in the dark."

So I stood out of her light.

She took the lamp down off its hook and set it on the flags at the corner's edge, low, at about the height of a kneeling person, so that its light went along the ground instead of down onto it. Then she put her coat over the end of the nearest bench, and rolled her sleeves back off her wrists, and knelt at the edge of the bed, and before she began she laid her hand flat on the grit and left it there a moment. Whether that was a grower testing her ground or something else, I did not ask.

Past the blank north wall the ground went up in the dark toward the crown, and there was nothing whatever to be seen of it from where I stood, and I stood there anyway with my hands behind me, being of no use, that being the part I had come down to take.

I offered no working and she asked for none.

She dug the hole herself, kneeling, cutting the sides square. Then we tipped the crock together, one on each handle, and the rootball came out into her hands, thirty years of cellar dark in a net of white roots. She set it in the ground of my hill. She firmed it down with her knuckles and sat back on her heels.

That was all. No word over it. Her grandmother had been a woman of exact observances and no ceremonies, and the granddaughter buried the charge the same way.

The crock lay on its side on the flags where we had left it, empty, a rind of old soil in the bottom of it and its mouth toward the dark.

She stayed on her heels a while. I stayed where I was. There was soil under her nails to the quick and a smear of it high on one cheekbone where she had put her wrist up, and she made no move to see to either. Somewhere down the house a drip started off a propagation bench and kept its own time. The lamp on the flags threw both our shadows up the boarded wall. And the plant stood in the middle of it with its four paces of empty bed around it, looking as it had looked in a cellar in the village that afternoon.

"Thirty years I've watered a thing that wasn't thirsty," she said at last, on her heels, looking at it. "Fed it what it didn't want. Kept it out of every light it turned from. My gran said keep it, so it was kept. She never said it would tell me when it was done being kept. I had to work that out." She stood, and knocked the soil from her knees, and looked down the long dark house toward the south glass, where the valley's few lights stood small and warm in the black. "We'll see by morning," she said. "Plants don't lie. It's why I prefer them."

I walked her home down the way with a lamp, and came back up alone, and did not go into the glasshouse again that night, because the corner was not mine to watch.

She was at my door before the birds had started. She did not knock. She has never once knocked; the door became a place Brenna was standing, in the grey before sunrise, with her shawl wrong and her breath standing out in the cold, and she said, "Come down."

I got my coat. I asked her nothing on the way down and she offered nothing, and we went down the slope in a light too early to have a colour in it, past the barn with Snow shifting about in her box, and past the top gate standing open where she had come through it and not stopped to shut it. In the years I have known that woman that is the only untidy thing I have ever seen her leave behind her.

There was ice in the ruts by the gate, not thick enough to bear a boot. The grass had gone white on the slope. The hill smelled of cold iron, and the beck was loud, the way it always is before sunrise when there is nothing else going. She went ahead of me the last of it and did not once look back to see whether I was coming.

The house at first light was all pale glass and bed-steam. We went up the walk fast, and she stopped me at the last bench with one hand flat on my chest, and we went the final steps slow, the way you come up on a bird you do not want to lift, and stood at the edge of the north corner in the half-dark, and looked.

The plant stood in its new ground where her knuckles had firmed it. And every leaf on it had turned.

Not toward the south glass, where the light was coming up. Not toward the lamp, still burning at the corner's edge. Away from both. Every leaf lay over at the one same angle, uphill, through the blank north wall and the ground behind it, toward the cellar under the Study.

I did not need to take the bearing. I had taken it already, four times, on my knees in the cold, and had it written in my notes in a dead woman's year and mine.

We stood there a long time. The vents ticked as the glass warmed. A drip went somewhere off the propagation bench, and the beds let up their slow green breath into the cold.

"Wind?" she said.

"There's no wind in here."

"No." She crouched, and put one finger under a leaf, and lifted it, and let it go, and it went back to its angle like a needle. "Light, then. Say it's light."

"The lamp's the wrong way. So is the morning."

"I know where the lamp is." She stayed down there a while. "I wanted you to say it first."

"I mark it," Brenna said at last. It was her grandmother's phrase. Then she went and got the watering-can, because the beds wanted water whatever else the morning had brought.

I stayed at the corner a while after she had gone for the can, and then went up the walk and out, and shut the door behind me, and stood on the slope in the first of the sun with the valley coming up out of grey into colour underneath me. Then I went home and washed and shaved and got the fire up, because the bread would be up at eight and the day had the rest of itself to get through.

The rest of that day set about being ordinary.

The post came up with the bread, and there were three things in it, and two were ordinary. A bill from Kellard's yard. A page from Penworth on the quarter's figures, with one line at the foot of it in the tone he kept for anything that was not business, to say that his daughter Nerys had finished at her school and was proving, he was sorry to report, better at the work than he had been at her age.

The bread had come up in a cloth still warm in the middle of it and the bag had come up with it, and I took the two of them on the doorstone in my shirtsleeves with the cold coming up through the boards under my feet, and did not go in again for a while.

Kellard writes a beautiful hand, small and upright and without one flourish in it, and he rules his columns with a rule, and every line on that bill was a thing that had come up my hill on a cart: nails by the pound, lamp oil, two hundredweight of grit, a coil of wire, and the haulage on the lot set out by itself and, as always, a shade under what the man could have had for it. At the foot, in the same small upright hand, the terms as they had stood for two years. And under the terms, with no explanation attached to it, a line crediting me for a crate that had gone back down empty, which I had forgotten and he had not.

Penworth's page was Penworth's page: four short paragraphs of the quarter, in the order he has used since I was a younger man and he was a younger one. What had come in. What had gone out. What stood. What he proposed. The patents were paying what he had said they would pay, in a year when I did not believe him. The lamp trade had come off its best quarter and its dullest month, and he set the two facts beside each other and drew nothing out of them, that being his way of drawing something out of them. And at the foot of the column, where for two years there had stood a figure with a bracket round it, there stood a figure with no bracket round it. He made nothing of that either. He gave it no sentence. He set it down in the same ink as everything else and left it there to be looked at.

The line about his daughter cost him something to write and cost him more to leave in. I read it four or five times. He had written that he was sorry to report it. He had meant a piece of that and the exact opposite of it, and he had known I would take both and answer neither.

I set the bread on the table and the two of them beside it. Then I went back to the third.

The third had no signature. It was the third of its kind since the patents went up for licence in the spring, and I had kept the first two out of tidiness rather than suspicion: careful letters, admiring, patient, each of them circling in its own way toward the same small door, my methods, my notation, the unpublished parts, the parts a man keeps. Two other correspondents that year had done the same thing over their own names, and I had thought all of it the ordinary weather of a man who has sold a lamp.

I stood in my doorway with the third one and did not think that any more. It was the hand. Not the words, which were courteous and said almost nothing. The hand had been trained, and I knew the training, because the notation it had learned was mine. Not my trade's. Mine, the private forms I had built for my own working papers half a lifetime ago, and taught to exactly one person, and buried with her, as I had believed, in a sealed house, in an unread inventory.

I read it twice standing up, in the cold, with the bread going hard under my arm. Then I went in and wrote to Penworth and asked him to engage somebody discreet, and I did not say why on paper. The office's letter was in the desk drawer. I had stopped hearing that one most days, which is not the same as its stopping.

The letter took four lines and the better part of an hour. I had it written inside five minutes and then sat looking at it, because everything I had left out of it stood up off the paper louder than anything I had put in. At the last I let it stand as it was: that I would be obliged if he would engage somebody discreet; that the expense was mine; that the discretion was to be absolute, including as regarded me; and that I would explain myself when I had anything worth a man's time to explain. That last was a lie of a small and particular kind, and Penworth, who has been reading my omissions for better than a decade, would have it off the page before he had the seal broken.

Then I sealed it and put it out for the down post, and went and did an ordinary morning's work with my hands, because there is nothing else to be done with a morning of that sort, and because the vents wanted seeing to whatever else was true.

I saw them coming a long way off, and had the length of their climb to think about it, and got it wrong the whole way.

There were two figures on the flags far down, one small and one not much bigger, and they came up at a pace that told you everything before they were near enough to have faces. The old man went four flags and stopped, and stood with both hands on the head of his stick looking out over the valley as though the view were the reason he had stopped, and then went another four. The boy ran ahead and came back, ran ahead and came back, and somewhere below the elbow gave that up and walked at his grandfather's shoulder at his grandfather's pace, which for a boy with legs in him is harder work than the hill.

I went down as far as my own gate and made myself stop there. A man of that age does not climb a hill in order to be met halfway up it. And I should have had to come up again beside him, which turns into charity somewhere in the last hundred yards.

They were a good while yet in coming.

Cael came up the way at noon, on his stick, unannounced, with Soren for a shadow, and stood in my yard and looked about himself at the whole of it, the Study and the glasshouse shining down the slope and the storehouse walls rising on the east shelf. Then he delivered, in the manner of a man reporting weather, the news he had climbed a hill at his age to deliver himself: that the council had met, the proper council, all four of them and the clerk's book; and that it had voted, unpetitioned, unprompted, and unanimous, less one abstention on the grounds of conflicting interest, the abstainer being the owner of the inn at the foot of the way; and that what it had voted was this: the village would take up the wizard's way where it met the lane, and widen the whole of it, lane to hilltop, into a proper cart-road, graded and walled, at the village's own charge, beginning after harvest.

"It's a fool's expense," Cael said, in the voice he kept for things he had argued for. "Council money, on a road that goes up a hill to one man's gate. I said so at the meeting. Then I said we'd vote, and we voted."

"Four of you," I said. "And it carried."

"Three and an abstention, and I'd have had the fourth if she didn't let rooms to carters." He settled both hands on his stick. "You'll want to know how long it took. Everybody wants to know how long it took."

"How long did it take?"

"The clerk had the ink stopper off and back on again." He tipped his head at the boy without turning. "Soren. How many carts came up this way in the summer?"

"Thirty-one," Soren said.

"Thirty-one," said Cael, "and that's a boy who counts what he sees and not what he'd like. Widen it and it's sixty. That was the whole of my argument and it took a minute." He looked at me sideways. "You laid a way down to us, wizard. Seems the village won't be outbuilt. There's not a man on that council will say what the road's for, mind, so you'll oblige everybody by not asking."

I did not ask.

But I stood in my own yard while he said it and did a piece of arithmetic I had never thought to do. Three years I had lived in a valley that did not ask me things, and I had taken the not-asking for weather. Weather is nobody's doing. A question set down is somebody's, every time it happens. Cael at the well with his hand on his grandson's shoulder. Jorin looking at a wall bearing laid on a compass line and saying *for later* and leaving it there. Four councillors voting money onto a road and not one of them willing to say aloud what the road was for. An abstention entered in a clerk's book on the grounds of letting rooms to carters. Every one of those was somebody choosing, and choosing again, and I had thanked nobody, because I had never seen it being done.

Nor could I have. To thank them I should have had to name the thing they were declining to name, and the naming would have spent it. So I held my tongue. I have not found the way to pay it since.

They stayed. I had not exactly asked and Cael had not exactly accepted. What happened was that the afternoon went along and nobody mentioned going.

At about four he looked at the sky over the west shoulder and said, to the yard in general, "Light's going earlier every day of my life."

"There's a fire in," I said. "And food in the house."

"Well." He considered the middle distance. "There's no arguing with a man on his own ground."

And he sat himself down on the bench by my door.

I sent Soren for the cheese and then went after him, because the cellar under the Study is eleven steps down into the dark and the last of them comes sooner than a stranger expects, and I did not care to have somebody else's grandson find that out with both arms full.

We went down together with the lamp. What I remember is the cold of that place, the dressed-stone cold that does not know what month it is, and the smell of it, roots and flour and the ghost of last year's apples. He held the lamp while I got the cheese out from under its cloth and cut what we wanted and covered the rest. He touched nothing. He asked about nothing. He stood exactly where I had put him and looked up at the close vault over his head.

"It's colder than outside," he said, on the stairs.

"It is."

Then, two steps up, having gone on with it: "It isn't, though. It's the same as it was in the summer. It's the outside that's changed."

"That is exactly it."

He carried that up the rest of the steps and out into the yard, and then he put it down and did not pick it up again.

We ate at my table with the door shut and the fire up.

There was bread from the village oven cut thick, and the cheese, and butter out of a crock, and a dish of roots that had been in the ashes since noon, because I had known since noon that they were staying and had been lying to myself about it. And the last of the year's beans out of the glasshouse. They were the best thing on the table and nobody said a word about them.

Soren ate like a boy who has been up a hill twice in one day. He took a second helping of the roots when it was offered and a third when it was not. For the first while he said almost nothing. Then, with the edge off him, he began to answer questions, and it was his grandfather who asked them, in the flat unhurried voice of an old man who has put the same three questions to a child every evening of that child's life and has not once been bored by an answer.

What was asked was what he had seen that day, and what the beck was doing, and who he had spoken to. The answers came back short and complete and in order, with no opinion anywhere in them. The beck was down two hands on the ford stone. The oven had been lit late because the wood was wet. Ned Pardon had a pony loose on the lane that would not be caught, and had been at it, said Soren, since before the bread.

"He'll be at it yet," said Cael.

"He was at it yesterday."

Cael, having eaten, talked. He had carried that news up a hill and delivered it at noon in the shortest form it would go into, and found he had the better half of it still on his hands. He gave me the meeting a second time, at greater length and in less order, with all the parts he had left out at noon because they did not suit an announcement.

"Four of us and the clerk," he said, "and the clerk's book. And the book has more to say than the four of us together."

"Has it."

"It says what we did. We only ever say what we meant." He turned the cup round on the boards. "There was a lantern on that table. Meeting at four, dark at seven, and a lantern lit from the first word to the last, because it is a lantern and it is a meeting and that is how it is done. I have sat under that lantern thirty year and never once needed it, and I'd not have it put out for anything you could offer me."

"And the clerk kept his book."

"He kept it." He set the cup down. "Best-kept book in the county, that. Nothing in it but truth and crossings-out."

I gave him the fire end and kept his cup filled and let him go on, and he went on, and it was the best hour I had spent in a month.

The two of them sat at my table with the fire on the sides of their faces. They are not alike. The old man comes at everything sideways and the boy comes at everything straight on. But they sat the same, both of them a little forward with their forearms on the boards, and they ate at the same speed, and twice, when Cael's hand went out for the bread, the bread was already in it, and no part of that had been asked for by the one or noticed by the other.

There was a person missing out of the middle of that line, and had been since the boy was born. Neither of them said anything about her. Neither did I. The fire went on.

At the end of it Soren cleared the table without being asked and without being thanked, that being how it is done in that family, and stood the plates in the pail, and then stood at the fire with his hands behind his back in his grandfather's exact attitude, looking at nothing whatever, until his grandfather got his stick from beside the door and said, "Well," in the voice he keeps for that word, and put his hat on.

I gave them supper, the old man and the boy, and walked them down at dusk, and at the foot of the way, where the lane began and the village's road would begin its climbing in the spring, I stood a while after they had gone.

I could hear them a good way down the lane: the stick going on the stones with the sound of a slow clock, and the boy's feet beside it, and once the boy said something and the old man laughed, and then only the stick, and then nothing.

The evening was coming on cold and clear. Up the hill behind me the glasshouse held its last light, and under the crown a corner of dark leaves lay over toward a buried word. And below me, in the village, in the window of the inn, where in two years and more I had never once seen anything but tallow and firelight, there was a new light burning. Steady, unflickering, deep amber. I had made that colour.

She had never bought one. She had never asked, and I had never dared. But there it stood in the inn's front window, and I stood at the foot of my own way in the cold and looked at it a long time.

Light going both ways now.
