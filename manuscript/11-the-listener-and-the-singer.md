# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Eleven: The Listener and the Singer

I put off the reading of her grandmother's books for the better part of a year, and I would like to tell you it was out of respect, a man declining to open another soul's forty years until he was fit to read them. That was some of it. The rest was plain cowardice dressed in respect's good coat, which is the only coat cowardice ever wears that fits it well.

What finally sent me down the hill was the soldier's-heart. She had given me three cuttings that first grey afternoon, rosemary and grey sage and the soldier's-heart, and told me to water the last of them once a fortnight and no more and otherwise leave it be. The trick of most things worth keeping, she had said, was knowing which things those were. I had done exactly that, being a man who can follow one clear instruction even when he can follow nothing else, and some time in the dead of that first winter, when the valley was shut and the light was thin and nothing in the world had any business flowering, the soldier's-heart put out a single flower on the north sill, small and dark red and shaped like nothing so much as a closed fist, and held it there three weeks in defiance of every sensible thing a plant knows about February. I had sat and looked at it a good many evenings. And it came to me, watching a flower bloom in the cold out of pure contrariness, that I owed the woman who had given it to me a debt I had been a year not paying, and that the debt had a name, and the name was a chest of notebooks I had promised to read and had not.

So I went down. I rode, because it was a fair way to the far end of the lane and Snow had lately formed the opinion that she was underworked, an opinion she communicated by leaning her considerable weight against the barn door of a morning until someone attended to her. She had not taken to the new barn. She stood in it under protest and came out of it like a cork out of a bottle, and I had given up arguing the point with her, a mare being the one creature on that hill even less inclined than I was to be reasoned out of a grievance.

The lane to Brenna's ran along the beck past the last of the village and gave out at a low stone cottage set back among a stand of birches, close enough to the water that you could hear it from the doorstone. There was a drying-green stretched in the front window, herbs hung head-down in bunches gone grey and brittle and fragrant, and the whole place breathed out that green-and-bitter smell I had first caught riding in, cut stems and something clean and sharp beneath them, the smell of a place people come to when they are frightened for someone they love. I had bought small honest things of her across the year, a salve for the hands that stone ruins, a tea Lira swore by. I had never once been past the shop room into the house.

She was in the doorway before I had got Snow tied, which told me she had watched me come the whole length of the lane and decided, in the time it took, exactly how she meant to have me.

"Well," she said. "You took your year."

"I did."

"I'd have thought less of you if you'd come sooner." She looked me over the way she looked over everything, weighing it against some private standard she never troubled to explain, and then she stood back from the door. "Come in, then. Mind the low beam. Taller men than you have brained themselves on it and I've no more arnica to spare on fools."

I minded the beam.

Inside, the shop room gave onto a kitchen, and the kitchen was the house, low and dark and warm, with a fire banked small against the spring damp and every surface given over to the trade. There were pots of things steeping and things drying and things I had no name for. There were shelves to the ceiling, and the shelves were the first thing in that valley that had ever made me feel at home in the plain animal way, because they were ordered. Not tidy. Ordered, a different and rarer thing, every jar where the hand would want it and nothing where it merely looked well, the ordering of a person who uses what she keeps. My own workroom was ordered like that. I have trusted people my whole life on less evidence than a well-kept shelf, and been wrong less often than you would think.

"Sit," she said, "before you fall over looking at my walls. You've the face of a man reading another man's ledger."

"They're good shelves."

"They're my grandmother's shelves. The jars are mine; the order's hers. I never saw the sense in improving on a thing that already worked." She set a kettle on without asking whether I wanted tea, which in that valley was not a question anyone asked, and she stood with her back to me a moment longer than the kettle needed, and I understood that she was doing what she had told me at the door I had done the right thing not to hurry: she was coming to it in her own time. I have never in my life been sorry I let a careful person arrive at a thing by their own road. So I sat, and I looked at the good shelves, and I waited.

"The soldier's-heart flowered," I said, when the waiting had gone on long enough that it wanted a small thing put into it.

She turned around. "In the cold?"

"Deep in it. February, near enough. Three weeks it held the one flower."

Something moved in her face that she did not trouble to hide, which I was learning was her particular form of honesty; she showed you the thing and let you make of it what you would. "My grandmother could never get it to do that up here. Twenty years she tried. It wants the dark and the quiet and to be let alone, she used to say, and there's none of that to be had in a village, which is why the poor thing sulked its whole life on her sill and never once bloomed." She set two cups down. "And you put it on a hill that nothing grows on, and ignored it the way I told you, and it flowered in the dead of winter."

"I followed your instruction. That's the whole of the credit I'll take."

"It's not credit I'm handing you," she said. "It's a fact I'm turning over." And she poured the tea, and sat down across the small scrubbed table from me, and turned it over in plain view, the way she turned over everything, and did not tell me what she made of it, because she had not yet finished making anything of it, and Brenna was not a woman who spoke before she was done.

It was a good while and two cups before she got up, without a word, and took a candle from the mantel and lit it at the fire, and said, "You'll want to see the thing itself, I expect, before I trust you with the reading of it. Come down. Mind your head again; it gets no higher on the stairs."

---

The cellar of that cottage was cut into the cold ground under the kitchen, low and dry and older than the house on top of it, the walls gone the soft dark of earth that has never once been warm. It was where she kept what wanted keeping cold and dark, roots and the winter's store and the things of her trade that spoiled in the light. The candle made a small warm room of it and left the corners to themselves. And in the far corner, on a shelf of its own, in an old glazed pot no bigger than a milk crock, stood the plant.

I do not know what I had expected. Something stranger, I think; a story would have made it luminous, or vast, or writhing. It was none of those. It was a low, dark, tough-leaved thing, no taller than my hand, with leaves like cut leather and a stem gone woody with age, and it sat in its pot in the dark and the cold and the quiet doing, so far as the eye could tell, nothing whatever. It did not look like a wonder. It looked like a plant that had refused, across a great many years, to either thrive or die, and had settled its whole existence into the narrow country between the two.

"Thirty years in this cellar," Brenna said, low, the way you lower your voice near a sleeping thing without deciding to. "Longer with her before that. She had it from her own grandmother, and she never knew its right name, and neither do I; she called it the keeper, because it kept, and because it kept her company down here on the bad nights, and because it would not be killed. She tried, once, near the end, when her hands had gone and she couldn't tend it. Left it a whole winter with no water and no light, half meaning it as a mercy. It was exactly the same in the spring. Not better. Not worse. Waiting." She looked at it, and then she looked at me looking at it. "It doesn't grow. In thirty years it has not put out a leaf. My grandmother marked it in her book, every leaf it ever had, because there was never a new one to mark."

I crouched down to it. I did not touch it. There are things you do not put your hand to without leave, and another person's grief and another person's plant are two of them. But I looked at it closely, in the candlelight, with the particular attention I give a made thing when I am trying to feel the shape of the working inside it, and I did not tell Brenna the half of what I saw, so let me be honest about it now.

I saw three new leaves.

They were small, and pale at the base the way new growth is pale, and they had come up near the crown of the woody old stem where the light of my candle caught them, three of them, unmistakable and unmistakably young, on a plant that had not made a leaf in thirty years. And I felt, crouched there in the cold with my elbows on my knees, the same faint pressure I had felt lying on the crown my first nights on the hill, below hearing, below almost everything, the sense of a very large quiet thing turned very slightly in my direction; except that here it was not under me and vast, but small, and close, and sitting in a crock in a cellar a mile from my hill, and pointed, if a plant can be said to point, the way that oak in the north wood was pointed: away, toward something I could not hear.

"Brenna," I said. "When did it put these out."

She came and crouched beside me, stiff about the knees, and was quiet a moment. "A year they've been coming," she said. "I mark it. Three leaves." She said it flatly, the way she had said the thing about the flower, a fact turned over in plain view. "The week you took the hill, near enough, it started. I didn't tell you, because there's no telling a thing like that to a man's face without sounding a fool, and because I wanted to see would you notice." She looked at me sidelong in the candlelight. "You noticed."

"I notice plants," I said. It was true, and it was nothing, and it was the exact size of the lie I had decided to tell her, which was not a lie of fact but a lie of omission the size of a hill. Because what I did not say, crouched there, was that I had spent a year now lying awake on that hill in the dark at the two hours it chose to speak, a listening I had told no living soul of, and that this plant, in a cellar I had not known existed, had spent that same year turning, very slowly, toward the same silence I lay awake listening to. I was not ready to say it. I did not know her well enough, or the hill well enough, or my own mind on the matter well enough. A man does not hand another person a weight like that until he has some notion what it is he is asking them to carry. So I said I noticed plants, and I made to stand up out of the cold before my face could say more than I meant it to.

Brenna rose with me, and did not let me past. She looked at me the length of a breath with her grandmother's attention, and what she saw did not sit well with her sense of a fair exchange. "You've gone grey as that wall," she said. She was not unkind about it. She was only unwilling to let the thing walk by unnamed. "A man doesn't lose the colour out of his face over noticing a plant. I've laid out folk at their own firesides less shaken than you are this minute, and I'll not stand in my own cellar and be told it's nothing."

I said nothing, which with Brenna was the only answer that was not a worse lie than the one I had already told.

She took that in, and gave a short nod, as if I had in fact replied and she had weighed the reply and found it honest as far as it went. "No," she said, to my silence. "You'll not tell me what it is. That's your right. It's your own face, and they're my grandmother's leaves, and a body's allowed to keep the one the way I'm allowed to read the other. But you'll not call it nothing to me again." She lifted the candle so the light stood up between us. "Come up out of the cold, before I've two shaken things in this cellar to fret over. We'll say no more of what you won't say."

We went back up the stairs and left the keeper in its dark, and she asked me nothing further, then or after. But she did not once pretend she had not seen exactly how far the colour had gone out of me, and I found, a little to my surprise, that I was glad of it, the way you are glad of the one person who will not let you get away with yourself.

---

The notebooks were in a chest by the kitchen fire, a plain dry cedar chest with a good lid, and there were more of them than I had let myself imagine. Forty years is not a figure until you see it stacked. She lifted the lid and I looked at four decades of a woman's attention laid down in courses like a wall, small books and large, some bound and some only stitched, the spines lettered in a hand that got surer in the early volumes and shakier again at the end, and the sight of it did something to me that I had not braced for. I have spent my life setting things down. I know the particular loneliness of the person who writes what no one has asked to read. Here were forty years of it, kept thirty more by the one soul who would not let it be thrown out, and I stood in front of it and was, for a moment, unfit to speak.

Brenna, mercifully, was not a woman who required speech to fill a silence. She let me look.

"You'll not take the lot," she said at last, and it was not a question; it was her deciding. "I've thought about this. If I hand you forty years in a sack you'll do one of two things, and I don't like either. You'll set them on a shelf and mean to get to them, which is how good things die, on the shelf of a busy man who means to. Or you'll sit up three nights running and read the whole of it drunk on it and understand a tenth and think you've got the measure of her, which would be worse." She reached into the chest and took out the topmost of the early volumes, a small brown book gone soft at the corners, and held it a moment, and then held it out to me. "One at a time. This one first; it's where she started. You read it, all of it, slow, and you bring it back, and we'll talk about what's in it, and then you'll have earned the next. Forty years took her forty years. You'll not have it in an afternoon, and I'll not let you try."

"That's a hard bargain," I said.

"It's the only bargain," she said. "She's not here to protect herself from being read badly. So I'll do it for her."

I took the book. It was warm from the fire and lighter than it had any right to be for what it held. "That," I said, "is the fairest thing anyone has said to me about the reading of another soul in a good long while."

"Don't flatter me. Open it here, so I can watch you not race."

So I opened it there, at the kitchen table, with Brenna watching me the way a mother watches a stranger hold her child, and I read the first page of a dead woman's forty years, slow, because I was told to and because it deserved it.

I will not give you the whole of what was in that first book, both because much of it I did not understand until far later and because some of it was hers and not mine to hand on. But I will give you the shape of the beginning, because the beginning is where the pair of them got their names, and got them before she can have known what she was naming.

She wrote plainly, in the early pages, like a woman keeping accounts. So many days of listening, in such weather, at such an hour. The hill sounded at dusk and again before dawn; she had that from her first year, the same two hours I had come to by lying on it, and it steadied something in me to see a stranger thirty years dead arrive at the identical fact from the identical patient method, which is the nearest thing my trade has to proof. And she wrote of the oak. The oak in the north wood, which she went to as often as she went up the hill, and which she found, always, turned away, listening hard at something she could not hear, with no notice to spare for the old woman at its foot. She gave them names, in the second month of the first book, in a line I read three times at that table and have never since got out from under.

In the early book, in the shaky beginning hand, she wrote only this: *I think the hill is the one that makes the sound, and the tree is the one that listens. Two of them. A singer and a listener. And I cannot for the life of me tell what the song is about, only that it is not for me, and not, I think, for the tree either. The tree is only holding it. Passing it on. I will keep listening. Perhaps it will say.*

I sat with that a while. Brenna let me.

"She never did work out the rest," Brenna said, quietly, from the fire. "It's in the later books, what she came to think, and I'll tell you now it goes to a queer place, queerer than a singing hill, and I'll not hand it to you tonight because you've not earned it and because you'd not credit it out of order. She earned every step of it across forty years and I'll make you earn it across as many months as it takes. But that's where she began. A singer, and a listener, and a song that was for neither of them." She looked at me. "You've a hill that woke for you and a plant a mile off that grew for you and a lamp in your window that burns at her two hours. I think you began somewhere near where she began. So begin there. Read the girl before you read the wise woman. She'd have wanted that; nobody ever reads the young version of anyone, and it's a loss."

"I'll read her in order," I said. "You have my word."

"I have your book back when you've read it. That's better than your word; it's a thing I can hold." But she said it with the corner of her mouth, and I understood I had passed, that afternoon, some test I had not been told the terms of, which is the only kind Brenna ever set.

---

I rode back up in the last of the light with the small brown book buttoned inside my coat against the damp, and Snow, freed from the indignity of standing tied at another creature's door, made such good time up the giving-up road that I had to remind her twice that neither of us was being chased. The valley was going to its evening below me, the beck loud with the spring, a first thread of woodsmoke standing up from the Wray place down in the bottoms where a family that had come with nothing was making something out of ground nobody had wanted. Halfway up I passed the boy, Soren, sat on the wall at the turn of the track with his knees drawn up, doing the particular nothing he did that was in truth a kind of listening, and he looked at the shape of the book inside my coat, because he saw everything, and he looked at me, and he did not ask.

"Brenna's grandmother's," I said, because he had earned an answer to a question he was too well-mannered to put. "Her notebooks. I'm to read them one at a time or not at all."

He thought about that with the gravity he brought to everything. "That's right," he said, at last, as if he had considered the alternatives and found them wanting. "You can't hear a whole person at once." And he unfolded himself off the wall and fell into step beside Snow without being asked, up toward his grandfather's smoke, and left me to carry that sentence the rest of the way up my hill, a good way further than the boy had any idea he had thrown it.

That night I set the small brown book on the workroom bench beside the lumen, in the amber light the hill made, and I did not open it again. I had read the first pages once, slow, as I was told, and that was the day's ration and I meant to keep it. I sat with the book closed under my hand and the light coming up as the dusk-fail pulse came through the floor, brightening the way it did at the hour a dead woman had once sat in a village a mile off and written *it is not for me*, and I thought about a plant in a cold cellar putting out three leaves toward a silence, and a boy who could not be surprised by anything, and a careful woman who had decided, on the evidence of a winter flower, to trust me with the one thing she had left of someone she loved.

I was not, I understood, the first to sit on this hill and be answered. I had known that since the day Brenna climbed my track with her basket. But it was one thing to know it and another to hold the proof of it closed under my hand in the lamplight, forty years of a stranger's patient listening, waiting, like the plant, like the hill, like the whole of that quiet valley, for someone with the time to hear it out.

I meant to give it the time. It was, as it turned out, the one thing I had come there with plenty of.
