# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Eleven: The Listener and the Singer

The bottoms were not the only thing I had said I would see to and then left sitting. There was an older one, and it was on no list, because I did not need a list to keep it in front of me.

I put off the reading of her grandmother's books for the better part of a year. I would like to tell you it was out of respect, a man declining to open another soul's forty years until he was fit to read them. That was some of it. The rest was cowardice dressed in respect's good coat.

What finally sent me down the hill was the soldier's-heart. She had given me three cuttings that first grey afternoon, rosemary and grey sage and the soldier's-heart, and told me to water the last of them once a fortnight and no more and otherwise leave it be. The trick of most things worth keeping, she had said, was knowing which things those were. I did exactly that. Some time in the dead of that first winter, when the valley was shut and the light was thin and nothing in the world had any business flowering, the soldier's-heart put out one flower on the north sill, small and dark red and shaped like a closed fist, and held it there three weeks. I sat and looked at it a good many evenings.

Those evenings had a shape to them. I would come in off the crown with the cold still in my hands, set the kettle over the fire, and go and stand at the north sill while it made up its mind, because standing there was the last unhurried thing left in the day. The three cuttings sat in a row in three unmatched pots. The rosemary did what rosemary does in a hard winter, without event. The grey sage had gone the colour of a wet slate roof and stopped there. Between the two of them the soldier's-heart held its one flower up out of the leaves on a stem no thicker than drawn wire, dark red, shut tight, the whole of it not much bigger than the ball of my thumb.

I watered it once a fortnight and no more, as I had been told, and I kept the count of the fortnights on the back leaf of the book I set my lamp figures in, because I did not trust myself to remember a thing that only wanted doing twice a month.

I had handled fine work in my life. Blown glass, drawn wire, once an escapement no wider than a thumbnail. One night I took the jeweller's glass to that flower on honest professional grounds, wanting to see how the petals were folded. They were not folded. They had grown that way from the first, shut around nothing whatever, and there was no seam anywhere in it for a man to get his eye into.

Outside the north window the light would be going off the snow on the crown, blue first and then no colour at all, and past the crown the ground fell away to the meadow and the black line of the wood, with nothing standing up out of any of it that a man could take for company. Then the light in the room would change. The lumen was down at the far end of the bench and came up at its own hour whether anybody was watching it or not, and the sill would go from grey to amber without one thing in the room appearing to move, and the flower would take that colour and hold it, dark red going to garnet.

I drank my tea standing up on those nights. Then I went back to the bench and got on with the work. Not always at once.

And it came to me, watching a flower hold on in the cold, that I owed the woman who had given it to me a debt I had been a year not paying, and that the debt had a name, and the name was a chest of notebooks I had promised to read and had not.

So I went down. I rode, because it was a fair way to the far end of the lane and Snow had stood a week in the new barn with nothing asked of her. She had never taken to that barn. She had her head over the half-door before I was across the yard, and she stood for the saddle without complaint, and went out of the gate faster than I wanted her to.

The track down was soft and the beck below it was loud. Late spring in that valley smells of wet stone and cut grass and, if the wind is off the low road, of somebody's byre.

The village was at its middle-afternoon. A woman coming up from the beck with two buckets set them down to let us by, and got a nod for it, and gave me one back. The hammer was going at the top of the street, unhurried, the way it went most days. Somebody's washing stood out stiff on a line in the wind.

I stopped at Kellard's, because I had said I would, and because he ran my carriage for me and was owed the sight of my face now and again.

His yard is the bank and the post of that village and looks like neither. It is a gate, a barrow, a lean-to of stacked crates, and a store with a floor worn into a hollow along the line a man walks from the door to the counter. He came out of the back with his sleeves turned and his book already under his arm. He had seen me the length of the street and had taken the time to think about it.

"Two went down on the last carriage," he said. "Not three. The third's collar wasn't on it."

"The third's collar isn't made."

"That's what I put." He opened the book on the counter and turned it round so I could see the entry. He did that every time, and I had never once needed it. His hand is small and upright and entirely legible, the hand of a man who has been asked to prove a thing at least once in his life and did not enjoy it. "Straw and the crating, against you. Carriage, against you. What's owing to you sits under it and it isn't much, and I'll not pretend it is."

"It isn't meant to be much."

"No." He looked at that for a moment as though it were a figure he could not get to add. "Essa's had two in the shop asking after the like of hers. One of them from over the far side. I told them what I tell anybody. I don't hold them and I can't say when."

"That's the truth, and it's the useful thing to say."

"It's the only thing to say." He wrote something short in the book, and turned it back round, and shut it. Then he stood with one hand flat on the cover a moment. "Wednesday, mind. If you want the carrier's dates off me, come Tuesday or come Thursday. He'll have me up the path on Wednesday and I'll not be here to be asked."

I said Tuesday would do.

It was not until I was halfway down the lane that I worked out who *he* was. The path was Cael's path, and Wednesday was Cael's day, and I had just been handed a piece of the parish's calendar and not a piece of Kellard's at all. Those are two separate reckonings in that valley. I had been in it a year and had not got them straight.

He came out after me while I was untying Snow, carrying a half-sack in the crook of his arm, and set it against the wall without offering it.

"Oats," he said. "Went through a wet sack in the store and the mice have had the best of them. No use to me. You may as well have the carrying of it away."

The sack was dry. There was no sign a mouse had been at it.

"That's a shame about the wet," I said.

"It is," said Kellard. "Shut at six," he said, and went in.

The lane to Brenna's ran along the beck past the last of the village and gave out at a low stone cottage set back among a stand of birches, close enough to the water that you could hear it from the doorstone. There was a drying-green stretched in the front window, herbs hung head-down in bunches gone grey and brittle and fragrant, and the whole place breathed out that green-and-bitter smell I had first caught riding in, cut stems and something clean and sharp beneath them. I had bought small honest things of her across the year, a salve for the hands that stone ruins, a tea Lira swore by.

The salve I had gone down for the summer before, in the thick of the Study work, when my hands had got past the state a man can go on ignoring. Dressing stone does that to you. The lime takes the skin off, the grit works into every place a knuckle bends, and by the back end of that summer mine were split across the fingers of both hands and opened again each morning the moment I closed them round a chisel.

The shop room was four paces by five. A counter of scrubbed elm, a set of scales worn bright at the pan-edges, papers of seed laid out in ranks with every one folded the same way. The drying-green in the window took what light there was and gave the whole room the colour of weak tea. Brenna was stripping thyme off the stem into a bowl and did not stop for me.

"Wizard," she said.

"Brenna."

"What's wanted."

I said I had a small trouble with my hands and would take whatever she thought fit for it. She set the bowl down. Then she put her own hand out flat, palm up, and waited, and I laid mine into it, and she turned it over and looked at it about as long as a farrier looks at a foot.

"Lime," she said.

"Lime."

"You'll have been washing it off with water."

"A quantity of water."

"That's the half of your trouble. Water and lime are old friends and they get on best in a crack in a man's hand." She let go. "Grease it before you begin, not after you've finished. And you'll want this."

She made it up in front of me on the counter, without hurry and without any performance about it. Mutton fat rendered three times until it was near white. A spoon of honey. A green oil out of a stoppered bottle she did not name, measured by counting the drops under her breath. She worked the whole together with the back of a horn spoon until it took the light like new butter, turned it into a shallow pot, and pressed a round of waxed paper down onto it with her thumb.

"Four copper," she said. "And bring the pot back when it's done."

I paid the four copper. Then, because I had heard the tea spoken well of, I asked for the one Lira kept.

Brenna gave me a look I did not deserve. "Lira drinks that because it's cheap, and because it wakes her at four in the morning, and it does her no harm at all."

"I'd like some regardless."

"You'd like some regardless," she agreed, and weighed it out, and named a price that did me no favours and cheated me of nothing. While she was folding the paper I looked past her shoulder.

The inner door stood half open. Beyond it was firelight low on a stone floor, and the corner of a table, and the smell of the house standing behind the smell of the trade, a different thing entirely and every bit as particular. She finished the fold and creased it flat and set the packet on the counter between us. I said good day, and went out into the lane, and rode home.

I had never once been past the shop room into the house.

So it stood for a year, and would have stood longer, and did not, because a plant flowered on a north sill in February and put a name to what I owed. I came down the lane that late-spring afternoon with the debt sitting on me and no clear notion how to set it down.

She was in the doorway before I had got Snow tied, which told me she had watched me come the whole length of the lane and decided, in the time it took, exactly how she meant to have me.

"Well," she said. "You took your year."

"I did."

"I'd have thought less of you if you'd come sooner." She looked me over the way she looked over everything, weighing it against some private standard she never troubled to explain, and then she stood back from the door. "Come in, then. Mind the low beam. Taller men than you have brained themselves on it."

I minded the beam.

Inside, the shop room gave onto a kitchen, and the kitchen was the house, low and dark and warm, with a fire banked small against the spring damp and every surface given over to the trade. There were pots of things steeping and things drying and things I had no name for.

A wide shallow pan of seed heads stood on the end of the table under a cloth, keeping out of the draught. A crock by the hearth had a linen tied over its mouth and was working at something under it, slow and sour and in no hurry about it. Bunches hung nose-down from the beam in ranks, each tied at the stem with a different coloured thread, and one bunch tied with no thread at all. There was a mortar the size of a small bucket with the pestle standing in it. There was a jar of dark honey, and beside it a jar of something the exact colour of dark honey and certainly not honey.

I asked, because I ask.

"Nettle," she said, of the pan. "The seed, not the leaf. The leaf's a spring job and it's done and I'm sick of it." And of the crock: "Vinegar. Mine's better than what Kellard sells and it costs me nothing but the waiting."

I looked at the third jar.

"That's mine," said Brenna, and went on past it. That was the whole of the answer.

There were shelves to the ceiling, and the shelves were the first thing in that valley that had ever made me feel at home in the plain animal way, because they were ordered. Not tidy. Ordered, a different and rarer thing, every jar where the hand would want it and nothing where it merely looked well, the ordering of somebody who uses what she keeps. My own workroom was ordered like that.

"Sit," she said, "before you fall over looking at my walls. You've the face of a man reading another man's ledger."

"They're good shelves."

"They're my grandmother's shelves. The jars are mine; the order's hers. I never saw the sense in improving on a thing that already worked." She set a kettle on without asking whether I wanted tea, which in that valley was not a question anyone asked, and then she stood with her back to me a moment longer than the kettle needed. So I sat, and I looked at the good shelves, and I waited.

The kettle in that kitchen was a black iron thing with a lid that did not quite sit, hung on a chain over the fire, and it took its own time about everything. Brenna did not stand over it. She went back to what she had been at before she saw me coming up the lane: a bundle taken down off the beam and picked over on a board, leaf by leaf, the good going into a bowl and the poor into her apron for the fire. It is slow work. She did not hurry it and she did not skimp it.

I sat with my hat on my knee and let her do it. The fire ticked. The beck came in through the doorstone, never loud, only always. Above the ceiling a board took up the cold and let it go again with a small sound like a knuckle.

When the kettle began to think about it she warmed the pot at the fire and tipped the water out into the ash. She measured the leaf with two fingers and not a spoon, twice, and then a third pinch on top of it. Then she set the pot on the table between us to stand, and put her palm flat on the lid, and left it there, and looked at nothing much.

We had got about as far into the afternoon as two people can get without one of them saying anything.

"The soldier's-heart flowered," I said, when the waiting had gone on long enough that it wanted a small thing put into it.

She turned around. "In the cold?"

"Deep in it. February, near enough. Three weeks it held the one flower."

Something moved in her face that she did not trouble to hide. "My grandmother could never get it to do that up here. Twenty years she tried. It wants the dark and the quiet and to be let alone, she used to say, and there's none of that to be had in a village, which is why the poor thing sulked its whole life on her sill and never once bloomed." She set two cups down. "And you put it on a hill that nothing grows on, and ignored it the way I told you, and it flowered in the dead of winter."

"I followed your instruction. That's the whole of the credit I'll take."

"It's not credit I'm handing you," she said. "It's a fact I'm turning over." And she poured the tea, and sat down across the small scrubbed table from me, and turned it over in view, the way she turned over everything, and did not tell me what she made of it. She had not finished making anything of it yet.

The two cups she had set down were not a pair. One was brown glaze. The other had lost its handle at some point and had it back on with a rivet through it, and she had taken the mended one for herself without appearing to choose it. I had known she would before her hand got to the shelf.

So we talked about other things while the pot went down, as people do when the true subject has been set out on the table and neither of them is ready to pick it up.

She asked after the Wrays. That surprised me for about as long as it takes to think about it. Bess Wray had sent one of the children up the lane in their first week with an egg in each fist and a request for something for a cough, and had got it, and had sent two more eggs the week after that were not owed to anybody.

"She'll not take a thing without paying for it," Brenna said. "It's a good fault and it's a nuisance." She turned her cup a half-turn on the table. "How's the field?"

"Coming. Slowly. I'm told slowly is the only way it comes."

"It is." She let that sit. "There's been no smoke off that chimney in a dozen year. I looked for it out my own window the first night and it was there, and I'll not pretend I wasn't glad."

The lamp she had off me at the turn of the winter stood on the shop-room sill, in the clear end of the window where the drying-green did not reach, and I could see it through the open door, cold and clear and doing nothing whatever in the afternoon light. She saw me looking at it.

"It's on before I want it," she said. "That's my complaint and that's the whole of my complaint. I come through from the kitchen of an evening with both hands full and there's the light already up and waiting on me, and I've caught myself thanking it twice."

"You'd sooner it waited to be asked."

"I'd sooner I hadn't got used to it so fast." It was not a compliment, and she did not offer it as one. "I found what I wanted at the back of the third shelf at midnight in February. I've not been able to do that in this house since my grandmother died."

I said I was glad of it, and I said nothing after that, because nothing after that belonged to her. She let the gap stand a moment in case I meant to fill it.

"It doesn't burn," she said.

"No."

"And it doesn't want feeding."

"It doesn't want anything at all."

"Hm," said Brenna, and drank her tea, and let the whole matter go past her the way you let a strange cart go past on the road. She never asked me how a thing of mine was made, then or after.

The fire settled. Out in the lane somebody's dog took an interest in something and then thought better of it.

"You brought the salve pot back," she said.

"You told me to."

"Folk are told all manner of things. There's a pot of mine on a windowsill in half the houses in this valley, and I'd not get the whole of them home with a cart and a summons." She looked at me across the table with that flat considering of hers, and took her time about it, and I sat and let her take it. "You brought it back washed."

"That seemed to be the arrangement."

"It was the arrangement." She set her cup down and squared it to nothing in particular. "My grandmother wouldn't lend a knife to a man who hadn't sharpened the last one. She'd not explain it either; you were expected to know why, and if you didn't, that was your answer and hers."

I said that seemed a hard rule to be judged by.

"It's a fair one to hold," said Brenna.

It was a good while and two cups before she got up, without a word, and took a candle from the mantel and lit it at the fire, and said, "You'll want to see the thing itself, I expect, before I trust you with the reading of it. Come down. Mind your head again; it gets no higher on the stairs."

---

There were nine steps down and no two of them the same height, cut into the ground at whatever angles the ground had been willing to give up a hundred years ago. Brenna went first with the candle and did not look back to see how I was managing. The cold came up to meet us about the fourth step, a bare earth cold with stone and old sacking in the smell of it, and by the bottom the kitchen's warmth had been left behind altogether, hanging up there like a coat on a hook.

The cellar of that cottage was cut into the cold ground under the kitchen, low and dry and older than the house on top of it, the walls gone the soft dark of earth that has never once been warm. It was where she kept what wanted keeping cold and dark, roots and the winter's store and the things of her trade that spoiled in the light.

Carrots lay in sand in a long box against the near wall. A rack of onions had gone to paper and rustled at the candle as it went by. There was a shelf of stoppered bottles laid down on their sides, and a shelf of crocks with a year scratched into the glaze of each, and at the end of that a basket holding the last of the winter's apples with the two bad ones already taken out and gone. Nothing down there was where it had merely fitted.

The candle made a small warm room of it and left the corners to themselves. And in the far corner, on a shelf of its own, in an old glazed pot no bigger than a milk crock, stood the plant.

I do not know what I had expected. Something stranger, I think; a story would have made it luminous, or vast, or writhing. It was none of those. It was a low, dark, tough-leaved thing, no taller than my hand, with leaves like cut leather and a stem gone woody with age, and it sat in its pot in the dark and the cold and the quiet doing, so far as the eye could tell, nothing whatever. It did not look like a wonder. It looked like a plant that had refused, across a great many years, to either thrive or die, and had settled its whole existence into the narrow country between the two.

"Thirty years in this cellar," Brenna said, low, the way you lower your voice near a sleeping thing without deciding to. "Longer with her before that. She had it from her own grandmother, and she never knew its right name, and neither do I; she called it the keeper, because it kept, and because it kept her company down here on the bad nights, and because it would not be killed. She tried, once, near the end, when her hands had gone and she couldn't tend it. Left it a whole winter with no water and no light, half meaning it as a mercy. It was exactly the same in the spring. Not better. Not worse. Waiting." She looked at it, and then she looked at me looking at it. "It doesn't grow. In thirty years it has not put out a leaf. My grandmother marked it in her book, every leaf it ever had, because there was never a new one to mark."

I crouched down to it. I did not touch it. There are things you do not put your hand to without leave, and another person's grief and another person's plant are two of them. But I looked at it closely, in the candlelight, with the attention I gave a made thing when I was trying to feel the shape of the working inside it, and I did not tell Brenna the half of what I saw. Let me be honest about it now.

I saw three new leaves.

They were small, and pale at the base the way new growth is pale, and they had come up near the crown of the old woody stem where my candle caught them. Three. On a plant that had not made a leaf in thirty years.

And crouched there with my elbows on my knees I felt what I had felt lying on the crown my first nights on the hill. Below hearing. Below nearly everything. A large quiet thing turned a little way toward me.

Only it was not large here, and it was not under me. It was small, and close, and standing in a crock in a cellar a mile from my hill. And it was turned the way the oak in the north wood was turned. Away. Toward something I could not hear.

"Brenna," I said. "When did it put these out."

She came and crouched beside me and was quiet a moment. "A year they've been coming," she said. "I mark it. Three leaves." She said it flatly, the way she had said the thing about the flower, a fact turned over in plain view. "The week you took the hill, near enough, it started. I didn't tell you, because there's no telling a thing like that to a man's face without sounding a fool, and because I wanted to see would you notice." She looked at me sidelong in the candlelight. "You noticed."

"I notice plants," I said. It was true. It was also nothing. It was not a lie of fact but a lie of omission the size of a hill. What I did not say, crouched there, was that I had spent a year lying awake on that hill in the dark at the two hours it chose to speak, a listening I had told no living soul of, and that this plant, in a cellar I had not known existed, had spent the same year turning toward the same silence. I was not ready to say it. I did not know her well enough, or the hill well enough, or my own mind on it well enough. So I said I noticed plants, and I made to stand up out of the cold before my face said more than I meant it to.

Brenna rose with me, and did not let me past. She looked at me the length of a breath with her grandmother's attention. "You've gone grey as that wall," she said. "A man doesn't lose the colour out of his face over noticing a plant. I've laid out folk at their own firesides less shaken than you are this minute, and I'll not stand in my own cellar and be told it's nothing."

I said nothing, which with Brenna was the only answer that was not a worse lie than the one I had already told.

She took that in, and gave a short nod, as if I had in fact replied and she had weighed the reply and found it honest as far as it went. "No," she said, to my silence. "You'll not tell me what it is. That's your right. It's your own face, and they're my grandmother's leaves, and a body's allowed to keep the one the way I'm allowed to read the other. But you'll not call it nothing to me again." She lifted the candle so the light stood up between us. "Come up out of the cold, before I've two shaken things in this cellar to fret over. We'll say no more of what you won't say."

The notebooks were down there too. That was the other reason we had come down, and the reason she had brought the candle and not a lamp, and I understood it only when she turned from the plant and lifted her light toward the wall behind me. Paper wants what roots want, she said: cold, and dry, and dark, and left alone. Her grandmother had put them below the year she stopped trusting the cottage roof, and they had sat out four decades of weather down there and come to no harm at all.

We went back up the stairs with our arms full and left the keeper in its dark. She had a crock under one arm and a fist of roots in the other hand, the ordinary errand done at the same time as the extraordinary one.

The kitchen came at us out of the stairwell as we climbed, warm and smoky and smelling of the whole trade at once, and I took the warmth back off its hook at the top of the stairs and put it on again. I remembered the beam this time. She saw me remember it and said nothing about it.

The tea in the pot had gone stone cold on the table. She laid the back of her hand against the belly of it, made a short sound, and set it in on the hearth to take the chill off. Then she swept a space clear on the scrubbed boards with her forearm and set the crock and the roots down out of the way at the end.

"Sit," she said. "You've a while yet before the light goes."

I sat. My hands had gone cold down there, and when the pot had warmed she poured for me again, and I put both hands round the cup and did not apologise for wanting it. Outside the doorstone the beck went on with its spring business. Down the lane somebody shut a gate, and then shut it again, because it had not caught the first time.

She asked me nothing further, then or after. She did not once pretend she had not seen how far the colour had gone out of me, either. I sat on in her kitchen with both hands round the cup, in no hurry at all to be up my own hill. I had not sat in anybody's kitchen like that in three years.

---

They had been in a chest against the dry wall, a plain cedar chest with a good lid, and there were more of them than I had let myself imagine. Forty years is not a figure until you see it stacked. She lifted the lid, and I looked at four decades of a woman's attention laid down in courses like a wall, small books and large, some bound and some only stitched, the spines lettered in a hand that got surer in the early volumes and shakier again at the end. I had spent my life setting things down that nobody had asked me to set down. Here were forty years of it, kept thirty more by the one soul who would not let it be thrown out. I stood in front of it and was, for a moment, unfit to speak.

Brenna did not require speech to fill a silence. She let me look.

She held the lid propped on her forearm, and the cedar came up out of the chest at me, dry and sweet with a bitterness at the back of it, the smell of a box that has done one job its whole life and never been asked to do another. The books lay in courses with the spines outward, packed close enough that not one of them could lean.

Some were bound in calf gone the colour of an old saddle. Some were only stitched at the fold with linen thread, the covers cut from whatever had been going that year: a piece of grey pasteboard, the back board of a ledger with another hand's ruling still on it. There were no titles anywhere. Where a spine was lettered at all it carried a year, or a season and a year, in ink that had gone the brown that black ink goes.

"I turn them once a year," Brenna said. "End of the summer, when that wall's at its driest. Every one out, every one looked at, every one back down. It takes me a day."

"Every year."

"Every year since I was nineteen." She said it the way a woman gives you the size of a field. "There's a trap set behind the chest and I bait it whether I've seen anything or not. You lose a thing like this to the mice or you lose it to the damp. The damp's the slower of the two and I'd not back myself against either of them."

Then she said nothing for a while, with the lid still up on her arm and the candle standing on the crock shelf behind her.

"You'll not take the lot," she said at last, and it was not a question; it was her deciding. "I've thought about this. If I hand you forty years in a sack you'll do one of two things, and I don't like either. You'll set them on a shelf and mean to get to them, which is how good things die, on the shelf of a busy man who means to. Or you'll sit up three nights running and read the whole of it drunk on it and understand a tenth and think you've got the measure of her, which would be worse." She reached into the chest and took out the topmost of the early volumes, a small brown book gone soft at the corners, and held it a moment, and then held it out to me. "One at a time. This one first; it's where she started. You read it, all of it, slow, and you bring it back, and we'll talk about what's in it, and then you'll have earned the next. Forty years took her forty years. You'll not have it in an afternoon, and I'll not let you try."

"That's a hard bargain," I said.

"It's the only bargain," she said. "She's not here to protect herself from being read badly. So I'll do it for her."

She did not put it into my hands down there in the cold. She carried it up herself, and laid it on the hearthstone to take the chill out of it while she mended the fire, and it was only when the fire had caught and the room had its colour back that she picked the book up and turned round and held it out to me a second time.

I took the book. It was warm from the fire and lighter than it had any right to be for what it held. "That," I said, "is the fairest thing anyone has said to me about the reading of another soul in a good long while."

"Don't flatter me. Open it here, so I can watch you not race."

So I opened it there, at the kitchen table, with Brenna watching me the way a mother watches a stranger hold her child, and I read the first page of a dead woman's forty years, slow, because I was told to and because it deserved it.

I will not give you the whole of what was in that first book, both because much of it I did not understand until far later and because some of it was hers and not mine to hand on. But I will give you the shape of the beginning, because the beginning is where the pair of them got their names, and got them before she can have known what she was naming.

She wrote plainly, in the early pages, like a woman keeping accounts. So many days of listening, in such weather, at such an hour. The entries in that first year run to three lines and rarely four.

*Went up after the milking. Wind off the north and cold with it. Nothing I could swear to. Home by dark.*

*Sat on the top from the last of the light until my feet had gone. It came at the usual time and stayed the usual while. Counted two hundred of my own breaths and then lost the count.*

*Rain, and the fourth day of it. Went up regardless. Wet through for nothing and glad enough of the walk.*

*Before light, and that is six mornings together. The same hour as before, by no clock but my own bones.*

There are no names in that first book. Not the village's, not her own household's, not one soul she must have passed twice a day on that lane for a year. There is the weather, and the hour, and what she heard or did not hear, and the state of her own feet, and nothing else at all. I took it at first for a countrywoman's reticence. By about the tenth page I understood it was only that none of the rest of it was what she had gone up there to write down.

There is no wonder anywhere in those pages and no reaching after any. She was a woman with a cellar full of winter to keep and a trade to run and a mile of wet lane each way with a hill to climb at the end of it, and she set down what she had done and what the weather had been doing while she did it, in the tone of somebody entering eggs.

The book had been handled every day of its year and it showed. The stitching at the fold had been re-sewn once, in a thread that did not match. There was a ring on one page where a cup had stood, and in the margin of another a thumb-print in something dark, set there by a hand that had come straight in from the work and not stopped to wash.

Somewhere about the twelfth page I caught myself reading the way I read a report: down the middle, skimming for the useful, waiting for the part that would tell me something. It is a bad habit and it is twenty years old in me and I do not believe I would have caught it that evening if I had been alone in the room. I went back to the top of the page and read it again at the pace she had written it. It took four times as long. It was the first thing I had done in a year that felt like paying anybody back.

Brenna, over at the fire, said nothing about it at all.

The hill sounded at dusk and again before dawn; she had that from her first year, the same two hours I had come to by lying on it, and it steadied something in me to see a stranger thirty years dead arrive at the identical fact from the identical patient method, which is the nearest thing my trade has to proof. And she wrote, more than once and always in the same puzzled way, that the sound did not sit in the hill the way water sits in a bowl. It lay along something. She had a word for it and never troubled anywhere to explain the word, having made it up for her own use and forgotten it was not everybody's. She called it the grain. She wrote of listening along the grain and across it, and of the sound coming truer one way than the other, as a countrywoman speaks of a board or a cut of cloth.

And she wrote of the oak. The oak in the north wood, which she went to as often as she went up the hill, and which she found, always, turned away, listening hard at something she could not hear, with no notice to spare for the old woman at its foot. She gave them names, in the second month of the first book, in a line I read three times at that table and have never since got out from under.

In the early book, in the shaky beginning hand, she wrote only this: *I think the hill is the one that makes the sound, and the tree is the one that listens. Two of them. A singer and a listener. And I cannot for the life of me tell what the song is about, only that it is not for me, and not, I think, for the tree either. The tree is only holding it. Passing it on. I will keep listening. Perhaps it will say.*

I sat with that a while. Brenna let me.

"She never did work out the rest," Brenna said, quietly, from the fire. "It's in the later books, what she came to think, and I'll tell you now it goes to a queer place, queerer than a singing hill, and I'll not hand it to you tonight because you've not earned it and because you'd not credit it out of order. She earned every step of it across forty years and I'll make you earn it across as many months as it takes. But that's where she began. A singer, and a listener, and a song that was for neither of them." She looked at me. "You've a hill that woke for you and a plant a mile off that grew for you and a lamp in your window that burns at her two hours. I think you began somewhere near where she began. So begin there. Read the girl before you read the wise woman. She'd have wanted that; nobody ever reads the young version of anyone, and it's a loss."

"I'll read her in order," I said. "You have my word."

"I have your book back when you've read it. That's better than your word; it's a thing I can hold." But she said it with the corner of her mouth.

I rode back up in the last of the light with the small brown book buttoned inside my coat against the damp.

We had come out through an empty village. Kellard's shutters were to. The hammer had stopped at the top of the street and the smell of the forge hung about after it, iron and coal and scorched hoof, as it does for an hour after a fire is banked. The beck ran high alongside the lane and drowned out the sound of our own going. The book knocked against my ribs at every stride, and I put my hand flat on my coat over it once, and then made myself take the hand away and let it knock.

The valley was going to its evening below me, the beck loud with the spring, a first thread of woodsmoke standing up from the Wray place down in the bottoms where a family that had come with nothing was making something out of ground nobody had wanted. Halfway up I passed the boy, Soren, sat on the wall at the turn of the track with his knees drawn up, doing the particular nothing he did that was in truth a kind of listening, and he looked at the shape of the book inside my coat, because he saw everything, and he looked at me, and he did not ask.

"Brenna's grandmother's," I said, because he had earned an answer to a question he was too well-mannered to put. "Her notebooks. I'm to read them one at a time or not at all."

He thought about that with the gravity he brought to everything. He had a bit of stick and the folding knife he had got for his birthday, and he went on taking curls off the stick while he thought. That was how he did most of his thinking.

"How many pages is the one you've got?"

"I didn't look."

He glanced up at me then, and went back to the stick.

"A hundred and something," I said. "It's a small book."

"How many a night?"

"A page. Two, if it's a good night."

He was quiet for about four curls off the stick. "A hundred nights, then, near enough, if it's half and half," he said. "That's past the harvest. If you don't miss."

"I'll miss."

"Everybody misses." He said it without any judgment in it whatever, a fact about the world he had already made his peace with, and went back to the stick. "Grandda misses the parish book. He does the whole of it in one go on the Tuesday night."

We watched the light go a while, him on the wall and me on the mare, and neither of us troubled the other about it.

"Who says when you've read one properly?" he asked.

"She does."

"That's right," he said, at last, as if he had considered the alternatives and found them wanting. "You can't hear a whole person at once." And he unfolded himself off the wall and fell into step beside Snow without being asked, up toward his grandfather's smoke, and left me to carry that sentence the rest of the way up my hill, a good way further than the boy had any idea he had thrown it.

He came as far as the top of the rise with me and then turned off down toward his grandfather's gate. He did not say goodnight. He never did.

The last of the light went while I was still climbing. I got Snow in and rubbed her down and gave her Kellard's ruined oats, and she ate them. Then water, and hay, and the door, and the bolt.

I went up the shoulder in the blue dark with the book buttoned inside my coat. The Study stood on the crown ahead of me with one square of light in its south window, low yet, the lamp not come up to its hour. I stopped once on the way and looked at it, the way a man looks at a lit window that is his own and has not yet stopped surprising him.

That night I set the small brown book on the workroom bench beside the lumen, in the amber light the hill made, and I did not open it again. I had read the first pages once, slow, as I was told, and that was the day's ration and I meant to keep it.

I had cleared the bench for it before I ever brought it in, because a book like that does not go down among files and filings and the ends of a day's work. The evening's business went to the far end and the boards were wiped. Then I laid the jeweller's glass out beside the clean part, and moved it away again, having reached for it out of pure habit and having no use on earth for it that night.

I sat with the book closed under my hand. The light came up as the dusk-fail pulse came through the floor, brightening the way it did at the hour a dead woman had once sat in a village a mile off and written *it is not for me*. I thought about a plant in a cold cellar putting out three leaves toward a silence, and a boy who could not be surprised by anything, and a careful woman who had decided, on the evidence of a winter flower, to trust me with the one thing she had left of someone she loved.

I was not the first to sit on this hill and be answered. I had known that since the day Brenna climbed my track with her basket. But it was one thing to know it and another to hold the proof of it closed under my hand in the lamplight, forty years of a stranger's patient listening, waiting, like the plant, like the hill, like the whole of that quiet valley, for somebody with the time to hear it out.

I had the time. That was the one thing I had come there with plenty of.

I left the book shut where it lay on the clean boards, beside the lumen, in the amber the hill was making, and went down the shoulder to my bed, and the light stayed up behind me in the empty room.
