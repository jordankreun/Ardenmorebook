# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Eleven: What Tomas Carried

The lamp trade, once it caught, ran on iron, which is a thing I had not foreseen and ought to have. A light that does not burn still wants a housing that will not warp. It wants a collar to seat the glass, and a bracket true enough that the working hangs square to the room, and all of that is a smith's business and none of it a wizard's. So from the back end of that first winter, when Kellard's carriage had carried the first small run down the valley and word had come back up it that he wanted more, I had become a fixture at the top of the village street, at the forge, where Tomas made me collars in twos and threes to a pattern the two of us had settled between us the way careful men settle a thing: slowly, and once, and for good.

I had a shape to my days by then that I would not have credited a year before. I read Brenna's grandmother a page or two of an evening, slow, as I had been bid, and carried each small book back when it was done and earned the next. Between the reading and the building and the quiet steady trade in light, I was as near to content as I had been since the life I had meant to live came apart in my hands. I tell you all this so you will understand what kind of errand took me to the forge that evening. It was the plainest a man ever had. I came for four brass collars. What I carried back up the hill was not on the bench when I came in.

It was the front of summer, and the light was long, and I had left the collecting late on purpose. A hot forge is no place to stand of a summer afternoon, and Tomas kept his fire to the cool ends of the day when the work allowed it. The street was closing itself down for the night as I came up it, shutters drawing to, the smell of someone's supper on the air, and under all of it the ring of Tomas's hammer, steady and unhurried, the last working forge in the valley still at it while the rest of the street put itself to bed. I have always liked the sound of another man's good work heard from off. It is one of the few sounds in the world that asks nothing whatever of the man who hears it.

The collars were done, of course. They lay on the bench by the door, cooled and true, four of them ranged like coins, and he had wrapped them in a bit of oiled rag against the damp of my saddlebag without being asked, which was Tomas entire. I have told you he kept the quietest forge I ever stood in, and that he would spend perhaps forty words on the whole of any dealing between us. Both of those things were true, and both of them were about to stop being the whole truth of the man.

He did not hand me the collars. That was the first thing, and I marked it. Any other evening he would have named his price, and I would have paid it, and we would have traded the short good-nights of men who think well of each other and have nothing left to prove, and I would have ridden up. This evening he finished the piece he was at and set the hammer down, which he did not do while a customer stood waiting. Then he turned to the fire and banked it, slow, with his back half to me, the way he had banked it the day he made the collar for the lumen and started to say a thing and thought better of it.

"That hill," he said.

Two words, and then the old silence after them, the silence of a man standing at the head of a stair he has stood at the top of for a year. And I understood, in the cooling forge with the summer dusk coming down blue in the doorway, that this time he meant to go down it. So I did the one useful thing I have ever learned to do at such a moment, which is nothing at all. I did not help him to it. I did not fill the quiet or make it easy. A thing a man has carried that long, you let him set it down himself, in his own time and his own way, or you have no business being the one he sets it down in front of.

It took him a while to come to it. I let it take as long as it wanted.

"I hear it," he said, at last. "Up there. Have done a good while." He was quiet again. "Two year, near enough. Longer, maybe. It's a hard thing to say when a thing began that you spent the first of it telling yourself was nothing."

Now here is the place a lesser evening would have gone wrong, and where I might well have gone wrong in it, and did not, and have been glad of the not every day since. I did not tell him he was right. I did not tell him what I knew, which was a good deal more than he was telling me, and older, and which lay awake with me at the very hours he was speaking of. That was mine. I was not ready to hand it to anyone, and in any case a man does not answer another man's hard confession by turning it round to his own. But I did not tell him he was wrong, either, which is the thing men do to one another most, and mean kindly by it, and it is the cruelest thing on offer. I did not tell him he was tired, or lonely, or that a solitary trade breeds queer notions in the quiet.

I said, "At dusk. And again before it's light."

He looked at me then, full on, which he did not do; he was a man who talked to his work and let you watch him do it. And something happened in that big still frame of his that I will not insult by naming, except to say this. A weight a man has held alone for two years changes him in the holding. It sets into his shoulders and his jaw and the way he stands over his own fire. I watched a portion of that go out of him in the space of a single breath, there in the red light of the banked coals, because another living soul had named the second hour before he could. He had not said before dawn. I had. That was how he knew I was not humoring him. You cannot humor a man with a fact he has told to no one.

"Then what is it," he said.

Not a cry. Barely a question, by the sound. A thing set down on the bench between us to see what I would do with it, the way Jorin had set the hill's two lifetimes on the table, the way Cael had set down his father. The one real question, from a man who only ever asked the one. "What is it, up there. That I've been hearing."

And I gave him the only honest answer I had, which was the hardest of the three I might have given, harder than a lie and harder than a comfort.

"I don't know," I said. I made myself say it plain, because he had earned the plain thing and not the soft one. "I've put that question to the hill every way I have the craft to put it, better than a year now, with more tools for the asking than most men are handed in a life. I've learned a great deal about the way it answers. I've learned not one certain thing about what it is."

I watched that land on him, and it was not what he had come down the stair hoping to be told, and I gave him the rest of it anyway, because he had earned the truth from me and not the comfort. The two are not often the same gift.

"But I mean to find out," I said. "I can't tell you when. It may be a long business; the hill does nothing in a hurry, and neither, I've discovered, do I. But I'll not leave off at it. And when I know what it is, Tomas, you will know. You have my word on that ahead of my word on anything. Whatever it proves to be, you'll not hear it from the valley, or hear it late, or hear it wrong. You'll hear it from me, and first, because you are the one other man who has stood where you're standing tonight and told the truth of it out loud."

He took that in the way he took the weight of a thing before he would lift it, turning it once to be sure it was what it gave out to be. Then he nodded, the once, and reached and picked the hammer back up, and I thought that was the whole of his answer and a fair one. But he said the other thing before the iron rang again. He said the word he used to close every honest dealing the two of us ever had, the word that meant a thing was settled and the accounts of it square.

"Even," he said.

And the hammer came down, and the forge took up its steady beat again as though it had never once left off, and I paid him for my collars and finished the wrapping of them myself and rode up my hill in the dark with the ring of his work following me the length of the street until the turn of the track took it.

I did not know, going up, that I had just that evening wound a clock that would keep time in my life for years. But that is what the giving of the word had done. From that night Tomas carried the thing differently, which is to say he no longer carried it by himself, and every time I came down for iron after, he would look up from the work and put the question to me without troubling to say it, only a small lift of the chin toward the hill above the roofs. And every time, for a long while, I gave him the same answer, and it was the only honest one I had, and it was two words, the way so much between the two of us was two words. We kept a slow patient time on it, he and I, the way you count the long gap between the lightning and the thunder, knowing the storm is real, and a distance off, and coming, and that there is nothing to be done in the meantime but count, and wait.

He would lift his chin at the hill. And I would shake my head, small, and tell him the truth.

"Not yet."
