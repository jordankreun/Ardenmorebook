# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Interlude II

There is a stone in the hill now that was not grown in the hill.

The hill knows the difference the way it knows the difference between the rain that falls and the water that was always in it. This stone came down from the weight's own hand and was set into the floor of the cut place, the cold room the weight opened in the hill's own body and did not, in the opening, push. It asked that too. The hill let the room be made the way it lets the frost into a crack, because the making went with the grain and not against it, and now the room sits in the dark under the crown, dry and square and still, and the stranger stone lies in the middle of its floor and is cold and clear and does nothing.

But it listens. The hill has no word for listening, having done it always and alone, but it has, in that stone, met for the first time a thing that does the same. At the fall of the light the hill rises, as it has always risen, and the stone takes the rising and holds it, does not warm to it, does not answer it, only keeps it, exact, the way deep water keeps the shape of a stone dropped through it long after the stone is gone. The hill does not understand this. It is not troubled by it. It is only, in some slow grain-deep way, less alone in the dark than it was, the way a thing that has spoken to no one for an age is less alone the first night something in the room repeats it back.

There is a new small weight in the cold room besides. A worked thing, dense and closed, that the great weight carried down the new stair one-handed and set in the driest corner and left. The hill does not mind it. It has held things laid in it before, bones and roots and the slow settling of its own stone, and it holds this one the same, without weighing what it is. It only notes, in the grain-deep way it notes anything, that the corner the small closed weight sits in is a corner it has always kept a little to itself, colder than cold, for reasons that are the hill's own and have no words even in the hill.

Above, in the small warm house on the shoulder, the weight sleeps. And the hill does the new thing it has learned to do, the thing it could not do a year ago because it had not yet been given the shape to do it with. It says him back. Dusk and before dawn, along with its own two ancient risings, it turns toward the sleeping weight on its crown and says the shape of him into the dark, the particular grain and lean of this one warmth among all the warmths that have crossed it, the way you might hum back a tune a traveller left in the room, not knowing whose it was or where it went, only that the room is not so quiet now as it was before he came. The hill has no name for him and does not know it is a shape it is keeping. It only turns toward him, and holds its cold corner, and rises, and settles, and is, for the first time in an age of dusks, kept company in the dark.
