# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Interlude II

There is a stone in the hill now that was not grown in the hill.

The hill knows the difference the way it knows the difference between the rain that falls and the water that was always in it. This stone came down from the weight's own hand and was set into the floor of the cut place, the cold room the weight opened in the hill's own body and did not, in the opening, push. It asked that too. The hill let the room be made the way it lets the frost into a crack, because the making went with the grain and not against it, and now the room sits in the dark under the crown, dry and square and still, and the stranger stone lies in the middle of its floor and is cold and clear and does nothing.

But it listens. The hill has no word for listening, having done it always and alone, but it has, in that stone, met for the first time a thing that does the same. At the fall of the light the hill rises, as it has always risen, and the stone takes the rising and holds it, does not warm to it, does not answer it, only keeps it, exact, the way deep water keeps the shape of a stone dropped through it long after the stone is gone. The hill does not understand this. It does not need to. It rises, and the stone keeps the rising, and at the next dusk the hill rises again, and something of the last rising is still there, kept, the way a footpath keeps feet.

Night on night the same thing happens, and the sameness does not thin. At the fall of the light the hill rises, and the stone keeps the rising. In the cold hour it rises again, and the stone keeps that too. The one underneath does not go faint for having the next laid over it. The hill has nothing in it to measure that with. It knows only that what goes into the cold clear thing stays there, and that the staying has run on past the turning it takes for grass to come up and seed, and that the thing has not filled.

There are two things on the hill now that take the risings, and neither takes them the same way. Up in the room of carried stone on the crown a light takes them and gives them straight back, gentled and warm, into a space where a weight can see by it. By the time the dark is old it has given back all it took and holds nothing. Down in the cut room the stone takes them and gives back nothing, and has nothing there to give them back to, and is not empty in the morning. The hill puts the same rising out to both. What is done with it after is not the hill's.

The room itself is a new kind of nothing. Where there was close dark packed solid there is open dark now, squared and dry, the hill's own stone set back against the hill. None of it pushes. But the cold standing in it is not the cold that comes down at the going of the light and lifts again when the light returns. It is the other cold, the one out of the deep layer, brought up to where the quick weights can walk about in it. Over the room's stone roof the grain runs on and past, unbroken, as it ran when there was nothing there but more hill.

There is a new small weight in the cold room besides. A worked thing, dense and closed, that the great weight carried down the new stair one-handed and set in the driest corner and left. The hill does not mind it. It has held things laid in it before, bones and roots and the slow settling of its own stone, and it holds this one the same, without weighing what it is.

It knows the difference between water that comes down and water that comes up. The one arrives and stands and goes and comes again with the turning of the light. The other has come out of the hill's south face without ever stopping, cold in the long part of the round and cold in the short, and the hill does not feel it arrive at all, only that it is going. It knows the difference between stone carried up and stone always here, and holds both, and does not hold them the same.

The round is at its long end. The dark between the two risings has narrowed to a thin thing, and what dark there is comes down warm and goes up warm again. The wet has gone out of the top of the good black soil and gone down after itself, and the grain up there has turned light and quick and loose. Below that it is as it always is. The roots have followed the wet down and hold there and take what they take. On the crown the grass has seeded and lain over on itself. The hill has been dry before, many times over, and the wet has always come back down into it.

The smaller quick weight still comes up, at spacings the hill has no count for. It lays a bare hand flat on the carried stone and goes very still, and holds the stillness past the stillness of roots. It does not push. It does not ask either, not the way the great weight asked. It only rests there with its warmth going down through the one small place where it touches. Then it takes the warmth away again, and the place cools at the same pace as all the rest of the crown.

Far out past the meadow, across the low ground, the old one in the wood has its long attention turned this way and holds it there. It has held it a long while. Nothing has come of the holding. The hill does not turn toward the wood as it turns toward water and toward warmth, and does not turn toward it now.

Above, in the small warm house on the shoulder, the weight sleeps. And the hill does the new thing it has learned to do, the thing it could not do a year ago because it had not yet been given the shape to do it with. It says him back. Dusk and before dawn, along with its own two ancient risings, it turns toward the sleeping weight on its crown and says the shape of him into the dark, the particular grain and lean of this one warmth among all the warmths that have crossed it, the way you might hum back a tune a traveller left in the room, not knowing whose it was or where it went, only that the room is not so quiet now as it was before he came. The hill has no name for him and does not know it is a shape it is keeping. It only turns toward him, and holds its cold corner, and rises, and settles, and is, for the first time in an age of dusks, kept company in the dark.
