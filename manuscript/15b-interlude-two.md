# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Interlude II

There is a stone in the hill now that was not grown in the hill.

It knows the difference between them, as it knows the rain that falls from the water that was always in it. This stone came down out of the weight's own hand and was set into the floor of the cut place, the cold room the weight opened in the hill and did not, in the opening, push. It asked that too. The hill let the room be made the way it lets the frost into a crack, because the making went with the grain and not against it. The room sits in the dark under the crown now, dry and square and still. The stranger stone lies in the middle of its floor, cold and clear, and does nothing.

But it keeps. At the fall of the light the hill rises, as it has always risen, and the stone takes the rising and holds it. It does not warm to it. It does not answer it. It only keeps it, exact. The hill does not understand this. It does not need to. It rises, and the stone keeps the rising, and at the next fall of the light the hill rises again, and something of the last rising is still there, kept, the way a footpath keeps feet.

Night on night the same thing happens, and the sameness does not thin. The hill keeps things too. A cold laid down over a warm, both of them going soft at the edges where they meet, until what is down there is one long grain and not the days that made it. The cold clear thing does not do that. What it takes in the cold hour lies over what it took at the fall of the light, and neither runs into the other, and the one underneath does not go faint for having the next laid on it. The hill has nothing in it to measure that with. It knows only that the staying has run on since the light was still growing, and that the thing has not filled.

Up in the room of carried stone on the crown a light takes what comes at the fall of the light and gives it back at once, gentled and warm, into a room where a weight can see by it, and by the return of the light there is nothing in it the hill did not just put there. Down in the cut room the stone gives back nothing, and has nothing there to give it back to, and is not empty. The hill puts the same rising out to both. What is done with it after is not the hill's.

Where there was close dark packed solid there is open dark now, and the hill's own stone taken up out of the cut and set back round the outside of it, and none of it leaning. The cold standing in that room is not the cold that comes down at the going of the light and lifts again when the light returns. It is the other cold, the one out of the deep layer, brought up to where the quick weights come down into it. Over the room's stone roof the grain runs on and past, unbroken, as it ran when there was nothing there but more hill.

There is a new small weight in the cold room. A worked thing, dense and closed, that the great weight carried down out of the light one-handed and set in the driest corner and left. The hill has held things laid in it before, bones and roots and the slow settling of its own stone, and it holds this one the same, without weighing what it is.

Warmth comes to the crown two ways. One comes down over the whole of it at once. It arrives and lifts and arrives again with the turning of the light, and the hill has had it always, and makes no more of it than it makes of stone. The other is what a weight leaves behind it in one small place. It comes to the ground already leaving. The hill has felt many of them come and go, and has kept none of them.

The round is at its long end. The dark between the two risings has narrowed to a thin thing, and what dark there is comes down warm and goes up warm again. The wet has gone out of the top of the good black soil and gone down after itself, and the roots have followed it down and hold there and take what they take. On the crown the grass has seeded and lain over on itself. The hill has been dry before, many times over, and the wet has always come back down into it.

The smaller quick weight still comes up, at spacings the hill has no count for. It lays a bare hand flat on the carried stone and goes very still, and holds the stillness past the stillness of roots. It does not push, and it does not ask either, not the way the great weight asked. It only rests there, its warmth going down through the one small place where it touches. Then it takes the warmth away again, and the place cools at the same pace as all the rest of the crown.

Far out past the meadow, across the low ground, the old one in the wood has its long attention turned this way and holds it there. It has held it a long while. The hill does not turn toward the wood the way it turns toward water and toward warmth. It never has. It only rises, and the old one goes on holding, and neither of them stops.

In the small warm room on the shoulder the weight sleeps. The hill does the new thing it has learned to do, the thing it could not do before the asking, because it had not yet been given the shape to do it with. It says him back. At the fall of the light, and again before the light comes back, along with its own two risings, it turns toward the sleeping weight and says the shape of him into the dark. The grain and lean of this one warmth, out of all the warmths that have crossed it. The hill has no name for him and does not know it is a shape it is keeping. It only turns, and rises, and settles, and holds its cold corner in the dark.
