# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Thirty-Two: The Needle

The lock I made in my old life was a good lock, and it did not open for the asking the way a latch opens. It opened the way ice goes off a pond, from the edges, in its own order, testing the question against every year I had spent not asking it, and I knelt there with my hands flat on the wood and held the question steady and let the work of it take what it took.

I should say plainly what that took.

A binding of that kind is not a wall and there is nothing in it to push against. It is an agreement, made once, by a man in a particular condition, and it holds for exactly as long as the man remains that man. Mine was well made. I was good at my trade in those years and I had been in deadly earnest, and a thing made by a competent man in earnest does not come apart because a later version of him has had a nice evening by the fire.

So I asked. And asking is not the saying of a sentence; the sentence is only the place you put your attention while the whole of the rest of you answers, and every part of you that answers otherwise is heard. The lock heard all of it. It went out through me at its own pace, in its own order, from the edges, and everywhere the answer was not true it stopped and waited, and I had to come and stand beside it and look at what it had found and not argue.

It found the drawer I had not planed in years. It found two pages of an inventory signed at the foot and unread in the body. It found a storage bill paid quarterly, punctually, on a room full of crates I had never once written to ask the number of. It found three letters begun to Hesper and two posted, and both of those about a house. It found the smooth sentence I kept ready for strangers who asked me at dinner whether I had ever married, worn round as a river stone from use. And it found the one I would have paid money not to have found, which is that for about a week in December, with my house down and the ash still warm and the whole valley on my hill being kind to me, I had been something not far from grateful, because a burned house is an occupation and an occupation is a thing to hold in front of you.

None of that could be answered by wanting to answer it. It could only be met, one item and then the next, without defence, which is a very slow business and cannot be done at any pace except its own.

And under all of it was the man who had tied the binding. Six years younger, in a cold room, doing the last competent thing left to him with steady hands because steady hands were all he had. The lock wanted to know whether I would be answerable for him too, for what he had shut and why he had shut it, and I found that I would, and that I did not despise him, and that took longer than everything before it put together. At some point the cold stopped mattering. At some point, and I cannot place it closer than that, the binding I had woven around that chest six years before came to the end of itself, quietly, with no light and no sound, the way a held breath ends, and the old black lid stood free under my hands for the first time since a spring I had walked out of alive and she had not.

I sat back on my heels then and was no use to anybody for a while.

My hands had gone past cold into the state where you can watch them doing a thing and not feel them do it, and when I brought them up off the lid the wood held the two shapes of them a moment in the cold and then let them go. My throat was raw. I had been speaking, some of it aloud, without any knowledge that I was doing it, which is a habit I have had since I was a student and have never trained out of myself. Behind both eyes there was the particular headache that comes of holding one fine thing steady for hours, the kind that sits down inside your skull and folds its hands. I have had it perhaps a dozen times in my life. I have never had it worse.

My shirt was wet through at the back and going cold against me on the coldest night of that year, and I noticed it the way you notice weather in another country. And the lumen, standing on the flags a foot from my knee, had come all the way down to its deep burn without my noticing it go. That was how I found out what the working had cost me in hours. It had cost me most of them.

I did not open it at once. I had the night, and the night had me, and there is an order to these things that has nothing to do with hurry.

So I stayed where I was and let my hands come back, and looked at the thing I had been kneeling in front of.

It is three feet of black wood banded in a metal that is not iron and has never rusted, and there is no keyhole anywhere on it for a locksmith to be insulted by. It is older than anything else I own. The corners have been worn round by hands that were dust before my grandfather was born, and the grain of the lid comes up under a lamp in long slow figures like water going over a weir. It is not a fine piece and was never meant to be. It was made to outlast the fashion of everything around it, and it has.

It sat in the northeast corner where I had set it the day the cellar was finished, because that is where the wall stands driest, and it had sat there through two winters of my going up and down that stair for cheese. The rest of the room was the room. Dressed stone, dry, the vault close overhead. The sacks of roots along the west wall with their tops rolled down. The apples on their rack breathing out sweetness into the cold the way they do all winter. A cheese under its cloth, crocks of butter, the flour in its bin with the lid weighted against mice. The winter's food and the black chest keeping the same small room, and the room making no distinction whatever between them, and I have come round to the room's opinion.

In the middle of the floor, flush in the stone, the blank lay where I had bedded it in the spring, a palm's width of grown crystal, clear and cold, taking down what came up out of the hill at a depth below my hearing.

And the hill was quiet. That was a fact and I noted it as one. The dusk-fail had come and gone hours before I came down. What lay under that floor was as still as I had ever felt it, still the way a house is still when everyone in it is awake.

When I did open it, what came up first was the smell, and I had not braced for that, and it did what such things do. Cedar, from the old wood. Paper. And under those, faint as the lavender in her mother's letter, the particular clean smell of the river house's linen-press, sealed in with everything else on the day I had packed that chest with my own hands and my own bindings and whatever of my life would fit in three feet of black wood. Six years, and the air of a finished world still in it, and I knelt in the lamplight and breathed it and let that be the first thing, because it insisted, and it had the right.

I will not walk you through the chest. Some of what was in it belongs to later days, and some of it belongs to no one but me, and the inventory of a man's sealed grief is his own, even here, even now. I took things out one at a time, in the deep of that night, and set each on the cellar floor in the lamplight, and looked at it for as long as it asked, and what I can tell you is that her mother had been right the way that generation of women is right, flatly, about the whole architecture of a thing they have seen once across a room: it had all been alive in there. Not ghosts. Not wounds. Things, hers and ours, patient in the dark exactly as patient as the ground they had been sitting in, waiting not to be mourned but to be carried out into some spring and given their share of the day. I had sealed that chest believing I was burying it. I had been, it turned out, keeping it, and the difference between those two words is the whole distance I had come, and I did not learn the difference until my own hands were lifting the proof of it out one piece at a time.

What I can give you is the manner of it.

A folded sheet of linen lay over the whole of it, and I had put it there, and it came up stiff with six years of lying still and held its own folds in the air like something made of tin. I set it on the floor behind me. Under it the packing was mine and was good. I had packed that chest the way I pack instruments, which is the only way I know how to pack anything, each thing in its own cloth, the heavy at the bottom, nothing given room to travel.

I took them out one at a time. I did not sort. I did not hurry. I did not put anything back. Each came up out of the dark into the lamp's circle, and I unwrapped it if it was wrapped, and held it as long as it wanted holding, and set it down on the stone, and reached in again. After a while there was a half ring of them standing round my knees on the cellar floor, and the floor looked like a stall at a market and looked nothing at all like a stall at a market.

Most of them I will keep. Two I will give you.

Near the top, flat, in brown paper and tied, was a book of paper samples. It was one of three we carried up to the bare east rooms of the river house on a bright cold afternoon so that she could hold them one after another against the light, and it was the one that never went back to the man who brought them. She had turned down the corner of a page. I knelt there with the book open on my knee and looked at a green with a small figure in it, three depths of the same green running up the sheet, and the corner turned over and pressed flat with a thumbnail, and she had said nothing to me about it, that day or any day after. I did not remember putting the book in the chest. My own two hands had put it there and I have no memory of the act at all, and that is what those weeks were made of.

Lower down, a bundle of her letters to me, tied with tape, and the knot was mine, and six years old. I turned the bundle over twice in the lamplight and read the top line of the outermost sheet, which was about a train. Then I set it down on the stone with the others and left the knot alone. I am aware how that reads. It is what I did.

The cold worked on me the whole time, in the way it does when a man is still: the knees first, then the hands, then one shudder that comes up through the whole length of you and is gone, and after that the long dishonest comfort of not minding it. Twice I got up and walked the length of the room and swung my arms until my fingers hurt, and came back. Once I picked her mother's letter up off the flags where I had laid it by the lamp and put it inside my coat, for no reason I could have given if asked.

The lower I went the heavier the packing was, and the more of it was flannel, and the slower my hands got. I know what I was doing. I had left the bottom of that chest to be the bottom of the night.

Her scarf was near the bottom, wrapped around a weight.

I knew the weight before my hands knew it, the way you know a step on a stair. There was exactly one thing ever wrapped in that scarf, the deep green one with the thread of copper in the weave that she wore from the first frost onward every year on the grounds that being cold was a superstition, and I sat back on my heels with the wrapped weight in my two hands for a long moment before I did anything at all, because this, of everything under that lid, was the piece I had put deepest, and I had put it deepest because it was the piece that was most mine, and therefore, by the arithmetic of those years, the most hers, and therefore the one I could least afford to look at.

The wool had gone stiff along the folds. The green was exactly the green; six years in a dark box does nothing whatever to a colour. The copper thread took the lamp as it had always taken a candle, one thread at a time as I turned the bundle, a small bright line running away round the curve of it and coming back.

It smelled of cedar and of wool and of the inside of that chest and of nothing else at all. I had been ready for it to smell of her. It did not, not in the least particular, not anywhere in it, and I put my face into it like a fool and made certain. That is what six years does, and the wool owed me nothing.

She wore it from the first frost until the end of March, and would have worn it in June on any excuse the weather gave her. She wore it over good clothes into rooms where it was wrong, knowing quite well it was wrong, which was most of the point. She had kept it as long as I had known her.

I sat on my heels and turned it over in the light and did not unwind it for a while, and the weight of it lay in the middle of the folds and waited, as it had been waiting.

I will tell you what it is, now, plainly.

In the last good years of my old life, at the far edge of my own trade, I built an instrument. My published work stopped a long way short of it; what the Conclave holds of my methods stops a long way short of it; I built it alone, over four winters, for the oldest reason in my line of work, which is that the theory said it could not be done and the theory was talking to me in my father's voice. Every compass ever made before it points along the world, iron answering the world's own deep grain. Mine did not answer iron's questions. I had built it to answer a different one, the question under my whole craft, the question a planewright is: not *where is north*, but *where is the way through*. It reads the architecture the way Ren reads a frame, and where the architecture stands ready, where a made place could be, where a door is possible in the deep grammar of things, it points.

I should say what four winters means, since I have set the number down as though it were a fact about a calendar.

It means a bench under a window in a house I no longer own, and a lamp, and the hours between eleven and three, which are the only hours in which nobody wants anything from you. The bench was deal, and I set it hard against the glass in a small room at the back, because the daylight there was no use to me and because nobody else in that house had a claim on the room. In winter the cold came off the window at my left shoulder the whole night through. I wore my coat indoors for four years and never once thought of moving the bench, because the cold kept me honest, a thing I told myself at midnight and did not examine by daylight. There was a clock two rooms off and I worked to it. Eleven, and I began. Three, and I stopped, not because I had finished but because after three the hands go, and a hand that has gone will undo in twenty minutes what it took a week to lay down, and you find out in the morning.

I have never done my best work at a reasonable hour. I do not recommend it and I could not have done otherwise. The house would go down under me floor by floor, a light and then a light and then the last one, and the wheels would leave the street, and somewhere in there the quiet would come up round the bench like water round a stone, and I would look up and find that I had been at it an hour without having noticed the hour begin.

It means eleven housings before the one that held, and the ten failures stacked on the end of the bench in order like a row of teeth, because I could not bring myself to melt them down while they still had anything to teach me.

They stood in the order they were made in, left to right, and anybody who knew the trade could have read those four winters off the end of that bench without asking me a single question. The first three I am least ashamed of, because they failed at the top of their voices. The first was too heavy and swamped the very thing it was built to carry, and I knew it was too heavy before it had gone cool, and finished it anyway, to have the weight of it in my hands once. The second sang. Everything I brought near it set the whole housing going like a struck glass, and I spent the better part of a month chasing that and got a lesson out of it worth the month. The third had no fault in it anywhere and no life in it either, and it is the one I kept nearest my hand for four years, because a piece of work that is right in every line and dead in the middle is the most useful object a maker can own.

The middle four were the long grind, and the grind is where the work actually gets done, and it does not tell well. The eighth and the ninth were nearly it, and being nearly it is its own kind of expensive. The tenth was a beautiful thing, and there was a week of that third winter in which I believed it was finished, and the week is on the bench too, in its place in the row, last of the ten. Then the eleventh held. I cannot tell you why it held and the tenth did not in any terms that would satisfy anybody; there is a place in that trade where you have stopped reasoning and are only attending to what your own hands are telling you, and my hands said this one, and my hands were right, and I could not have written the paper.

A needle for an instrument of that kind is not bought. It is drawn, down through plate after plate, every hole a hair narrower than the last, and annealed when it begins to argue with you, and drawn again, and the whole art of it is knowing when it has had enough, and nobody can tell you that, and everybody will. I drew more of them than I ever counted. I hung them on their pivots one after another in a still room with the fire dead and the lamp shaded and the house shut for the night, and I watched them, and they crept. Never much. A degree in an hour and a degree back, the small restless drift of a thing that has not found its rest. So I filed the pivot finer. I lightened the tail. I balanced them across a knife back and shaved the heavy end until the shavings were not worth the sweeping.

It means a needle I drew and drew again through three winters and could not get to hang true, until the fourth winter, when I stopped trying to make it hang true and understood that it had been hanging true the whole while and the fault was in what I had been asking it.

I can tell you where I was standing. Not at the bench. I was on the stairs with a candle, going up, and I stopped on the third or fourth step from the top, and stood there long enough for the candle to begin wanting attention, and then turned round and went back down and did not sleep.

It means a year in which I published nothing and answered my letters late.

There is a stack that forms on the corner of a hall table when a man stops answering, and it forms quietly, and it is the most courteous reproach there is. Most of what was in mine I could have dealt with in a morning. I knew that then; I know it better now. I used to carry the whole stack up and set it on the end of the bench beside the failures, in the honest belief that I would get to it at three when I stopped, and at three I would put the lamp out and go up, and in the morning it would still be sitting there being courteous at me.

It means being laughed at, mildly, by good men, at a table in Vethmark, and going home and going on with it.

It was a dinner. They were men of my own line, two of them better than I was at things I could have named, and every one of them had been decent to me for years and went on being decent to me afterward. Somebody asked what I had in hand. I was tired, and I was pleased with the eighth housing, and I told them, in the plainest words I could find, and I did not hedge a syllable of it.

There is a quality of silence that comes into a room when men who like you understand that you are serious. It lasted about as long as it takes to set a glass down. Then somebody laughed, kindly, and the laugh was a way out held open for me, costing nothing and asking nothing, and I did not take it, and the table moved on to something else with real grace and did not come back to it. Nobody was even wrong. By every argument available to any man at that table I was spending the best years I had on an instrument that could not exist and would have nothing to point at if it did. It did exist. The rest of it they had right for a very long time.

I walked back across Vethmark in the cold with my collar up, and I was not angry, and that surprised me then and does not surprise me now. What I was, was quiet. I let myself in and did not go up, and I was at the bench by eleven.

And it means her coming down at two in the morning with bread and cheese on a plate and setting it at my elbow and going back up without a word, so as not to break whatever she found me in the middle of. She did that, by my own count, on something over a hundred nights across those four winters.

February, the third winter, blowing hard. I was filing the tail of a needle that was going to fail. I did not hear her on the stairs and I did not hear the latch. What reached me was the room going a shade warmer at my back, and then the small clean knock of a plate meeting deal, set down on my left, clear of the papers, square to the edge of the bench, because she was not able to put a thing down crooked. Brown bread cut thick. A wedge of hard cheese with the rind left on. No knife, because there was a knife in my hand already. She was gone up before I had my head off the work.

I ate it at three, standing, with the lamp out and the window black and the wind still at it, and the bread was faintly warm yet, so she had cut it and come straight down and stopped nowhere on the way, and had been awake to do it, at two in the morning, in a cold house, in February.

I thanked her for it perhaps twice.

It had pointed at nothing, mostly. Four winters of my life, and the finest instrument my hands ever made, and I carried it half a year across two countries when it was new, and the needle hung slack in its housing like a plumb-line at rest, because the architecture of the world is finished almost everywhere, almost always, and doors that could be are the rarest thing there is. It found two in half a year, both known, both charted, both a century worked-out and walled. And so it went into its case, my beautiful pointless masterpiece, and in time I put it into her keeping, into the safest place I knew, which was never a strongbox, and she wrapped it in the green scarf against the cold on the grounds that instruments catch chills, being half her joke and half, I have come to believe, her exact understanding of what I had handed her, and her hands were the last hands that held it.

Until the black bottom of that solstice night, in a cellar under a hill, when I unwound her scarf from it at last.

It was wound about four times with the end tucked under, and the tuck was hers. I know a tuck. Mine goes over and flat; hers went under and to the left and pulled up snug so the whole of it held itself without a pin, and my thumb found it in the lamplight and stopped there a moment before it did anything.

I unwound it slowly, partly because my hands were bad by then and partly for the plain reason. Four turns, the cloth coming away heavier at each turn as the weight in it came free, and I let the last of it fall open across my knees so the thing lay in the middle of the wool where it had lain since she put it there.

The case I had made myself, after the instrument, out of quartered oak with a small brass catch, because leather seemed wrong for it and I could not have told you why then and cannot now. The catch had sat six years and had gone stiff. I worked it with my thumb as you work any catch that has sat, back and off and back again, easing it, not forcing it, and it gave, and the lid came up on its two little hinges with the sound a well-made small hinge makes, which is almost none.

It lay in its bed of cloth exactly as I had laid it. The housing had that dull inward glow metal takes by lamplight and never takes by day. There was a mark in the corner of the glass over the face, a scratch out of the second winter, put there by me and kept by me and never once mended, because a maker's own scratch on his own work is a date. And the needle lay slack across the face at the angle it had lain at for six years, resting on nothing, aimed at nothing, which was the whole and honest history of that instrument.

I looked at it a while with my hands in my lap. Then I put two fingers on the housing.

It woke at my touch. That is proper; I made it, it knows my hand; a bound instrument sleeps between its maker's touches and I had expected the waking as you expect a lamp to light. What I had not expected, what stood every hair on my arms up before my mind had named the reason, was that it woke already working. No slack needle. No plumb-line rest. The needle came up out of its sleep under load, straining, the whole fine housing of it alive in my palms like a held bird, and above me and around me and under me, in that same moment, at the hour of the winter solstice that is the bottom of the year's whole turning, the hill rose into its deepest pulse.

I have felt that pulse a thousand nights. I have measured it, audited it, learned its grammar, taught my ribs its count. I had never felt it like this, and I never have again. It came up through the cellar floor not as a wave but as a word, one vast slow syllable shaped by two years of patient practice on the learned lines of my own hand, so nearly language that my whole trained mind leaned into it the way you lean toward a voice through a wall, and in my two hands, in the middle of that rising, the needle of the only instrument of its kind in this world turned once, cleanly, without hunting, the way a thing turns when it has always known and was only waiting to be asked.

And settled.

And pointed straight down.

I knelt there and did not move, and the pulse crested around me and held, longer than it had ever held, and I looked at the needle and the needle looked at the floor, and every fact I owned about that hill stood up all at once and quietly turned around. The hum that ran under the crown and nowhere else. The quiet that lay on the hilltop a little deeper than the height explained. The ground that had thrown off every builder who told it and opened like a door for the one who asked. The salutation, sustained, on a rising bearing, toward the corner, toward the chest, which I had read four times and never once read right.

I did not move. I want to be exact about the body, because the body is what I have to be exact with. My knees had stopped reporting some hours before. The hand the instrument sat in I could not feel from the wrist down, and it was holding, and I could see that it was holding. The pulse came up through the flags and into my shins and through my ribs and went out somewhere at the back of my skull, and it did not break, and I counted it the way I have counted it a thousand nights, out of pure habit, because habit is what is left of a man at such a moment. I lost the count twice and began again both times.

Around me on the stone the things I had lifted out stood in their half ring in the lamplight, each where I had set it, and none of them meant anything more than it had meant an hour before, and I could not look away from the needle long enough to look at any of them.

And in the middle of the floor, flush in the stone, the blank lay taking the whole of it down, patient, below hearing, minding its one instruction. I have that record still.

The frost had come through the vent by then, as it did on the hardest nights, and flowered on the stone at the cellar's edge in white ferns that caught the lumen's amber. Away under the east shelf, behind a door of Tomas's making, in the hill-cut dark, my apprentice's first lamp burned its small steady burn, as it burns tonight. And in the middle of my opened life, on my knees in a cellar on the shortest night of the year, in the palm of a hand I could not entirely feel, a needle pointed down through the floor of the world, and held there, quivering very slightly.
