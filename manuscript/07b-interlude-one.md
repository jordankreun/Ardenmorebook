# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Interlude I

The hill does not keep years. Years are a thing that happens to the small quick weights that cross it and are gone. It keeps something slower: a long grain of cold and warm, of dark and dark again, and the two risings it has always had, the one at the fall of the light and the one before the light comes back. It does not know what they are. It only has them, the way it has stone.

It keeps the going-hard and the letting-go. Wet standing in a crack that stiffens and swells and opens the stone a hair wider than it lay, then loosens and runs and gathers and stiffens again. That is happening now. The light part of the round is shrinking at both ends, and cold is working down into the good black soil, a finger's depth and then another, and under it the grain goes slower. It has always gone slower. It has never once stopped.

Weights have come to its crown before. Rain, which is only weight that leaves. Beasts, which are weight that wanders. Wind, weight that leans and lets go and was never on the ground at all. Cold water that comes down slow and does not run away, and lies over the whole crown at once, even and everywhere and no heavier in one place than another, and lifts again in its own time, and was only ever water that had not yet run. Small quick weights crossing the crown and going on over it, leaving a little warmth in the grass where they lay down, and the grass keeping it a while after they have gone. Roots come down slow and stay and take what they take, and the grain closes round them and goes on.

Once, an age ago as the quick ones reckon ages, a weight that pushed: stone laid on stone, a shape forced down against the grain, a telling. There was a wet grey weight laid in between the stones, and it did not go hard. It stayed soft in the joints long past the time that kind of weight stays soft anywhere else on the ground, and then it only dried, and went on drying, and never once came to the going-hard at all. It lay in the joints the way dust lies, and gave way under anything that leaned on it. The joints stayed open. What is open takes water.

The hill did to it what it does to all pushing. It let the frost in. It loosened. It gave the pushing back over a season, until the pushing stopped and went away and the crown was bare again. There was a night in the middle of that with no wind in it at all, and nothing came against the stone, and a wall lay down. Then there was more of the crown open to the rain than there had been, and then more again. Then the shapes that had been walls were only long low weights lying where they had come to rest, going quieter under the grass a little further every cold, and nothing in them pushing any more, and nothing in them being told. That was neither triumph nor sorrow. Only the grain having its way.

This weight is not like that.

This weight came and lay down on the crown in the dark and was still, and after a long stillness it did not tell the ground anything. It asked. Down through the good black soil and the deep layer the question came, small and open and wanting nothing in particular, and the hill, which had not been asked in longer than the old one in the wood has stood, turned toward it. The way roots turn toward water.

Through all of that the weight lay down at night on the crown under a light skin of something that was not stone. It moved in the wind and lay where it was set down and pressed nothing at all. It went up while the light was still growing, and then it only stayed. Rain came off it and ran away down the outside and went into the ground where the ground was, and in none of it was there anything for the hill to answer.

Beside it there was a small hot place. A shallow hollow cut out of the good black soil, and ringed about after with the hill's own loose stone, brought up off the scree on the cold side and set back down into the turf. It came up warm at the going of the light and went cold in the night and came up warm again, over and over, and the soil under it dried a hand's depth down and stayed dried, and the roots in that one small hollow drew back a little and went on living. Fire has been on the crown before. Not often. Never in the one place, over and over, until this.

Then, through the long part of the round, many small quick weights came up the slope with hard weight slung between them. They came in pairs, and each pair went as one weight, the front and the back setting a foot down at the same moment, so that what climbed the track was not a pair and a load but one long weight going up. Carried stone, a piece at a time the whole way up, and up again, and up again, past any counting. There were places on that slope where a pair could set the load down on ground that was already level, and the hill had those places long before anything found them. They set the stone down at the last in the place the hill had already turned toward. Nothing was forced. Nothing was told. The hill came in under the footing and held it.

It sits there yet. It is more weight than the pushing thing ever was, and none of it pushes. The grain runs through it as it runs through the hill's own outcrop, and not against.

The light skin came away after that, at the cold end of the long light, and left the grass under it lying flat in a shape that stood itself back up inside a few days. The small hot place came up warm less and less and then not at all. What warmth there is on the crown now is inside the stone, and stays there, and goes out of it all night into the good black soil.

It knows the edges of the weight a little now. There is a smaller weight that comes with it and lays a bare hand flat on the stone and goes very still, the way almost nothing goes still.

There is a hoofed weight that comes up sometimes and stands. Hard points set close in under one long body, more of it carried at the front than the back, and it shifts its own load from point to point without going anywhere. The wandering weights have never stood on the crown like that. It puts its head down and the grass goes, and it leans, and the leaning travels down through the points into the grain and is gone in the ground before it has finished arriving.

There is a light on the crown now that drinks the fall-of-light rising and gives it back gentled, so that the hill's own slow risen thing burns where the weight can see by it. In the cold hour it takes the other rising the same way and gives that back too, into a dark where the weights are lying still and nothing looks at it. Both risings go out of the hill as they have always gone. Both of them, now, come a little way back into it. None of this is anything the hill has words for. It has weight, and warmth, and a turning-toward.

The cold is coming down harder now. It is in the top of the grain already and it will go further, as it goes every time. The wandering weights will come down off the high ground. The crossing weights will cross less, and then hardly at all, and the little warmth they leave in the grass will be gone out of it before they are off the slope. The going-hard will have its long turn. The hill has been cold before, and has carried frost in it through whole turnings of the light, and has let it go again.

Far off across the low ground, past the meadow, the hill feels the old one in the wood turn its long attention toward the crown, and hold there, as it has always held.

The hill settles the weight deeper into the good grain and holds it there. Above it in the dark a light burns steady, and the hill turns toward that too. The grain has changed its lie, the way a slope changes the year the water first finds a new way down it, and it will not change back.
