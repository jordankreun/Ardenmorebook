# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Interlude I

The hill does not keep years. Years are a thing that happens to the small quick weights that cross it and are gone. It keeps something slower: a long grain of cold and warm, of dark and dark again, and the two risings it has always had, the one at the fall of the light and the one before the light comes back. It does not know what they are. It only has them, the way it has stone.

It keeps the going-hard and the letting-go. Wet standing in a crack that stiffens and swells and opens the stone a hair wider than it lay, then loosens and runs and gathers and stiffens again. That is happening now. The light part of the round is shrinking at both ends, and cold is working down into the good black soil, a finger's depth and then another, and under it the grain goes slower. It has always gone slower. It has never once stopped.

Weights have come to its crown before. Rain, which is only weight that leaves. Beasts, which are weight that wanders. Wind, weight that leans and lets go and was never on the ground at all. Snow, weight that lies down whole over the crown and lifts again and was only ever water waiting. Small quick weights crossing on their two legs, going over and going on, leaving a little warmth in the grass where they sat down and taking it away again with them. Roots come down slow and stay and take what they take. That is not pushing. The hill lets them.

Once, an age ago as the quick ones reckon ages, a weight that pushed: stone laid on stone, a shape forced down against the grain, a telling. There was a wet grey weight laid in between the stones, and it did not go hard. It stayed soft in the joints a long while, soft past the time that kind of weight stays soft anywhere else on the ground, and then it went hard badly and in the wrong places. The hill has no word for the going-hard that did not come and does not need one. It only knows that the joints stayed open, and that what is open takes water.

The hill did to it what it does to all pushing. It let the frost in. It loosened. On a night with no wind in it a wall lay down. Nothing had struck the wall. The grain had only gone on being the grain underneath it, out and out and a little wider each cold, until there was nothing under the stone but the hill's own slow loosening. It gave the pushing back, patiently, over a season, until the pushing stopped and went away and the crown was bare again. That was neither triumph nor sorrow. Only the grain having its way, as the grain does.

This weight is not like that.

This weight came and lay down on the crown in the dark and was still, and after a long stillness it did not tell the ground anything. It asked. Down through the good black soil and the deep layer the question came, small and open and wanting nothing in particular, and the hill, which had not been asked in longer than the old one in the wood has stood, turned toward it. The way roots turn toward water. The way ground warms in the track the sun has lain along.

Through all of that the weight slept on the crown under a light skin of something that was not stone. It moved in the wind and lay where it was set down and pressed nothing at all. It went up. It stayed a season. It came away again and left the grass lying flat in a shape that stood itself back up inside a few days, and in none of it was there anything for the hill to answer.

Beside it there was a small hot place. Ringed round with the hill's own loose stone, laid on the ground and not into it. It came up warm at the going of the light and went cold in the night and came up warm again, over and over, and the soil beneath it dried a hand's depth down and stayed dried, and the roots in that one small round pulled back a little and went on living. Fire has been on the crown before. Not often. Never in the same place twice until this one.

Then, over a long turning of the light, many small quick weights came up the slope with hard weight carried in their hands. Cut stone, and more cut stone, a piece at a time the whole long way up, and up again, and up again, past any counting. They set it down in the place the hill had already turned toward. Nothing was forced. Nothing was told. The weights leaned and breathed hard and put the stone down where the carrying ended, and the hill came in under the footing and held it.

It sits there yet. It is more weight than the pushing thing ever was, and none of it pushes. The grain runs through it the way the grain runs through the hill's own outcrop, and not against.

It knows the edges of the weight a little now. There is a smaller weight that comes with it, quick and grave, that lays a bare hand flat on the stone and goes very still, the way almost nothing goes still.

There is a hoofed weight that comes up sometimes and stands. Four hard points set close in under one long body, more of it carried at the front than the back, and it shifts its own load from point to point without going anywhere. Nothing has ever stood on the crown like that. It puts its head down and the grass goes, and it leans, and the leaning travels down through the four points into the grain and is gone in the ground before it has finished arriving. The hill takes it the way it takes rain on a slope.

There is a light, lately, on the crown, that drinks the fall-of-light rising and gives it back gentled, so that the hill's own slow risen thing burns now where the weight can see by it. In the cold hour it takes the other rising the same way and gives that back too, into a dark where the weights are asleep and nothing looks at it. Both risings go out of the hill as they have always gone. One of them, now, comes partway back. None of this is anything the hill has words for. It has weight, and warmth, and a turning-toward, and that is enough.

The cold is coming down harder now. It is in the top of the grain already and it will go further, as it goes every time. The wandering weights will come down off the high ground. The crossing weights will cross less. The wet in the stone will stiffen and hold and the going-hard will have its long turn. The hill has been cold before, and has carried frost in it through whole turnings of the light, and has let it go again.

Far off across the low ground, past the meadow, the hill feels the old one in the wood turn its long attention toward the crown, and hold there, and wait, as it has always waited.

The hill settles the weight deeper into the good grain and holds it there. Above it in the dark a light burns steady, and the hill turns toward that too. It has waited a long time. It can wait longer. But the grain has changed its lie, the way a slope changes the year the water first finds a new way down it, and it will not change back.
