# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Interlude I

The hill does not keep years. Years are a thing that happens to the small quick weights that cross it and are gone. It keeps something slower: a long grain of cold and warm, of dark and dark again, and the two risings it has always had, the one at the fall of the light and the one before the light comes back. It does not know these are voices. It has no word for voice. It only has them, the way it has stone.

Weights have come to its crown before. Rain, which is only weight that leaves. Beasts, which are weight that wanders. Once, an age ago as the quick ones reckon ages, a weight that pushed: stone laid on stone, a shape forced down against the grain, a telling. The hill did to it what it does to all pushing. It let the frost in. It loosened. It gave the pushing back, patiently, over a season, until the pushing stopped and went away and the crown was bare again. That was neither triumph nor sorrow. Only the grain having its way, as the grain does.

This weight is not like that.

This weight came and lay down on the crown in the dark and was still, and after a long stillness it did not tell the ground anything. It asked. Down through the footing and the deep layer the question came, small and open and wanting nothing in particular, and the hill, which had not been asked in longer than the old one in the wood has stood, turned toward it. The way roots turn toward water. The way ground warms in the track the sun has lain along.

It knows the edges of the weight a little now. There is a smaller weight that comes with it, quick and grave, that lays a bare hand flat on the stone and goes very still, the way almost nothing goes still. There is a light, lately, on the crown, that drinks the fall-of-light rising and gives it back gentled, so that the hill's own slow risen thing burns now where the weight can see by it. None of this is anything the hill has words for. It has weight, and warmth, and a turning-toward, and that is enough.

Far off across the low ground, past the meadow, the old one in the wood feels the hill go quiet in a new way, and holds still, and waits, as it has always waited.

The hill settles the weight deeper into the good grain and holds it there. Above it in the dark a light burns steady, drawn from its own risen thing and given back changed, and the hill turns toward that too. It has waited a long time. It can wait longer. But the grain has changed its lie, the way a slope changes the year the water first finds a new way down it, and it will not change back.
