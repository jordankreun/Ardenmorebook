# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Three: Boy's Ready

Jorin came up the way on the second evening of those four days, with a roll of my drawings under his arm and the particular walk of a man who has read them and has opinions.

I had drawn the storehouse over the winter, in the evenings the glasshouse left me, which were few and late. It wanted building on the east shelf, into the slope, where the ground had already done half the work: the cave that Ren had found on harvest night sat back in that deeper green like a room the hill had roughed out and left for a tenant, dry, stone-floored, cool the year round. My drawings took it as they found it. Face the mouth, floor it proper, wall the front, roof what stood proud of the slope, and let the hill itself be the cold store's back and sides, the way a good root cellar is mostly borrowed ground with a door on it. The inner chamber I had drawn deeper, smaller, and I had specified its rear wall with a care that stood out of the page a little from the plainness of the rest: set true, dressed both faces, to a line I had drawn with more care than any store wall wants.

I had drawn it on the good board with my father's instruments, which is a sentence I could not have written two years before without putting the pen down. There were four sheets. A plan at a quarter inch to the foot; a long section through the mouth and both chambers; an elevation of the front wall with the door in it; and a sheet of details at half size, the lintel, the jamb, the rebate the door would shut into, the drain.

The rest of the specification was as dull as good building always is on paper. Front wall two feet thick in squared block, laid to courses, with a batter of an inch in the foot to throw the weather off it. The mouth faced and squared back to sound stone, no further than wanted. Floor of flags on nine inches of clean grit, laid to a fall toward the mouth, so that anything wet in that building would leave it without being asked twice. A stone lintel and not oak, because oak in a cold store is a slow argument that you lose. Slate over the part that stood proud of the slope and turf over the slate, so that from twenty paces up the shelf you would see a bank with a door in it. Vents in the front wall at the top, hooded, with a slide on each, because a cold store that cannot breathe is a cellar full of mould and one that breathes too freely is a cellar full of March.

And behind all of that, past the outer chamber where the barrels would stand, through the low way into the deeper room, one wall. Nine feet by seven. Dressed both faces, set to the line, jointed close.

Jorin spread the sheets on my table under the lumen and went through them the way he went through everything, in order, twice, saying nothing the first time. The rain had followed him up and was busy on the window. I poured what I had and let him read.

What I had was the end of a bottle Penworth had sent up at midwinter, a heel of yesterday's loaf, and some of Brenna's hard cheese. Jorin took the bread and the cheese and left the bottle alone until the first pass was done, which was how he drank.

He read standing. His coat steamed on the back of the chair and the room filled slowly with the smell of wet wool and warm paper. He went sheet by sheet in the order I had numbered them, which not one man in ten does either, and he touched them only at the corners. Twice he laid his thumbnail flat on the paper and slid it along a line, which is a builder measuring in the one unit he entirely trusts. Once he grunted. It was not a bad grunt.

The lumen sat at the corner of the table giving out its steady amber, and outside the rain thickened and settled into the sound it makes when it has decided to go on all night, and the second evening of those four days went quietly along, and I sat on my hands and let a man read.

Then he went back to the first sheet and did the whole of it again.

"Root cellar," he said at last. "Grown up and gone respectable."

"That's about it."

"It's what I'd have drawn." He said it flat, which from Jorin was a diploma with a seal on it.

Then he went at the work, which was the real reading, and it took the better part of an hour.

"Vents."

"Two, hooded, with slides."

"Make it three." His finger came down on the front wall. "It's a long room and the far end's the cold end. Two will damp at the back by the second summer and you'll lose a season of apples finding out why."

I made the note on the sheet in pencil. Three vents. I have that sheet still and the pencil is still under the ink.

"Fall on the floor."

"One in eighty."

"One in sixty. It's a store, not a parlour. Nobody will ever notice it, and you'll want it the year the beck comes up."

"One in sixty."

"Door."

"Oak, two leaf, with a wicket in the one."

"One leaf." He said it without heat. "Two leaf on a north face in this valley is two chances for the wet and one of them will take it. Make the single leaf wide enough for a barrel on a barrow and you'll never once want the other."

"Tomas's hinges."

"Tomas's hinges," he agreed, and that was the only thing all evening he did not argue with.

We went round the lintel twice. I had drawn a single stone across the mouth, five foot six of it, and Jorin did not like the bearing at the east end where the natural rock ran out into a seam. He was right. We settled it by carrying the front wall in a foot on that side and losing an inch of doorway, which I minded and which mattered not at all.

Then he said, "When."

"Now. It's built by harvest or it's built next year, and I would rather have the apples in it."

"Six weeks. Four men after the second week." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, which in him was a man doing sums. "Wall goes up in a fortnight if the stone comes when it says it will. Stone never comes when it says it will."

"And the crew?"

"They'll be glad of it. It's dry work under a hill in a wet summer, and there's not a man of mine would say no to that twice." He set his cup down, square, and squared it again. "One question, then."

His finger came down on the rear wall of the inner chamber, on the careful figures of its dressing.

"That," he said. "Dressed both faces, and set truer than a church. A store wall wants one face and no opinions. What's it for?"

I had known he would find it, and I had decided, some weeks before, what I would say when he did, and what I would not.

"For later," I said. "A wall dressed proper now is cheaper than a regret in ten years. Call it a habit of my other trade. We build for the drawing we haven't made yet."

Jorin looked at the sheet a moment longer. On the sheet, as on the ground, the far side of that wall was the inside of the hill and nothing else, forty feet of dark stone to anywhere.

Jorin looked at me a while. I have said he looked at walls to see whether they were plumb, and I will tell you it is a different thing entirely to stand on the receiving end of it, and that I did not enjoy it, and that I held still under it the way you hold still for a doctor.

"Right," he said, and rolled the sheet, and that was the whole of his inquiry, then and ever. I have thought since that of all the things that man built on my hill, the one I owed him most for was everything he chose not to ask.

The letter had come up that morning, with the bread.

Soren brought it. He came up the way at the run he had used ever since the flags went down, and I heard him on the last twenty of them and had the door open before he got to it, which pleased neither of us as much as pretending I had not would have done.

He had the bread under one arm in its cloth and the hill's post held flat and square in the other hand, the way somebody had taught him, probably Lira. It was a bright morning, the first proper one that week, the shelf steaming where the sun had got at it and the glasshouse throwing its long light down the slope. The boy stood on my doorstone and did not hand the post over, because he was looking at the top letter.

"That one's got a picture on it."

"It has."

"A tower. And a book, open."

"That's the office I used to belong to."

He thought about it. Soren thought about things visibly and without hurry and had never once in his life been embarrassed by a pause.

"Does it want something?"

I turned the letter over and looked at the seal, the plum-coloured wax, the deep clean press of it, a thing made by a machine in a room four days' road away.

"I don't know yet."

"Mm," said Soren, and handed me the lot, and took yesterday's cloth off the sill, and went away down the shelf to look at the storehouse mouth on his road home, which he did every morning that spring and which nobody had ever asked him to do.

I knew the seal before I had the letter out of the boy's hand, and I stood in my own doorway a moment with it, the way you stand a moment with a cold cup before you drink it. Cream paper, heavy, a clerk's iron hand. The tower and the open book, pressed deep in wax the colour of a dried plum. I had not had one under that seal in a long time, and there had been a stretch of my life when they had come thick, and I had folded that stretch up and put it away with some care, and here was the office's wax sitting warm in my palm on a bright spring morning, four days' road past the end of the railway, on a hill I had bought precisely because nothing with that seal was supposed to know the road to it.

I should say here what the office had ever wanted with me, because it was never the size of the talent. Talent is countable, and the office counts it, and there is nothing in the counting that has ever kept anybody awake. It was the notation. I had gone out of the practice having published a way of setting a working down on paper that was not the way anyone else sets one down, and the difference in it, for a man who read close enough to find the difference, was that mine carried marks for what a thing was already doing on its own before you ever laid a hand on it. There is no call for such a mark in a discipline where the world is the material and the working is the will. I had built a grammar with a listening in it, alone, over a good many years, out of no tradition I could have pointed to, and I had put it in the literature under my own name, and then I had walked out of the practice and left it lying there for anybody.

I read it standing. It was three sentences long, which for the office is a kind of shouting.

It informed me, with compliments, that the inquiry opened at the time of my withdrawal from the planewright's practice, it gave the year, as though I might have mislaid it, had been formally closed by determination of the standing committee, no further submission being required on my part. It thanked me for my correspondence in the matter of the lamp patents, which had been noted. And it closed, above a signature I did not know, in the way of a door left one deliberate inch ajar:

*The office remains interested.*

I read it twice more that day and did not answer it, there being nothing in it that asked to be answered, which was of course its craft. A letter that asks can be refused. A letter that merely stands in the corner of a man's morning, holding its hat, being interested, cannot even be shown the door. I put it in the desk, in the shallow drawer with the licences, where the paper of my old life was kept, and I will not pretend to you that I put it out of my mind, because a man does not put a thing like that out of his mind. He puts it where he can hear it breathing.

It was Ren, in the end, who put the shine back on that week, and he did it by walking away from me down a road with a paper in his coat.

The storehouse wanted stone, good squared block for the front wall and the facings, more than the waste heap owed anybody. That meant the quarry, and a proper order: measures, counts, grades, a price, a delivery, the whole small machinery of one yard talking to another. In the ordinary way of that valley, such an errand was Jorin's, or mine with Jorin's list in my hand.

"Send Ren," Jorin said.

He said it at the site, on the shelf, in the morning, without looking up from the peg he was driving, and for a moment I thought I had misheard him. The boy was eighteen. He had never in his life ordered so much as a hinge on his own word. Across the shelf I saw Ren's hands stop on the line he was holding, and then go on holding it, very carefully, like a man determined not to startle a bird that had landed on him.

"The whole order?" I said.

"Boy knows stone." Jorin struck the peg home. "Knows what the wall wants. Knows what a fair count looks like and what a short one looks like. Edran will try him on the grade, because Edran tries everyone once, and the boy will catch it, and after that Edran will know he catches it, and that will be worth more to him than the wall." He straightened up and looked at his son for the first time in the whole exchange, and delivered the entire commission in two words. "Boy's ready."

We wrote the order that evening at my table, the three of us, with the drawings weighted down and the rain gone off.

I say the three of us. What happened was that Jorin sat back with his arms folded and said nothing whatever for an hour, and Ren and I did the work, and every time the boy looked up at his father for a word his father looked back at him and did not give him one.

He wrote a fair hand, slower than mine and a good deal straighter. Squared block for the front wall, so many foot run, so many courses, to the dimensions on the sheet. Facings, dressed one face, counted separate. The lintel specified by itself with its bearing lengths written out, because a lintel is not a quantity, it is an object, and you order it the way you order a horse.

"Delivery to the shelf," Ren said, writing it. "Not to the yard foot. It's a mile of bad ground and I'll not have it tipped at the bottom and carried up by us."

"Put a day on it," I said.

"I've put a week on it. He'll be a week late whatever I put." He looked up. "Da. What should it come to?"

"Four gold the lot," said Jorin, to the ceiling. "Delivered."

"And if he says five?"

"Then he says five."

That was the whole of the coaching the boy got, and he took it without complaint, and folded the order in three and put it inside his coat and buttoned the coat over it. Then he unbuttoned the coat and took the order out and read it through once more and put it back. He did that twice again before he went home. I have done the same with worse papers.

At the door he stopped. "The grade," he said. "Firsts for the facings. It says firsts."

"It says firsts," I agreed.

"Right," said Ren, in his father's voice exactly, and neither of them heard it, and I had to turn round and see to the fire.

Ren went the next morning, up the quarry road alone with the order in his coat and Snow lent under him for the dignity of the thing, sitting his borrowed horse the way you sit an examination.

Jorin and I set the building out that day and neither of us mentioned him once.

It is good work, setting out, and it takes a great deal longer than anybody watching believes. We cut the hazel back from the mouth and burned it wet, which is its own small misery. We ran the front line first, a cord between two pegs, and stood off from it, and moved it four inches, and stood off from it again. We squared off that line with the long rod and a three-four-five, checked the diagonals twice, and drove the corner pegs. Jorin drove his the way he drove everything, three strikes and done. Mine wanted five, and one of them wanted pulling and doing over.

Then we cut the footing trench across the front, two spits down through the shelf's black soil to the yellow underneath, and found the bedrock coming up handsomely at the west end and dropping away at the east, which meant a step in the footing and half a day that nobody had costed.

"Step it," said Jorin.

"Step it."

That was our entire conversation on the subject and it stood in for about a page of correspondence.

I straightened up in the middle of the afternoon with my hands in the small of my back, and the shelf was green and steaming and the beck was loud below, and I looked away down to where the quarry road comes over the far shoulder. Jorin, forty feet off and facing entirely the other way, said, "He'll not be back before dark."

We had the trench out by five, the pegs in and the strings up, and the whole front of the building stood there in cord and air with the hill's own room waiting behind it, and the light went long and yellow across the shelf, and there was a horse coming up the way. He came back at dusk with the order placed, the price a half-silver under what Jorin had told me to expect, and a new way of coming through a gate, and he made his report to the two of us at the site wall in the falling light, grade and count and delivery day, level as a mason's line, and neither of us smiled until he had gone down the way home, and then neither of us said anything either, because there was nothing to say that the evening had not already said.

The Edran business he gave last, folded in after the delivery day, as though it were part of the arithmetic. Edran had walked him down the yard past the good stacks to a stack at the back, and told him it was off the same bed as the facings, cut the same week, and that he could have it at the seconds price and do his master a favour.

"And?" I said.

"It's off the top course. It's been out in the weather since his father's day. Half of it's face-bedded and the other half will go that way in ten years." He said it the way a man says the day of the week. "So I asked to see the bed it came off, and he took me and showed me, and after that he was very pleasant."

Jorin, beside me, looked at the string lines and said nothing at all.

Jorin left last, as he always did, with the drawings under his arm. At the gap in the wall he stopped, and stood a moment, his back half to me, in the blue end of the light.

"He felt something in that cellar," Jorin said. "During the build. Hasn't said what."

And he went on down the way, and left it standing in the dusk behind him, exactly where he had set it, the way he set everything: square, level, and bearing more weight than it showed.
