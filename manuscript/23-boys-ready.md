# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Three: Boy's Ready

Jorin came up the way on the second evening of those four days, with a roll of my drawings under his arm and the particular walk of a man who has read them and has opinions.

He had come up in the rain and had not hurried in it. From my window I watched him take the last thirty flags at the pace he takes dry ones, hat brim shedding, my four sheets rolled and buttoned inside his coat where the wet could not get at them. That told me his opinion of the drawings before he was over the threshold with them.

I had drawn the storehouse over the winter, in the evenings the glasshouse left me, which were few and late. It wanted building on the east shelf, into the slope, where the ground had already done half the work: the cave that Ren had found on harvest night sat back in that deeper green like a room the hill had roughed out and left for a tenant, dry, stone-floored, cool the year round. My drawings took it as they found it. Face the mouth, floor it proper, wall the front, roof what stood proud of the slope, and let the hill itself be the cold store's back and sides, the way a good root cellar is mostly borrowed ground with a door on it. The inner chamber I had drawn deeper, smaller, and I had specified its rear wall with a care that stood out of the page a little from the plainness of the rest: set true, dressed both faces, to a line I had drawn with more care than any store wall wants.

I had gone up to that cave a dozen times over the winter before I put a line on paper, in weather that made the walk a decision, and stood inside it holding a lantern, with my head down under the low end, and let my eyes come round to the dark. A cave will tell you nothing in the first minute. It wants five. In the fifth minute you notice that the floor is dry in February, which no floor in that valley is; that the cold in it is a steady cold and not a draught; and that when you speak, the room gives you back a small close answer with no ring in it at all. Those three facts are the whole argument for a cold store, and the hill had made all three of them long before there was anybody in the valley to make them to.

I had drawn it on the good board with my father's instruments, which is a sentence I could not have written two years before without putting the pen down. There were four sheets. A plan at a quarter inch to the foot; a long section through the mouth and both chambers; an elevation of the front wall with the door in it; and a sheet of details at half size, the lintel, the jamb, the rebate the door would shut into, the drain.

I am no draughtsman and have never claimed to be one, but I was taught to draw by a man who was, at a table I was not tall enough for, and some of it took. His instruments had come out of their case in the autumn and stayed out on the shelf all winter, and that was a new arrangement in my house. The dividers with the stiff joint that wants a thumb and a certain amount of language. The ruling pen you fill with a brush and never dip. The small ivory rule gone the colour of weak tea.

Drawing a building is an argument with yourself in which you are obliged to write down both sides. On the ground you can say the door goes about there. At a quarter inch to the foot you must say where about there is, and the paper will not let you leave, and it cannot be hurried, and it has no opinion of you whatever. I drew the plan three times. The first was the cave as I wished it were. The second was the cave as it is, and it made a worse building. The third was those two having it out on one sheet, and that is what a drawing is for, and it is the one Jorin had rolled inside his coat.

The rest of the specification was as dull as good building always is on paper. Front wall two feet thick in squared block, laid to courses, with a batter of an inch in the foot to throw the weather off it. The mouth faced and squared back to sound stone, no further than wanted. Floor of flags on nine inches of clean grit, laid to a fall toward the mouth, so that anything wet in that building would leave it without being asked twice. A stone lintel and not oak, because oak in a cold store is a slow argument that you lose. Slate over the part that stood proud of the slope and turf over the slate, so that from twenty paces up the shelf you would see a bank with a door in it. Vents in the front wall at the top, hooded, with a slide on each, because a cold store that cannot breathe is a cellar full of mould and one that breathes too freely is a cellar full of March.

The drain I redrew more times than anything else on the sheet, and nobody will ever look at it. A cold store makes its own water: what comes off the flags in a wet month, what stands on the face of the wall in a thaw, what a barrel weeps in August. All of it has to go somewhere that is not the floor, and the somewhere has to still be a somewhere in thirty years, when whoever has the building has long since stopped wondering why there is a channel cut along the foot of the front wall. So: a gutter in the flags inside the door, a stone-lined run under the threshold, every joint of it laid dry and dressed, because a drain you have to open is a drain you will not open.

Turf over slate wants saying properly too, because it looks like thrift and is not. Slate alone on a roof that meets a bank leaks where it meets, always, along the one line nobody can get at. Turf laid over the slate carries the bank across the join and goes on being bank. It grows, it knits, it is mended by anybody with a spade and an afternoon, and in five summers nobody will be able to tell you where the hill stops.

And behind all of that, past the outer chamber where the barrels would stand, through the low way into the deeper room, one wall. Nine feet by seven. Dressed both faces, set to the line, jointed close.

Jorin spread the sheets on my table under the lumen and went through them the way he went through everything, in order, twice, saying nothing the first time. The rain had followed him up and was busy on the window. I poured what I had and let him read.

Clearing the table for him took longer than it had any right to. There were seed lists on it, and Brenna's spring order, and two of my own notebooks, and a plate I should have carried down at breakfast. I moved the lot to the bench under the north sill and wiped the boards with my sleeve and set the four sheets out in their numbered order, and weighted them with whatever was to hand: the tea tin, a whetstone, the flat of a chisel, and my own left palm until I thought of the inkstand.

What I had was the end of a bottle Penworth had sent up at midwinter, a heel of yesterday's loaf, and some of Brenna's hard cheese. Jorin took the bread and the cheese and left the bottle alone until the first pass was done, which was how he drank.

I had meant to have something better in the house for a night like that and had not managed it. The bottle had come up at midwinter packed in straw in a crate of other things, and I had been keeping it for an occasion and had failed to have one. Brenna's hard cheese cuts like roofing slate and tastes like being paid. The loaf was Soren's from the morning before, and a day-old loaf out of that oven is better bread than most places manage warm.

He read standing. His coat steamed on the back of the chair and the room filled slowly with the smell of wet wool and warm paper. He went sheet by sheet in the order I had numbered them, which not one man in ten does either, and he touched them only at the corners. Twice he laid his thumbnail flat on the paper and slid it along a line, which is a builder measuring in the one unit he entirely trusts. Once he grunted. It was not a bad grunt.

He read the plan as a man reads a field he is thinking of taking, and the section as he reads a letter with money in it. His lips did not move. Nothing about him moved except the thumb and the eyes, and once, at the long section, his chin came up perhaps an inch and went back down, and I would have paid to know which line did that.

I have watched men be handed drawings. Most of them go to the elevation, because the elevation is the picture, and say something admiring about the door. Jorin looked at the elevation for as long as it takes to count three and turned back to the section, and the section is where a building is either possible or it is not.

The lumen sat at the corner of the table giving out its steady amber, and outside the rain thickened and settled into the sound it makes when it has decided to go on all night, and the second evening of those four days went quietly along, and I sat on my hands and let a man read.

I had expected to find that hard to sit through. I did not. There is a particular quiet that comes into a room when somebody is reading your work properly, and it is not an anxious quiet at all; it is nearer to being carried. I have felt it three or four times in my life and it cannot be arranged for.

Then he went back to the first sheet and did the whole of it again.

"Root cellar," he said at last. "Grown up and gone respectable."

"That's about it."

"It's what I'd have drawn." He said it flat, which from Jorin was a diploma with a seal on it.

Then he went at the work, which was the real reading, and it took the better part of an hour.

Most of an hour of anything is not worth setting on paper. What follows are the parts where the building changed. Anybody who has never sat through such an hour supposes it must be tedious, and it is the opposite of tedious: it is the hour in which a thing on paper is going to stand up out of the ground or is not going to, and every dull sentence in it carries load.

"Vents."

"Two, hooded, with slides."

"Make it three." His finger came down on the front wall. "It's a long room and the far end's the cold end. Two will damp at the back by the second summer and you'll lose a season of apples finding out why."

I made the note on the sheet in pencil. Three vents. I have that sheet still and the pencil is still under the ink.

"Fall on the floor."

"One in eighty."

"One in sixty. It's a store, not a parlour. Nobody will ever notice it, and you'll want it the year the beck comes up."

"One in sixty."

"Grit."

"Nine inches. Clean."

"Where from."

"The beck."

"Off the beck. Not out of it." He had not looked up. "Out of the bed it carries fines, and fines travel, and in six years your fall is in the grit and not in the flags. Take it off the bank and heap it and let the rain have a fortnight at it."

I made that note too, and it is on the sheet in the same pencil, and it is the reason the floor of that building has never once stood water.

"Door."

"Oak, two leaf, with a wicket in the one."

"One leaf." He said it without heat. "Two leaf on a north face in this valley is two chances for the wet and one of them will take it. Make the single leaf wide enough for a barrel on a barrow and you'll never once want the other."

"Tomas's hinges."

"Tomas's hinges," he agreed, and that was the only thing all evening he did not argue with.

"Batter."

"An inch in the foot. On the face."

"And your jamb?"

I had not thought about the jamb. A wall that leans back an inch in every foot of its height, and a door that hangs plumb inside it, are two different intentions meeting in the same three feet of stonework, and one of them has to give way to the other in a manner somebody has decided in advance.

"Plumb," I said, when I had thought about it. "Reveal squared out of the batter, and the batter picked up again past the opening."

"More cutting."

"More cutting."

He nodded once at the sheet and made no note, because he never made notes. I have wondered since where that man kept a building between one day and the next, and the answer, so far as I could ever tell, was that he kept it in his hands.

We went round the lintel twice. I had drawn a single stone across the mouth, five foot six of it, and Jorin did not like the bearing at the east end where the natural rock ran out into a seam. He was right. We settled it by carrying the front wall in a foot on that side and losing an inch of doorway, which I minded and which mattered not at all.

"Turf."

"Over the slate. Lifted off the shelf in squares before ever we dig, stacked grass to grass, and put back."

"Put back in the autumn." He turned the detail sheet round to look at the roof edge the right way up. "Turf laid in a dry June dies on you. Then you've a brown roof for a year and every man in the valley with an opinion about it."

"Drain."

"Out under the threshold and away down the shelf."

"Not into the hazel." His finger went off the edge of the sheet and kept travelling, which builders do and draughtsmen hate. "Roots find a stone run inside four years and take it apart for the water in it. Carry it past them and let it out where you can stand and watch it come out."

Then he said, "When."

"Now. It's built by harvest or it's built next year, and I would rather have the apples in it."

"Six weeks. Four men after the second week." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, which in him was a man doing sums. "Wall goes up in a fortnight if the stone comes when it says it will. Stone never comes when it says it will."

Four men is a thing a builder says lightly and a client hears in metal. I said nothing, because I had nothing useful to say: the glasshouse had begun to sell a little, and every copper of the little was spoken for before it came, and half of that man's own winter wages stood deferred on his own books against exactly the money the glasshouse had not yet made. He put four men in the sum without a flicker and went on to the stone.

"And the crew?"

"They'll be glad of it. It's dry work under a hill in a wet summer, and there's not a man of mine would say no to that twice." He set his cup down, square, and squared it again. "One question, then."

His finger came down on the rear wall of the inner chamber, on the careful figures of its dressing.

"That," he said. "Dressed both faces, and set truer than a church. A store wall wants one face and no opinions. What's it for?"

I had known he would find it, and I had decided, some weeks before, what I would say when he did, and what I would not.

"For later," I said. "A wall dressed proper now is cheaper than a regret in ten years. Call it a habit of my other trade. We build for the drawing we haven't made yet."

Jorin looked at the sheet a moment longer. On the sheet, as on the ground, the far side of that wall was the inside of the hill and nothing else, forty feet of dark stone to anywhere.

Jorin looked at me a while. I have said he looked at walls to see whether they were plumb, and I will tell you it is a different thing entirely to stand on the receiving end of it, and that I did not enjoy it, and that I held still under it the way you hold still for a doctor.

"Right," he said, and rolled the sheet, and that was the whole of his inquiry, then and ever. I have thought since that of all the things that man built on my hill, the one I owed him most for was everything he chose not to ask.

The letter had come up that morning, with the bread.

Soren brought it. He came up the way at the run he had used ever since the flags went down, and I heard him on the last twenty of them and had the door open before he got to it, which pleased neither of us as much as pretending I had not would have done.

He was the only person in that valley who ran up my way, and I do not believe he had once walked it. Stone carries sound. You can hear a boy on it from the elbow up: a light quick clatter, a hitch in it where the seep runs under and the stone goes dark, then twenty flags of nothing while he takes the steep bit at a walk because somebody has told him to, then the clatter again.

He had the bread under one arm in its cloth and the hill's post held flat and square in the other hand, the way somebody had taught him, probably Lira. It was a bright morning, the first proper one that week, the shelf steaming where the sun had got at it and the glasshouse throwing its long light down the slope. The bread came up in the same cloth every morning and went back down in yesterday's, and at that hour it was still warm through the weave. I had got into the habit of taking it in both hands, with more ceremony than a loaf wants, because by then it was not entirely a loaf.

The boy stood on my doorstone and did not hand the post over, because he was looking at the top letter.

"That one's got a picture on it."

"It has."

"A tower. And a book, open."

"That's the office I used to belong to."

He thought about it. Soren thought about things visibly and without hurry and had never once in his life been embarrassed by a pause.

He did it standing perfectly still, with his eyes on the wax and not on my face. I have sat in rooms full of grown men who could not have managed either half of that.

"Does it want something?"

I turned the letter over and looked at the seal, the plum-coloured wax, the deep clean press of it, a thing made by a machine in a room four days' road away.

"I don't know yet."

"Mm," said Soren, and handed me the lot, and took yesterday's cloth off the sill, and went away down the shelf to look at the storehouse mouth on his road home, which he did every morning that spring and which nobody had ever asked him to do.

He had a route and he kept to it: down the shelf, along the front of the mouth, in as far as the light went and not a step past it, out again, and away down the flags with his hands in his pockets.

I knew the seal before I had the letter out of the boy's hand, and I stood in my own doorway a moment with it, the way you stand a moment with a cold cup before you drink it. Cream paper, heavy, a clerk's iron hand. The tower and the open book, pressed deep in wax the colour of a dried plum. I had not had one under that seal in a long time, and there had been a stretch of my life when they had come thick, and I had folded that stretch up and put it away with some care, and here was the office's wax sitting warm in my palm on a bright spring morning, four days' road past the end of the railway, on a hill I had bought precisely because nothing with that seal was supposed to know the road to it.

I did not open it on the doorstone. I took it in and set it on the table and put the bread beside it and went and did two ordinary things first, which I would recommend to anybody. I filled the kettle. I turned the three pots on the north sill so their good sides faced the light, as I do every morning of my life. Then I came back and looked at it lying there on my own boards, cream and heavy among the seed lists, with that wax on it like a thumbprint pressed into a valley four days' road from anywhere it had business being.

I should say here what the office had ever wanted with me, because it was never the size of the talent. Talent is countable, and the office counts it, and there is nothing in the counting that has ever kept anybody awake. It was the notation. I had gone out of the practice having published a way of setting a working down on paper that was not the way anyone else sets one down, and the difference in it, for a man who read close enough to find the difference, was that mine carried marks for what a thing was already doing on its own before you ever laid a hand on it. There is no call for such a mark in a discipline where the world is the material and the working is the will. I had built a grammar with a listening in it, alone, over a good many years, out of no tradition I could have pointed to, and I had put it in the literature under my own name, and then I had walked out of the practice and left it lying there for anybody.

The knife was in my hand before I had decided to use it. Good wax breaks clean along the press, and that wax was very good, and the seal came away in two pieces and I caught them both and set them down on the boards beside the paper without knowing why I was being careful with them, and there they stayed a week.

I read it standing. It was three sentences long, which for the office is a kind of shouting.

It informed me, with compliments, that the inquiry opened at the time of my withdrawal from the planewright's practice, it gave the year, as though I might have mislaid it, had been formally closed by determination of the standing committee, no further submission being required on my part. It thanked me for my correspondence in the matter of the lamp patents, which had been noted. And it closed, above a signature I did not know, in the way of a door left one deliberate inch ajar:

*The office remains interested.*

I read it twice more that day and did not answer it, there being nothing in it that asked to be answered, which was of course its craft. A letter that asks can be refused. A letter that merely stands in the corner of a man's morning, holding its hat, being interested, cannot even be shown the door. I put it in the desk, in the shallow drawer with the licences, where the paper of my old life was kept, and I will not pretend to you that I put it out of my mind, because a man does not put a thing like that out of his mind. He puts it where he can hear it breathing.

I laid it face down under the licences and shut the drawer on it, and went out and did the day's work, and the day's work that day was the glasshouse, and a glasshouse in the spring is all stooping, and stooping is a mercy.

It was Ren, in the end, who put the shine back on that week, and he did it by walking away from me down a road with a paper in his coat.

The storehouse wanted stone, good squared block for the front wall and the facings, more than the waste heap owed anybody. That meant the quarry, and a proper order: measures, counts, grades, a price, a delivery, the whole small machinery of one yard talking to another. In the ordinary way of that valley, such an errand was Jorin's, or mine with Jorin's list in my hand.

"Send Ren," Jorin said.

He said it at the site, on the shelf, in the morning, without looking up from the peg he was driving, and for a moment I thought I had misheard him. The boy was eighteen. He had never in his life ordered so much as a hinge on his own word. Across the shelf I saw Ren's hands stop on the line he was holding, and then go on holding it, very carefully, like a man determined not to startle a bird that had landed on him.

"The whole order?" I said.

"Boy knows stone." Jorin struck the peg home. "Knows what the wall wants. Knows what a fair count looks like and what a short one looks like. Edran will try him on the grade, because Edran tries everyone once, and the boy will catch it, and after that Edran will know he catches it, and that will be worth more to him than the wall." He straightened up and looked at his son for the first time in the whole exchange, and delivered the entire commission in two words. "Boy's ready."

Nobody said anything after that. Down the shelf the line went on being held, dead straight, by a pair of hands that had plenty else to occupy them. Jorin bent and took up the next peg. The morning went on, and that was the ceremony.

We wrote the order that evening at my table, the three of us, with the drawings weighted down and the rain gone off.

I say the three of us. What happened was that Jorin sat back with his arms folded and said nothing whatever for an hour, and Ren and I did the work, and every time the boy looked up at his father for a word his father looked back at him and did not give him one.

He had turned his chair to the end of the table, where he could see the pair of us and take part in nothing, and his cup stood untouched at his elbow from the first minute of it to the last. If I live to be very old I do not expect to watch harder work done by anybody sitting still.

He wrote a fair hand, slower than mine and a good deal straighter. Squared block for the front wall, so many foot run, so many courses, to the dimensions on the sheet. Facings, dressed one face, counted separate. The lintel specified by itself with its bearing lengths written out, because a lintel is not a quantity, it is an object, and you order it the way you order a horse.

He set it down as he had been taught to set down a cutting list, one thing to a line, the counts to the left where a yard man's thumb travels. He spelled two words wrong and neither of them was a stone word. He wrote the delivery place twice, once in the body and once again at the foot, having seen his father's orders come back short and knowing where the argument happens.

Twice he stopped and laid the pen down and read the whole thing back from the top, his lips moving at the numbers. Twice he changed nothing.

"Delivery to the shelf," Ren said, writing it. "Not to the yard foot. It's a mile of bad ground and I'll not have it tipped at the bottom and carried up by us."

"Put a day on it," I said.

"I've put a week on it. He'll be a week late whatever I put." He looked up. "Da. What should it come to?"

"Four gold the lot," said Jorin, to the ceiling. "Delivered."

"And if he says five?"

"Then he says five."

That was the whole of the coaching the boy got, and he took it without complaint, and folded the order in three and put it inside his coat and buttoned the coat over it. Then he unbuttoned the coat and took the order out and read it through once more and put it back. He did that twice again before he went home. I have done the same with worse papers.

His mother had put a supper into him before he came up, and he had eaten none of it, and I knew that because he told me so twice, in the manner of a man explaining why he is not hungry to somebody who has not offered him anything.

At the door he stopped. "The grade," he said. "Firsts for the facings. It says firsts."

"It says firsts," I agreed.

"Right," said Ren, in his father's voice exactly, and neither of them heard it, and I had to turn round and see to the fire.

Ren went the next morning, up the quarry road alone with the order in his coat and Snow lent under him for the dignity of the thing, sitting his borrowed horse the way you sit an examination.

Snow behaved beautifully, which I had not been able to promise anybody. She stood square while he found his stirrup and went off down the way with her head up, and I watched the two of them out of sight round the shoulder and then went and found something to do with my hands.

Jorin and I set the building out that day and neither of us mentioned him once.

It was a fine morning after the rain, the sort that comes in off the west in that country with everything washed and nothing dry, and we were on the shelf by seven with the grass wetting us to the knee inside the first ten minutes.

It is good work, setting out, and it takes a great deal longer than anybody watching believes. We cut the hazel back from the mouth and burned it wet, which is its own small misery.

Green hazel in leaf does not want to burn and must be talked into it. You get a fire going in the heart of the heap out of dry stuff carried up on purpose, and then you feed it green, and it sulks, and it steams, and it sends a white column straight up in still air that can be read from the street, and somewhere past noon it stops arguing and burns hot and clean and you cannot come within four feet of it. Jorin fed that heap all morning and never once discussed it with me. I have noticed since that a builder will let his client dig all day and will not let him mind the fire.

We cut the stools back to the crown and not to the ground, because hazel cut properly comes again and is hurdles in seven years, and there was no call to kill a thing whose only fault was standing in front of a door nobody had wanted until now.

We ran the front line first, a cord between two pegs, and stood off from it, and moved it four inches, and stood off from it again.

Four inches. On the sheet that is the thickness of the line I drew it with. On the ground it was the difference between a doorway you come at square off the shelf with a loaded barrow, and a doorway you have to remember to turn for, every time, for as long as the building stands. It took the two of us most of an hour and a dozen words between us. Jorin's method was to walk away: set the cord, go forty feet off, stand with his hands behind his back, come back, move it, go away again. Mine, left to itself, was to lie down on the wet grass and sight along it, and he let me do that without remark, and then went away forty feet and looked at it from there and moved it another inch, and was right.

We squared off that line with the long rod and a three-four-five, checked the diagonals twice, and drove the corner pegs.

The three-four-five is older than anybody's grandfather and it is the only piece of geometry I have ever watched a working man use without ever hearing it given a name, and the name does not signify, because he uses it correctly and he uses it twice. Jorin ran the knotted cord and made me read the knots off, and when I had read them he said the number back to me in his own mouth, and then we did it all again the other way about. Twice is not caution. Twice is the trade.

Jorin drove his the way he drove everything, three strikes and done. Mine wanted five, and one of them wanted pulling and doing over.

You can learn a man from his hammering. Jorin held the peg with two fingers at the very top and hit it as though he were putting it somewhere he had already seen it standing. I held mine in my whole fist. That is fear. I hit it a shade off the true, and that is fear as well, and it went in leaning, and it had to come out. He watched me pull it and said nothing, and watched me set it again, and when it went in on four he moved along to the next one. That was the whole of his commentary and it was enough.

Then we cut the footing trench across the front, two spits down through the shelf's black soil to the yellow underneath, and found the bedrock coming up handsomely at the west end and dropping away at the east, which meant a step in the footing and half a day that nobody had costed.

The finding of it is a small event with a sound. You are digging in soil, and then the spade rings, and nobody needs to be a builder to know what the ring means, and every man who has ever heard it does the same three things in the same order: hits it again to be sure, scrapes it clear with the edge of the spade, and stands up.

We stood up. The rock came out of the yellow at the west end like the shoulder of something asleep, clean and grey and going on back into the bank, and there was no arguing with it and no wanting to. Rock under a footing is the best news a wall ever gets. It was the east end that cost us. Four feet along it fell away and went on falling, and where it went we had to follow, because you cannot found half a wall on stone and half on soil and expect the two halves to agree about the winter.

Jorin got down on his heels at the change, rapped the rock twice with the handle of his hammer, and looked along the trench to the east end and back.

"Step it," said Jorin.

"Step it."

That was our entire conversation on the subject and it stood in for about a page of correspondence.

The half day went as half days go when nobody has costed them. We took the trench down in three steps instead of one clean run, each tread squared and each riser cut into the rock where the rock would take it, and it is fiddling work with a bar and a chisel, done mostly on the knees, and by three in the afternoon my hands had stopped belonging to me.

Nobody costed it and nobody mentioned costing it. That is what six weeks means when a builder says it in the spring. It means six weeks with half a day of rock in it, and a wet fortnight, and a cart that comes on the day it did not say. He had put the rock into the number before he knew the rock was there. I have been given estimates in the city by men in very good coats who could not have done that with a month and a clerk to help them.

We did not mention the boy, and the not mentioning got harder as the day went along. Twice in the morning I had the shape of the sentence in my mouth and put it back. Once, about noon, Jorin stopped and looked at nothing whatever for the space of three breaths, in a direction that had no work in it, and then bent and went on driving. I looked at my own peg and said not a word. Two grown men on a hillside, one of them the boy's father, working all day round a hole in it the size of a cart.

I straightened up in the middle of the afternoon with my hands in the small of my back, and the shelf was green and steaming and the beck was loud below, and I looked away down to where the quarry road comes over the far shoulder. Jorin, forty feet off and facing entirely the other way, said, "He'll not be back before dark."

I did not answer it. There was nothing in it to answer. He had said it to the trench, with his back to me, in the voice a man keeps for the weather, and it had taken him since seven that morning to get it out.

We had the trench out by five, the pegs in and the strings up, and the whole front of the building stood there in cord and air with the hill's own room waiting behind it, and the light went long and yellow across the shelf, and there was a horse coming up the way. He came back at dusk with the order placed, the price a half-silver under what Jorin had told me to expect, and a new way of coming through a gate, and he made his report to the two of us at the site wall in the falling light, grade and count and delivery day, level as a mason's line, and neither of us smiled until he had gone down the way home, and then neither of us said anything either, because there was nothing to say that the evening had not already said.

What I could see of him in that light is the only part of that day I have any claim on. He came up the last of the way leading her, which he need not have done and did. He was wet to the thigh from the crossing and had not noticed it. He gave the report standing, with his cap in one hand and the order folded back inside his coat, and he gave it in the order things had happened and not in the order that would have flattered him, and he did not once use the word I.

The Edran business he gave last, folded in after the delivery day, as though it were part of the arithmetic. Edran had walked him down the yard past the good stacks to a stack at the back, and told him it was off the same bed as the facings, cut the same week, and that he could have it at the seconds price and do his master a favour.

"And?" I said.

"It's off the top course. It's been out in the weather since his father's day. Half of it's face-bedded and the other half will go that way in ten years." He said it the way a man says the day of the week. "So I asked to see the bed it came off, and he took me and showed me, and after that he was very pleasant."

There was no pleasure in it anywhere. He might have been telling us the road was soft in one place and to mind it with a cart.

Jorin, beside me, looked at the string lines and said nothing at all.

We put the tools in under the mouth out of the wet, and left the strings up, and the shelf went blue.

Jorin left last, as he always did, with the drawings under his arm. At the gap in the wall he stopped, and stood a moment, his back half to me, in the blue end of the light.

He stood there long enough for me to say good night and not do it.

"He felt something in that cellar," Jorin said. "During the build. Hasn't said what."

And he went on down the way, and left it standing in the dusk behind him, exactly where he had set it, the way he set everything: square, level, and bearing more weight than it showed.
