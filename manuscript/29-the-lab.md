# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Nine: The Lab

The cold held off after that, and gave us a last fair week of the autumn. The storehouse was finished in it, and it was Ren who hung the door.

That was not sentiment, or not only sentiment. It was the order of the work. His father had made the door, and his father hung doors last, when the walls had said their final word about what was square. The boy had earned the hanging of this one twice over, having set its wall, cut its floor, and ordered its stone on his own name. But every man on that shelf knew what else was being finished when the hinges took the weight and the latch went home. The boy finishes what he builds. He swung it twice before he was satisfied, and once more with his hand flat on it, feeling the swing rather than watching it.

"Wants a shim under the top strap," he said.

"Does it."

"Half a one." He was already reaching for it. "It'll drop a hair when the frame takes the winter."

"Then shim it."

He shimmed it, and swung it again, and it went to the latch and stopped there without being asked, the way a door does perhaps once in twenty. He stepped back from it with the screw-iron still in his hand and looked at his father. Jorin looked at the door and not at him.

"That's done, then."

The rest of that morning was the plain business of a building being let go of. The trestles came down at nine and the last of the pointing was done by ten, and after that two of the crew went along the front of the wall on their knees with a bucket and a brush, washing the block down, because a smear of mortar on a face is there for the life of the wall. The turf went back over the slate in the squares it had been lifted in before ever we dug, grass to grass, laid by two men walking backward down the roof of it. By noon the last square was down and beaten flat with the back of a spade, and one of them went along the joints afterward with his thumbs, working the edges together.

They got their tools out from under the mouth, where the tools had lived since the spring. There is a sound to that. Bag after bag going up onto a shoulder, the space under the mouth emptying, and the shelf becoming a shelf again.

Jorin went down last with the drawings under his arm, as he went down last off every job of his. The others took the way at the pace men take a hill they are finished with. I stood up there in the cold clean sun with my hands in my pockets for longer than there was any call for, and did not go inside, and could not at that moment have told you why not.

The building was earned the slow way, wall by wall. From without it was nothing: a low stone front set into the green shelf, turf-roofed where it stood proud of the slope, a door and two deep windows, the whole of it looking less built than uncovered, as though the shelf had always had a house in it and somebody had cleared the bracken back. Within, it was two rooms, and the hill made both of them. The outer room was the cold store, floored and shelved, half its walls the living stone, holding the harvest at the one cold all year the way only borrowed ground can. We filled it that same week: Wray barley in the bins, the glasshouse's winter stock in crates and clamps, cheeses from three farms on the slate shelves, Pardon's fleece on a peg. The smell of it with the door shut, apples and stone and clean straw, was one of the honest wealths of my life. The hay we left where it lay. It keeps as well in a dry loft as under a hill, and the cold store's shelves had better claims on them that week, so the barn kept the winter's fodder and the storehouse took everything that could be sold or eaten.

The carts began on the Wednesday and came up in ones and twos for the best part of a week. I had spent two autumns wondering where I was going to put things, and I went down to meet every one of them. Hal Wray brought the barley himself in three loads, having said in September that he would send it and having, in the manner of that family, come with it. He put his shoulder under the first sack, carried it in, set it down, and stood looking at the bins Ren had built deep and lidded along the inner wall, and then past them at the living stone behind, and then went out for another sack. It was the fifth trip before he spoke.

"It'll keep here."

"It will."

"Better than mine keeps." He squared the sack with his boot. "Mine's over a byre."

Tam came with him and said nothing whatever, and stacked, laying the sacks mouths one way and butting the courses like a man building a wall out of barley.

The glasshouse stock came across the slope on the handbarrow, load after load: crates of roots in dry sand, the winter greens lifted with the soil still on them, two clamps' worth of what would not crate, and the last of Tam's lettuces going down to the village that week rather than into any store, because a lettuce keeps nowhere.

The cheeses came up on the Friday, from three farms in one morning, by an arrangement I had not made and never did learn the working of. The men who brought them I did not know well. Each of them, coming in out of the sun with his arms full, did the same thing: stopped, and put a flat hand on the stone of the side wall, and left it there a moment. One asked what we had done to get it so cold. I said we had not done anything. He took that for modesty and went out for the rest of his load, and he was wrong, and I let him be wrong.

Pardon came last, on foot, with a fleece over his shoulder and no cart, having walked the length of the north side with it on his back rather than trouble a man for a lift. He would not have it hung by me. He went along the wall until he found the peg he approved of, out of the drip and in the moving air, and hung it himself, and turned it once on the peg the way a man turns a coat on a hook.

"That'll do a twelvemonth there," he said. "Air moves in that corner."

"I'd not have known which peg."

"No," said Ned Pardon, and went out into the sun.

On the last evening of it I came down after supper with a lamp and shut the door behind me and stood in there a while by myself, doing no work and thinking nothing worth having. Every farmer in that valley had known a full store since he was old enough to carry. It took me better than forty years and a hill. I sat down on an upturned crate in the middle of my own cold store with the lamp on the floor beside me, like a man who has been handed something he cannot pay for.

At the back of the cold store, behind a second door of Tomas's making, was the inner chamber. The cave, as was; Ren's badger hole; the dry pocket the hill had kept under the bracken since before the village. We had faced it, floored it, set the long bench and the instrument shelves along its sides, and hung a Series lamp over the bench. At the back of it stood the rear wall, dressed both faces, set true to its bearing to the minute, the plainest piece of stonework on the hill and the only one built to a purpose I had declined to explain. The hill holds that wall the way a man holds a promise. I noticed it the first hour I worked in that room, and every hour after. Everywhere else on that hill the ground bears my buildings the way ground bears buildings. That wall it holds.

The lab was where my trade lived from then on. The Study kept the books and the desk and the long thinking. The working itself, the blanks and the bindings and the slow patient making, came down to the hill-cut room that autumn and never went back up, because the chamber was what a working room spends its whole life trying to be, still and dry and even-tempered and quiet in the deep way. The hum was closer down there than anywhere on the hill save the cellar itself. Not louder. Nearer, the way a voice is nearer through a wall than across a field.

The moving down of it took one afternoon.

We did it in trips, Ren and I, between the Study door and the shelf, with the wind coming up cold off the bottoms and the glasshouse throwing its long light down the slope beside us. The blanks went first, and they went in two hands with nothing else in them, the case shut, each stone in its own felt pocket so they could not mark one another. Then the wire, the fine spools of it on their drums; the waxed silk in its tin; the yellow wax; the small grey lump off my own crown that had gone everywhere I ever went. Then the glass, in straw, slowly. Then the housings out of Tomas's twenty in their box, brass on brass, making the noise a box of brass makes.

Ren carried the heavy end of everything without being asked, and put nothing down anywhere I had not shown him it went. About the fourth trip he stopped in the doorway with the bench-vice in his arms and looked at the emptying side of the room and asked the question he had been holding since the first trip.

"Are you keeping anything up here?"

"The books. The desk. The lamp."

"Not the lamp."

"The other one," I said. "The old one. It stays where it is."

He thought about that all the way down and did not ask why.

By dark the Study bench had more bare wood showing on it than it had shown since I built the room, the rings of a hundred crocks up out of the clutter at last and one lamp burning at the end of it. I stood in the door of the first thing I ever put up on that hill and understood that I had just demoted it.

The articles were signed on the equinox, at my table, Penworth's paper having come up the road with everything in it a suspicious man could want. Two copies, engrossed on paper stiff as a gate, in a clerk's hand with the capitals done properly and a foot of white left at the bottom of each for names. I read it twice at my own bench the night it came up, once for what it said and once for what it did not. But the paper was the least of it, and everybody concerned understood that the real binding would be done the old way, in the old form, which my own master had done for me in another life and which I had never once expected to stand on the other side of.

The old form is small and it is domestic. No hall, no gowns, no Latin of the trade. The master's own table, cleared. A loaf the prentice's mother baked. Salt the master provides. The article, read aloud, every word, so that no one can ever say the boy signed what he had not heard. And bread broken between the two of them, one loaf in two hands, each to keep his half against the term's end, because a term of years is a long road, and there are days on it when a man wants proof in a drawer that the beginning was real.

We did it in the lab, on the long bench cleared to the wood, on an evening with the first real frost making up its mind outside. Jorin stood by the door with his hat off. Ren's mother had baked the loaf and sent it up wrapped in the cloth she meant us to keep, and had not come, in the way of women of that country, who will build the whole of a ceremony and then decline to watch it. And Soren stood witness.

The form wants the witness asked out loud, in front of the parties, and I asked him.

"Soren. Will you stand witness to this, and say what you saw if you are ever asked?"

"Yes."

"You're not kin to him and not sworn to me?"

"No," he said, and thought about it, and added, "not yet."

Ren laughed once.

That was Ren's asking, not mine. The form wants a witness who is neither kin to the prentice nor sworn to the master, and there was exactly one soul on that hill who fitted, and Ren had asked him at the glasshouse door with two words, and Soren had answered with one.

So a boy of twelve stood at the end of my bench through the whole of the reading, his back straight as a plumb-line and his face as grave as the clerk's book, listening to the terms of another boy's future with an attention that went past manners into arithmetic. I read the articles aloud, five years of them, duties and keepings and the three conditions written in where Penworth had, to his credit, made them sound like law. All that time Soren stood reckoning, plain as figures on a slate: the term's years counted against his own, the door this opened measured for width, the distance from witness to the end of that bench taken and taken again. He caught me seeing it and did not look away, and I kept my face as grave as his, because a boy's arithmetic is his own. Mine at his age had been no different. Both of us knew the sum he was working, and neither of us said it, and it was not the night for it.

Something of what I read is worth having beside the room it was read in, because the paper was the only part of that evening a stranger could have looked at.

It began with the term: five years from the equinox day, and the day written out in words as well as in figures, so that nobody could improve it later with a pen. Then what I undertook, and my side ran longer than his. To instruct him in my craft so far as the same might be taught, and in the ordering and keeping of a working room, and in the care of what is kept in one. To feed, clothe and lodge him. Not to set him to work that was not of the craft, save such household work as any house asks of anybody living in it, and that clause is Penworth making it impossible for me to turn a prentice into a servant, knowing perfectly well that I never would.

Then the boy's side. To serve diligently. Not to absent himself. Not to carry the workings of the room out of the room. To keep what he was told to keep, and to say so when he had not understood, a covenant that stands in no articles anywhere in the Commonwealth, and that I had asked for, and that Penworth had put into a form no court could quarrel with.

Then the three conditions, in the order they had been given me across a kitchen table, and in a lawyer's English that had not spoiled one of them. That the apprentice shall finish what he builds. That the apprentice shall not be taken out of wood and stone. That the choosing shall be his own, and no man's else.

I read every word, including the words that stand in every such paper ever drawn and mean nothing to anybody, because the form does not permit skipping, and because the boy had a right to hear the whole of it. It took a quarter of an hour. Ren stood at the end of the bench and did not shift his feet once, and his father stood by the door with his hat in his two hands, and the lamp over the bench did not flicker, lamps of that kind not flickering, and outside the cold came down the shelf and laid its hand on the door.

There was a pen on the bench and an ink bottle I had carried down with the rest of it that same week, and I had thought beforehand about the order of it and settled it in the boy's favour, on the ground that a master who signs first is a master showing a prentice what has already been decided for him. I turned the sheet round and laid the pen across it and stepped back to where I could not watch it being done.

Ren signed. I signed. Jorin made his mark as father, pressing hard enough to be read from the far side of the paper.

He looked at it a moment when it was done, at the two names and his own mark under them, and set the pen across the neck of the bottle, and went back to the doorpost. I blotted the sheet and did the second copy and blotted that. The largest paper in that family's history took less than a minute, and was witnessed by a boy of twelve, and through the inner door the cold store lay dark and full behind us.

"The salt's yours to keep," I said, and set the dish in front of him. "Not to use."

"How long do I keep it?"

"Till the term's out. Then as long as you like."

Jorin spoke from the door for the first time that evening. "Loaf's my wife's," he said. "She'll want the cloth back."

"She'll have it."

"She won't ask for it." He looked at the wrapped bread and not at his son. "Ask her yourself, at the term's end, and she'll tell you she never sent one."

We broke the loaf, the boy's two hands and mine on it, and it came apart the way good bread does, unwilling and then all at once. I gave him the salt to keep and took my half of the bread. Jorin put his hat back on.

And then I set my apprentice his first working.

I had it ready at the bench's end: a lumen blank of the winter's run, sound but unbound, and one of Tomas's housings from the new twenty, brass collar and foot, the seam fine as a hair. Ren knew the theory cold. He had watched me bind a dozen lamps by then, and theory was never where that boy's difficulty lay. I talked him through the approach and stood back where a master stands, near enough to catch it, far enough to let it be his.

He did what every strong prentice does the first time, which is the thing I had done, in a room a week of roads away, half a lifetime ago. He pushed. He took hold of the light the way he would take hold of a beam, honest and able, and he meant to put it where it belonged. The blank went bright and crooked and wrong-colored, glare with a flinch in it, and the housing sang one thin unhappy note. His jaw set. He bore down. It got worse. It always gets worse.

"Ren," I said. "Stop pushing."

He stopped. The glare died back to a sullen flicker. He stood breathing over it, hands flat on the bench, a boy who had never yet met the job that did not yield to doing it harder, meeting it.

"It went crooked," he said.

"It went where you put it. That's the trouble."

"I had it square."

"You had it clamped."

"You're commanding it," I said. "You learned that from stone, and it has served you well, and with stone it is honest, because stone wants holding. This is not stone."

I came a half-step nearer. What I said to him then was not mine. I had it off the margin of an old book, on a rainy night, when I had nothing else.

"You do not tell a light what to be. You ask it for passage. Ask what it wants. Then get out of the way earlier than you think you ought."

He was quiet a moment. Then he took his hands off the bench, and rolled his shoulders once, his father's own movement before a lift, and bent to the blank again, and I watched him lay his will down, which is the hardest thing my trade asks of the strong ones, and ask.

The light came up in the glass the way morning comes up in a window. No glare in it. No flinch. It found the amber it wanted and settled into the collar and burned, steady as the one on Brenna's sill, steady as the one in the inn's window. It was the first light of my apprentice's making, in the first hour of his term.

Ren looked at it a long time. Then he looked at his hands.

"It wasn't me holding it," he said, working it through. "It's holding itself. I only... " He hunted for it and found it, eighteen years old, in one word. "Asked."

"That's the trade," I said. "The rest is penmanship."

"It's the same as the wall," he said. "You said that in the cellar. I didn't have it then."

"You had it. You had it in your hands and not in your mouth."

"Da says that about me."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. Outside, the frost had stopped making up its mind and got on with it, and the grass on the shelf would be white by morning and stiff enough to hear. Inside, the room held the cold it had held all afternoon and would hold all winter, and the four of us stood in it, and none of us moved toward the door.

Jorin came and looked at his son's lamp. He looked at it longer than he had looked at the door. Soren stood on the other side of the bench with the light in his eyes, adding it to the sum.

We left the lamp burning on the bench when we banked the place for the night. A first light should burn itself in. It burns there yet, under the hill, behind the door, beside the wall that waits, small and steady in the dark.
