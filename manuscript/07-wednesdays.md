# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Seven: Wednesdays

Word of the lumen went down the hill that autumn the way word of the wall had gone down it, and what came back up were people: one and two at a time, on invented errands, wanting to know whether such a light could be had for a house that stood on no hill at all. A life, if you let it, will find its own shape the way water finds the low ground, and mine found one that autumn out of exactly that, other people's errands and a boy and the bread, without my ever sitting down to design it. I have built a great many things on purpose in my time, and most of the things I built on purpose have fallen down, and the life that assembled itself on that hill while my back was turned is the one thing I made that has lasted. It was a small shape and an unremarkable one, and I would not trade a day of it for any grander thing I ever did, and I mean to give it its own plain place here before the year turned loud again.

The bread came up in the morning. That was the first fixed point of the day and the one I have missed longest. Soren brought it up from the village on his way to whatever a boy of his age is bound for of a morning, and he brought it without its ever having been asked for or arranged, the way he did most things, and it was always the right bread, the dense dark loaf from the communal oven that keeps a week, never the soft white that goes to dust in three days, because he had worked out, without being told, that a man alone on a hill wants bread that will wait for him. He would set it on the bench inside the door and take in the state of the fire with one glance and mend it if it wanted mending and be gone again before I had properly thanked him, and I learned to leave the thanking mostly unsaid, because thanks embarrassed him the way praise embarrasses a good workman, as a kind of interruption.

Then the kettle. Then the work. My days that autumn were full of the small honest labor of a practice getting itself onto its feet, and I found, to a surprise that shames me a little to remember, that I was glad of the work in a way I had not been glad of anything in years. Where the wall had brought the villagers up to watch a thing fail, the light brought them up to ask for something. It could not be had, not yet, though it would be, and I told them so honestly, and in the telling I found myself doing the plain work of my trade for people who needed it: a ward mended on a barn that took the damp, a set of drawing-instruments trued for the surveyor two valleys over who had heard there was a man at Ardenmoor who understood ground, a cold-spot in Kellard's storeroom talked out of the corner it had settled in. It was not grand work. It was the work of a country wizard in a small valley, and I did it well, and I was paid fairly for it, and every job of it was also, without exception, the slow business of a village deciding a man was a neighbor and not a rumor. I have done work that clever men still argue over. I am prouder of the cold-spot in Kellard's storeroom.

The cold-spot at Kellard's is worth the telling, because it was the first work I did in that valley that anyone but the boy saw me do, and because it shows the shape of the thing better than any amount of my saying so. Kellard kept the dry-goods store that was also, in the way of such places, the bank and the post and the parliament of the village, and he had a storeroom off the back where the flour and the salt and the good candles lived, and in the corner of it, that autumn, a cold had settled in that had no business being there. It was not the honest cold of a north wall. It was a wrongness, a patch of air three paces across that stayed at the temperature of a January midnight no matter what the season did outside it, and it was spoiling his stores from the inside, sweating the salt and freezing the tallow and putting a rime on the flour-sacks that turned them sour, and he had tried everything an honest tradesman tries: moved the goods, stuffed the chinks, cursed at it. None of it had answered.

I stood in that storeroom a long while with my hands in my pockets, which is most of what my trade is, whatever the tales tell you. A cold-spot like that is not put there. It grows, the way a knot grows in a tree, around a small original wrongness, some old grief in the ground or the stone that has been feeding on the ordinary cold of winters for longer than the store had stood, and the work of it is not to fight the cold, a fool's errand that only makes the thing dig in, but to find the small wrongness at the heart of it and give it, if you can, the thing it has been wanting all this time. Most often that is only to be noticed. I found it in the floor, under the third board from the corner, a place where something had been buried once, long before Kellard, long before the store, in grief or in haste, and had been asking about it ever since in the only voice it had, which was cold. I will not tell you what was under the board. It was a small sad thing and it was not mine to make a show of. I gave it what attention it wanted, and stood the way that work asks a man to stand, and closed the ground back over it gently, the way you draw a blanket up over someone who has finally gone to sleep. The cold went out of the corner between one breath and the next. By the time Kellard came back through with his lamp the storeroom was only a storeroom, and the air in it was the plain cool of an autumn afternoon, and he stood in the doorway and put his hand out into the place where the wrongness had been and could not find it, and looked at me with an expression I had seen before on men who have watched me work and did not fully want to have.

He paid me in credit against the spring, with the wary formality of a man who has been burned by cleverer men than me and means not to be again, and he wrote it in his book in a careful hand, and neither of us made anything of it. But he sent his boy up the hill with a sack of the good candles three days later with word that they had come in damaged and were no use to him, which was a lie, and a kindness, and the first of many, and that was the afternoon I understood that the account had turned over some invisible line and that I was, whether I had meant to be or not, a man the village had decided to keep. I have been given the keys of a city with less ceremony and meant less by it.

But the day I have set out to tell you about is Wednesday, because Wednesday was the boy's, and became, before I understood what was happening, the pin the whole week turned on.

Wednesday was the day his grandfather kept for the parish. Not that the parish always wanted him; whole months went by with nothing brought at all. But the day was set aside and known to be set aside, so that a man with a strayed ewe or a moved boundary-stone or a debt gone slow knew which afternoon to walk up Cael's path with it; and rather than have the boy sit through his neighbours' quarrels and learn too young how they argued, it had been let be understood, in the sideways way that valley let everything be understood, that he might spend the afternoon up the hill if the wizard would have him and if he was no trouble. He was no trouble. He was the least trouble I have ever had of another living soul.

I have been speaking of a grandfather and no one else, and that is because there was no one else. Soren was Cael's daughter's son, and she died the night he was born, and of his father the valley had finished saying whatever it ever said long before I came up that road, and said nothing at all now, which in a valley that size is a kind of masonry. The old man had raised him from the first hour.

A stranger would want to build the boy's gravity out of that, and the stranger would be wrong. He had never in his life had less than the whole of one old man's heart, and it showed. He was not solemn the way a bereaved child is solemn. He was solemn the way deep water is still, by nature and not by weather.

He would come up after his dinner and settle himself on the floor by the hearth with a slate his grandfather had given him, and he would be there, quiet as the light moving across the floor, for the whole of the afternoon, and the room was better for his being in it in a way I could not have told you and did not try to.

I taught him his letters on that slate, that autumn, because it turned out no one had, and because a boy that sharp going unlettered offended me the way a good tool left to rust offends a craftsman. He learned them fast enough, and his figures faster, for numbers were plainly a comfortable country to him, but I will be honest and say that the letters never took the way I hoped. He could read, by winter, well enough for the valley's purposes and better than his grandfather. But words on a slate were not where the boy lived, and I stopped, after a while, trying to move him into them, because a man should teach a child toward the thing the child is and not away from it, and the thing that boy was had nothing to do with letters.

What he was, was a boy who could look at a thing and know it. I do not mean he was clever, though he was. I mean that he looked at the made world the way most of us look at the faces of the people we love, seeing not the surface of the thing but the whole history of it at once, the intention in it and the labor in it and the place where the labor had gone wrong or gone right. He would turn my trade instruments over in his hands with a care that no one had taught him and set them down knowing, somehow, which of them was a hair out of true, and he was right every time, and it stopped being uncanny to me only because a thing stops being uncanny when you see it every Wednesday. I did not teach him my trade. He was too young, and it was not, in some part I could not yet have defended, entirely mine to give. But I taught him to look at ground, the first door of my trade and the one that stands open to anyone, and he walked through it the way you walk through a door in your own house, in the dark, without reaching for it.

I tested it once, that gift of his, though I am not proud of the testing. There was a Wednesday in the middle of the autumn when I had my trade instruments out on the bench, the whole nested case of them, the calipers and the trueing-square and the small brass level and the plumb and the dividers, because I had been truing the surveyor's set and my own alongside it, and I did a thing I should not have done. I took the good level, the one I had carried twenty years and trusted with my life on more than one bad night, and knocked it out of true a hair with my thumb, so small a wrongness that I could barely feel it myself, and set it back in the case among its fellows, and said nothing. The boy came up that afternoon and settled by the fire, and after a while, in the idle way he had, he asked might he look at the instruments, and I said he might, the way you say a thing you have arranged the answer to, and I was ashamed of myself even as I said it and did it anyway, because a man wants to know the edges of a wonder even when the wanting is a small meanness.

He took them out one at a time. He did not hurry. He turned each one in the firelight and felt the weight and the action of it and set it down in a row on the boards, in an order of his own that I have thought about since, because it was not the order they lived in the case, and I could not for my life have told you what the order was, only that it was one, that he was sorting them by some quality I did not have the sense to perceive. And he came to the level, my level, the sabotaged one, and he stopped. He did not say anything at first. He held it a moment longer than he had held the others, and a small line came between his brows, and he set it down not in the row with the rest but a little apart, by itself, and he looked at it lying there alone on the board, and then he looked up at me, and he said, "This one's sad." Not wrong. Not out of true. Sad. As though the small violence I had done it with my thumb registered to him not as a fact of geometry but as a hurt, a thing done to a thing that had feelings about the doing, and I sat there with the shame going all through me like cold water and understood that I had learned what I had meanly set out to learn and a good deal more besides. I trued the level while he watched, and told him the truth, that I had put it wrong on purpose to see if he would find it, and that it was a poor thing to have done and I was sorry for it, and he took that in with his terrible gravity and forgave me at once and completely, which was worse than if he had not, and said only, "It feels better now," which it did, and I never tested him again.

I should tell you about his solemnity, because a stranger meeting him for the first time expected a merry thing, an imp underfoot, and he was not that, and the expecting did him a disservice. He was the gravest child I have ever known. He took everything at its full weight. When his tenth birthday came, in the deep of that autumn, and I gave him a good folding knife of the kind a boy that age wants past all reason, he did not whoop or grin or do any of the things a child does. He opened it, and closed it, and opened it again, and felt the action of it with his thumb, and then he looked up at me with those still dark eyes and said, "This is a serious thing to give a person," and he was right. I have never in my life been thanked more completely. Cael told me later, in the one conversation he and I ever had about the boy directly rather than around him, that he worried the boy did not laugh enough, and I told him the truth: that a boy who feels things at that weight has to be careful with his laughing, the way a man has to be careful with a full cup on a stair, and that he should not mistake the care for sorrow. He took that in with a nod that closed the subject, and walked down my hill easier than he had come up it, and I have always been glad of what I said, because the old man had been carrying that worry alone for ten years, along with everything else he carried alone. He loved the boy without altogether understanding him, which is the harder kind of loving and, I have come to think, the truer.

So that was the shape of a Wednesday, and of the days it anchored: the bread, the kettle, the work, the boy on the floor with his slate and his grave attention, the light going long and then gold and then blue across the good boards I had laid, the lumen waking to amber as the dusk came on. It does not sound like much. But I had come up that valley a man who had arranged his whole life so that nothing in it could be taken from him, which is only another way of saying a man who had arranged to have nothing, and somewhere in the middle of those quiet autumn Wednesdays the arrangement came undone without my leave, and I found I had a hill, and a room, and a trade, and a boy on my floor, and a woman at the end of the lane whose light I watched for, and the whole apparatus of a life a man could lose. I noticed it the way you notice you have been warm for a while only when someone opens a door. And I sat with the noticing, by the fire, and let it stay true, and looked, from the outside, exactly like a man doing nothing at all.

It was on a Wednesday, at the very end of one, that the boy told me the thing I have turned over more than almost anything else that happened to me on that hill, and told it to me so plainly that I nearly missed it.

The light had gone. He had packed his slate away in the cloth he kept it in and was standing by the door in his coat, ready to go down before the dark beat him home to his grandfather's table, and I was banking the fire for the evening, and the lumen had just come up to its brightest as the dusk-fail pulse moved through the room, as it did every evening; a wonder you live with becomes furniture, and I had long since stopped remarking on it. And the boy stood there a moment with his hand on the door, not going, in the way he had when there was a thing he had decided to say, and he looked at the amber light in the glass, and then he looked at me, and he said, without any weight on it at all, as though he were telling me it looked like rain:

"The hill sounds different when you're working."

I stopped with the ash-shovel in my hand. I did not turn around at once, because I did not want to put anything on my face that might close the door he had just, so carelessly, set open. "Does it," I said, to the fire.

"It's louder in the evenings when you've been at something all day," he said. "Not louder. I don't know the word." He thought about it with the total seriousness he brought to everything. "Warmer. Like it's paying attention." He said it the way a boy tells you a plain fact about the world he assumes you know as well as he does, because he had assumed, all along, that everyone heard it, the way he heard it. And I understood in that moment, with a cold that had nothing to do with the evening, that he had been hearing it since before I ever came up that hill. He had been hearing the hum his whole short life. He had grown up in the sound of it. He had no more thought to mention it than he would have thought to mention the beck.

"I think you might be right," I said, and I kept my voice as level as his, which took some doing. "Best not to make a great deal of it, though. Not everyone hears the way you do."

He took that in, and turned it over, and accepted it the way he accepted the knife, as a serious thing given into his keeping. "All right," he said. And then he opened the door on the blue evening and the cold came in, and he said, "Goodnight," and went down the track home, quick and certain in the failing light, a grave small boy who heard a hill sing and had never once thought it strange, and left me standing in a room lit amber by a thing I had made, understanding for the first time that I was not the one teaching in that house, and had not been from the start.
