# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty: The Way Down

The thaw came late that year and then all at once, the way a man pays a debt he has been putting off, and for a fortnight the track down my hill was less a track than a long brown wound. I had gone up and down it for two years without once looking at it. I looked at it on a dripping morning in the first week of true spring, standing at the top of it while Bess Wray came up with a basket of eggs, and saw what it cost her: the choosing of every step, the wide way round the elbow where the seep ran, the one place she had to take hold of the thorn to keep her feet, eggs and all. She arrived at the top with mud to the shins and her dignity entire, which is Bess all over.

She would not have it mentioned, and I knew better than to mention it. She set the basket down on the doorstone and stood with her back to me, looking out at the valley as though she had come up for the view, and got her breath back without either of us having to notice she wanted it.

"There's nine," she said, to the basket. "There were ten at the bottom."

"I'm sorry for the tenth."

"It's the hen's loss and not mine." She pulled her sleeves down over her wrists. Then, because a true thing gets said in that family whether or not anybody has a use for it, she added, "Nell would have brought them up for me. I'd not let her."

That was the whole of the complaint and it was not offered as one. I asked her in and she said she had the wash on. I offered to walk her down and got a look I had seen from Bess Wray twice before and did not care to earn a third time. "They're my own legs," she said. "They've had worse mornings." So she went, and I stood on my own doorstone with a basket of eggs in my hands and watched her go: the wide swing out around the elbow, the check where the seep had taken the surface off, the hand going out to the thorn and coming back with the wrist barked, the last stretch above the stream taken sideways because sideways was the only footing left in it. She was the best part of twenty minutes getting to the bottom. It was eight minutes the other way in a dry week, at an old man's pace.

The truth is that the winter had worn it out. A track that had carried one man and a grey mare for two quiet years had carried, in a single season, eleven wagons of steel, a builder's crew twice a day for twelve weeks, a bed, a mirror, the better part of the village's curiosity, and every jar and sack and kindness that had come up that hill since the frost. It had been asked to be a road without anyone ever deciding it should be one, and it had done what ground does when it is asked too much without the asking being made proper. It gave up.

It had begun to cost me things I could count. Snow would not take the elbow at all after rain and I had stopped asking her to, so anything that came up in a sack came up on my own back from the stream crossing. Soren arrived every morning with his boots tied on and the bread held out in front of him in both hands, away from the mud and away from his coat. Kellard's man had twice refused the top of the track with a cart, and been civil about it, and had been quite right both times. And I had spent a winter persuading a valley that I was worth the walk up, and I did not care to stand at the top and watch the walk get harder.

I could not afford to mend it, and the work makes no sense unless the poverty of it is understood. The glasshouse had begun to sell, a little, early salads down to the village and the first potted stock gone out with Kellard toward Wainford, and every copper of it was spoken for before it arrived, because half of Jorin's winter wages stood deferred against that very money and Jorin was owed it first. I sat one evening with the figures and worked out what it would cost to have the track made good by men who make tracks good. Then I put the paper in the fire and got out my boots.

What one man cannot do is build a road. I knew that much before I started, and the knowing shaped the whole of it. A road wants width and grading and gravel by the wagonload and a crew who understand water, and it wants money, which is a material like any other and the one I did not have. But a way is a different thing from a road. A way wants nothing but somewhere firm to put your foot the whole distance, one foot after the next, dry-shod, in any weather. A child can use a way. An old man can use it. A woman with eggs can use it. And a way, unlike a road, is a thing one man can lay with his own hands, if he is not proud, and has a mare, and knows how to ask ground for a favour.

So that is what I did with the evenings of that spring. I laid a way down my hill.

The first thing a way wants is stone. Mine lay a day's round trip off at the end of the quarry road, and I went down for it on a bright cold morning with Snow under me and nothing in my pocket to offer anybody. It was an errand I had not run since I was a young man, and I had forgotten the taste of it.

Edran's quarry tells you it is coming a mile out. The road goes white, then the hedges go white, and then the hammering arrives, and the hammering comes down that valley with no reference whatever to the direction of the wind. The gate stood open because it always stood open. Inside, four men were working a face the colour of new bread, and a fifth was doing something unhurried to a horse, and not one of them looked up. I took that for manners.

The quarry did its business out of a shed with a window in it and a table across the width, and Edran was behind the table with a ledger open and his pen laid in the fold, and he did not shut it when I came in.

"I've come to ask you for your rubbish," I said.

He turned a page. "Which rubbish."

"The heap in the corner. The broken flags, the offcuts, and whatever has gone under them since your father's time."

I had rehearsed more than that on the road down. The state of my track, the state of my money, an undertaking to fetch every stone myself and shut his gate behind me, an offer of work in kind I was in no position to make good on before the autumn. None of it got said. Edran ended the speech for me without doing anything at all. He turned another page.

He let me take what I wanted off the waste heap, broken flags and offcuts and the flat misfortunes that come off good stone when it is being made into better, a heap the size of a cottage that had been growing since his father's day. He named no price. He looked at me a long moment first, and then he said the heap won't miss it, and went back to his ledger. He had the whole story round the village by supper. When I came down the next morning with Snow and the stone-boat there were two quarrymen leaning on the gate who had no reason on earth to be leaning on that gate, and they watched me load.

A stone-boat is a sledge with no wheels. It goes where a mare will drag it and nowhere else, and it will carry about as much as you have any business putting on it. For the first fortnight I put on more than that.

Flags come off a heap in no order. You take from the top because the top is what there is, and the top is what fell there last, so it is standing on edge with its weight in the wrong place, and you get your fingers under a corner and lift and find that what looked like an inch of stone is four inches of stone with three of them in shadow. I went down on my heels the first morning and put a flag on my own boot and stood there with my mouth shut, doing arithmetic about toes, and behind me one of the quarrymen said to the other, and not to me, "He'll want the barrow."

He was right, and they lent me the barrow, and neither of them offered to load it. If either man had offered I would have taken it, and the whole business would have begun as a favour and gone on being one.

They were on the gate the next morning as well, and the one after. On the third they had fetched a plank to sit on, and one of them had brought his lunch out with him.

"You'll be a while," he said.

"I will."

"How far are you taking it?"

"All of it. Crown to lane."

He looked at the heap, and at the mare, and at me, and put his lunch down on the plank beside him. "You'll be a while," he said again. It was not the same saying as the first one.

By the fourth trip I had the three things there are to learn. Take from the side of the heap and never the top. Build the load long and low, with the widest flag lying flat underneath. And a mare asked to start a dead weight on wet ground will not be asked twice in the same half hour, so ask her once, and mean it, and be standing where she can see you when you do.

Snow was better at it than I was. She had spent two years being the only working animal on a hill with almost no work on it, and there is a difference between being ridden about at a man's convenience and leaning into a real weight, and she knew the difference. She would put her head down and take the weight up through her shoulders and hold it there, and the boat would break out of the mud with the sound a boot makes coming out of it, and after that it was only a matter of walking.

The stone came off the quarry road into a heap of my own at the foot of the lane, and out of that heap it went up the hill three and four flags at a time on the boat, an evening's worth to wherever the work had got to. So there were two kinds of day in that spring: the whole days that were fetching, long and stupid and with nothing in them to think about, and the evenings that were laying. Only the evenings were the thing itself.

The valley got a season's talk out of it, and I let them have it. The wizard was cobbling his mountain. That was the word that went round, and it was fair. A man who could light a house without a flame was hauling broken stone up and down a hill three flags at a time behind a patient mare, on his knees in the mud of an evening, setting them one against the next like a poor man's kitchen floor, and no crew, and no wages, and no sense that anyone could see. One of the quarrymen asked me whether I meant to charge a toll. I told him the toll was that he would have no excuse now for arriving at my door with dry feet and an empty cup, and he took that away with him, and it was told at Lira's twice that season, improved both times.

One of the two tellings I heard myself, from a carter who did not know whose back was to him, and it had done away with the mare altogether. In that telling I hauled the stone up the hill in the traces, at night, and had refused the loan of six men from the quarry because I wanted the ground to know whose hands had laid it. "There's no beast in it at all," he said. "He's in the traces himself. Nights." I sat still and let it run out. It was a better story than the one I was living, and it cost nobody anything.

The work itself I will not make more of than it was. It was flags and mud and the plain arithmetic of one man's back. I cut the line straighter where the old track had wandered out of laziness rather than need, and let it keep its wander where the wander was honest, around the shoulder of rock, above the bad ground. I set the flags proud where the water wanted to sheet and flush where the wind would find them. Ren came up one Saturday with his own barrow and no explanation, sent by nobody, and set the ford stones at the stream crossing in an afternoon, each one true the first time, and went home again, and neither of us made anything of it, which is the only way Ren would have it.

That is all that was said about it, and the day was longer than the sentence. He came up at noon with the yard's dust still on him and a barrow in front of him, and in the barrow a shovel, a crow, a lump hammer, a coil of line and a bag of his own wedges. He set the barrow down at the stream and looked at the crossing for about as long as it takes a kettle to come on before he said anything at all.

"How wide are you taking it?"

"Wide enough for two feet and a basket."

"Then it wants five stones and not three. And they want to come out of the same block if there's a block to be had, so they wear down together."

I said he would have to make do with what was on the heap. He said that would do, and went along to the heap at the lane's foot and sorted it, and the sorting took him longer than the setting did. He would carry a flag out onto the grass, stand it up, look at it, turn it over, look at it, and put it back. I asked him once what he was looking for and he said, "The ones that are flat all the way across, and not just where you're stood," and went on looking.

"How much have you laid?" he said, after a while, to the heap and not to me.

"About two-fifths of it."

He straightened up and looked a long time at the hill. "There's a length above the shoulder of rock where no barrow's getting round. Every stone in that goes up on a man."

"I know it."

"Then carry the whole length's worth up first, while your back's fresh, and stack it at the top, and lay downhill from there. You'll walk to your work loaded the once and empty ever after."

It was the only advice anybody offered me about the whole undertaking, and it was worth more than the barrow, and he gave it with his back to me and never once referred to it again.

He would not take lunch. He had eaten, he said, in a voice that made a second offer look like bad manners, so I fetched him water and left him to it, and after I had watched him set the first stone I took myself off up the hill, because a young man who is being watched sets stone for the watcher.

When I came back down the crossing was in. He had taken the stream's own bed and had not argued with it anywhere. Each stone sat on a bed of the quarry's small stuff, settled with the handle of the hammer and never the head, three taps and a pause and three more; and when a stone was down he stood on it with his whole weight and rocked it, boot to boot, and then went and got the next one.

"The middle one stands a hand proud," he said, seeing where I was looking. "That's on purpose. You want to be stepping up out of the water and not down into it."

He asked me one thing about the crossing itself. "Does it come up?"

"After a wet week in the winter. To about there." I showed him the line of wrack along the bank.

He looked at that a while, and then went and got two more stones off the heap and put them in above the five, where nobody would need them nine months out of the year, and said nothing further on the subject. Then he washed his hands in his own work, and stacked his tools in the barrow in the order he would want them out again, and went down the lane whistling something with no tune in it.

Three evenings out of that spring will do for all of them, and I have picked them by their weather and not by their dates.

The first was cold and still had winter in it. I came off the hill at six with the light going and got nine feet done, and I did it wrong. I laid the flags on the ground I found, and the ground I found was soft, because taking out a spade's depth and putting back a bed of grit under every single stone looked to me that evening like four times the work for a refinement nobody would ever see. I went up in the morning and stood on my nine feet, and three of them rocked, and one had already begun tipping its water back into the hollow it had made for itself. So I lifted the nine feet and laid the nine feet again, and it cost me two more evenings. I have not believed since that a bed of grit is optional.

The body's account of that first fortnight is short and it is not heroic. My hands went first, in the web between thumb and forefinger where a flag's edge sits when you carry it in against your chest. Then the small of my back, which came right after four days and stayed right. Then my knees, which never did come right and have not to this day. I ate standing up. I fell asleep in the chair twice with my boots on and woke at two in the morning with the lamp burning and the fire out. There was a week in there when I could not have signed my own name to a paper that mattered.

The second was in the middle of the season, when the evenings had got long enough to be worth having and the ground had dried into something a spade could argue with. By then it had become a rhythm, and a rhythm is a thing you can hide inside. Cut the turf and set it aside grass down. Take out a spade's depth and a bit and throw the spoil below the line, because it will be wanted for the shoulder. A barrow of the quarry's own dust and chippings, spread and struck level with the back of the spade. Choose the flag, and there is always a right one within reach and it is almost never the one you had your eye on. Set it. Bed it with the handle. Stand on it, rock it, lift it again if it moves, and it will move, and put a pinch more grit under the corner that spoke to you. Stand on it again. Then the turf back down along both edges and pressed home with the heel, so that in a year you could not have said where the ground stopped and the work began.

Six flags is a decent evening. Nine is a very good one and you pay for the nine on the following day. I got into the habit of counting the last flag of an evening out loud, to nobody, and then walking up to the top of what I had done and standing there looking down at it. It was the most useless five minutes of the day and I never once left it out. The mind goes elsewhere in that kind of work and it does not go anywhere interesting. I thought about the glasshouse stove. I thought about whether a spade is a well designed object. Mostly I thought about nothing whatever, and came home tired in a way I had not been tired in twenty years, and slept like a dropped coat.

The third was late in the season and low down the hill, on the first evening warm enough to work in shirtsleeves. The light stayed and stayed. Midges came off the stream in slow columns and went where they liked. A blackbird sat in the thorn above the elbow and gave out the same six notes for the better part of an hour, and then a second one further down began giving them back wrong. My hands had stopped being a subject. I could take a flag up against my chest and put it down where I meant it to go without thinking about either end of the operation, and the spade had a shine worn on its shoulder where my boot went, and there was grit in the seams of every garment I owned and would be until midsummer.

I stood up at one point to straighten my back, that being the only reason I stood up any more, and looked behind me and up. There it went: a pale line stitched along the dark of the slope, bending twice, losing itself at the shoulder of rock and picking itself up again above it, and at the top of it my own roof with the last of the light coming off the slates. Nobody had asked me for it. Nobody had voted it, granted it, costed it or written a line of paper about it. It was the only thing I had made in two years that was not going to be entered in anybody's book. I stood there on the lower reaches of my own hill with a spade in my hand, and I mentioned it to nobody.

The one piece of wizardry in the whole business was at the elbow, the wet place where Bess had needed the thorn. There was a seep there that had been crossing the track since before the track, coming down off the high ground with nowhere better to be, and the old way of dealing with such a thing is a cut drain and a culvert and a fight you have again every autumn for the rest of your life. I did not cut anything. I went up at the dusk pulse with my hands in my pockets and stood at the elbow a good while, listening the way the hill had taught me to listen. The water was not going where it wanted to go. A fall of stone years back had thrown it off its old line and it had been making do ever since, without complaint and to everyone's cost. I asked the ground for passage. Not for dryness, and not for obedience. Passage, a way through for a thing that already wanted to move.

The first evening at the elbow went by without anything that anybody watching would have called work. I got there early, while the light was still honest, and stood on the wet with my hands in my pockets and my collar up and let the fidget go out of me, which it does in layers and cannot be hurried. Below me, the stream. Above me and off to the left, the high ground with a fortnight of thaw still coming out of it. The seep crossed the line of the track in four separate small tongues and not one of them was where it would rather have been, and you could tell that much through your boots.

Then the dusk pulse came up under all of it, later in the year than in winter and thinner with it, and for a while the hill and I were attending to the same thing.

I knelt down after that, because standing is for the first part of it and kneeling is for the second, and put both palms flat on the ground either side of the seep and left them there. Cold comes up through wet cloth quicker than a man expects and there is nothing to be done about that but stay. What I had under my hands was a line, and it was not the line the water was on. It ran a little east of it and about a foot down, gravel by the feel of it, an old channel with a lid of clay over the top and a shoulder of fallen stone lying across its head.

So I asked. Not out loud, and not in words, and it is a poorer thing to describe than it is to do. I put what was under my hands beside what the ground would rather have had, and held the two of them together, and left the difference lying open where the water could find it. Then I took my hands off the ground and got up and could not have told anybody whether I had done anything at all.

It took three evenings. It was slow small work and there was nothing grand in it. On the fourth morning the elbow was only a bend in a path, and the seep ran bright and orderly down a seam of its own choosing to the stream, and no one who had not watched me stand there would ever know the ground had been asked anything at all.

That is most of what my trade is, whatever the tales say. Finding out what a thing already wants, and getting out of the way of it earlier than anyone thought to.

Soren walked the way twice while it was building, top to bottom and back, slow, with his head down, not helping and not idling either, doing the particular thing he did that looked like a boy wasting an afternoon and was in truth a kind of surveying.

The second of those walks I had in front of me the whole afternoon, because I was working a length about forty yards above the elbow and he went by me twice. He came down first, unhurried, said good afternoon on his way past and did not stop, and went out of sight below the shoulder of rock, and a long while later I heard him at the stream, going over Ren's ford stones, and then going back over them, and then over them again. He came up past me at half that pace. He put a foot on every flag, and on some of them he stood a moment and shifted his weight from one boot to the other, and once he crouched and pressed the heel of his hand along the joint where a flag met its neighbour and then got up and came on. He did not touch my tools, and they were lying about everywhere. He did not offer to fetch or carry.

At the elbow he stopped the longest. He crouched and put his hand flat on the new-set flag, the way he had once put his hand on a stone at the crown of the hill, and then he took it off and stood and said, "It likes being crossed better than being stood in," and went on down, and I wrote that sentence into my working notes that night, word for word.

The last hundred yards were the easiest ground on the whole hill and I took the longest over them. The fall is gentle down there and the soil is deep and there was nothing to argue with, and I caught myself setting four flags where two would have carried anybody, and widening the line past any width a basket needed, and squaring off shoulders that nobody would look at, and going back over work that was finished. I had watched other men spin out the end of a job. I had not thought I was that sort of man.

There were a dozen or so flags left over on the heap at the lane's foot by then, and I went through them for the last one with more care than the choosing deserved. I did not pick it for its looks; it was not the handsomest thing left. It was the widest. It was flat all the way across and not just where you were stood, that being Ren's test and by then mine, and it would take two boots side by side with room to spare, because the first thing anybody coming off that lane would want was somewhere to put both feet while they made their minds up.

I finished at the bottom. I had left it for last on purpose, the way you save the last of something good. The way came down off my ground, over the stream on Ren's stones, and met the lane at the foot where the village began, and the last flag I set was the first one anyone coming up would stand on. I set it in the cool of an evening with the swifts going over, and I sat back on my heels in the mud, and a voice behind me said, "You're taking it the whole way down, then."

It was Lira. I had not heard her come. She was standing in the lane with her shawl about her and her arms folded, looking up the hill, and I got up and stood beside her and looked up it too, and for a while neither of us said anything. From the bottom, in that light, you could see the whole line of it, the pale flags going up the dark hill, bending where the ground had asked them to bend, up to where the Study sat grey and small against the sky.

"As far as the stream, at least," I said. "The lane's the village's. I'd not presume."

Lira looked at the way a while longer. Then she looked at me. It was the whole of her attention, and in two years she had turned it on me perhaps three times.

"It comes to the lane," she said, in the voice she kept for the ledger. "To the village."

I did not answer. She turned and went back in, and I gathered my tools, and when I came through the inn's door a quarter-hour later, mud to the knee and my hands past apologizing for, my usual place had a jug on it that I had not asked for, and my cup was full, and Lira was at the far end of the bar going through her accounts as though nothing in the room had anything to do with her.

"I'll mention it to Cael," she said, to the ledger, as I sat down.

That was all. Four words to a page of figures. I took it for the thaw it was, and did not think to wonder why, in two years of sitting at that bar, she had never once asked me a question about the hill.

I walked home up my own way in the last of the light, dry-shod the whole distance for the first time in two years. Behind me the village lamps were coming on. Ahead of me the flags held what the sky had left, going up the dark hill in a pale seam, and at the top of it my own lamp was burning amber in the Study window, where I had left it to come home to.

The stone had been down there under the Study through the whole of that laying, three seasons of the hill's nights on it by then. I went past its trap door every evening of that spring with my hands too sore to hold a pen, and did not lift it. I had begun to want to. I let it lie.

Soren came up the next morning with the bread. I heard him before I saw him. That had never once happened before, because he was not picking his way and swearing his small careful oaths at the mud. He was coming up the flags at a dead run, the way a boy runs when running is free, and he arrived at my door with dry boots and the bread unsquashed. He looked back down the long stitched line of it, and then at me, and said nothing.
