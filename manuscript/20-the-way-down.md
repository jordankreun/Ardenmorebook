# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty: The Way Down

The thaw came late that year and then all at once, the way a man pays a debt he has been putting off, and for a fortnight the track down my hill was less a track than a long brown wound. I had known it was bad. I had not known how bad until I stood at the top of it one dripping morning in the first week of true spring and watched Bess Wray come up it with a basket of eggs, and saw what it cost her, the choosing of every step, the wide way round the elbow where the seep ran, the one place she had to take hold of the thorn to keep her feet, eggs and all. She arrived at the top with mud to the shins and her dignity entire, which is Bess all over. But I had seen the track that morning the way you see your own room when a guest walks into it, and I did not like what I saw.

The truth is that the winter had worn it out. A track that had carried one man and a grey mare for two quiet years had carried, in a single season, eleven wagons of steel, a builder's crew twice a day for twelve weeks, a bed, a mirror, the better part of the village's curiosity, and every jar and sack and kindness that had come up that hill since the frost. It had been asked to be a road without anyone ever deciding it should be one, and it had done what ground does when it is asked too much without the asking being made proper. It gave up.

I could not afford to mend it. That wants saying plainly, because the work only makes sense if the poverty of it is understood. The glasshouse had begun to sell, a little, early salads down to the village and the first potted stock gone out with Kellard toward Wainford, and every copper of it was spoken for before it arrived, because half of Jorin's winter wages stood deferred against exactly this money, and a man pays his crew before he pays his convenience. I sat one evening with the figures and worked out what it would cost to have the track made good by men who make tracks good, and then I put the paper in the fire and got out my boots.

What one man cannot do is build a road. I knew that much before I started, and the knowing shaped the whole of it. A road wants width and grading and gravel by the wagonload and a crew who understand water, and it wants money, which is a material like any other and the one I did not have. But a way is a different thing from a road. A way wants nothing but somewhere firm to put your foot the whole distance, one foot after the next, dry-shod, in any weather. A child can use a way. An old man can use it. A woman with eggs can use it. And a way, unlike a road, is a thing one man can lay with his own hands, if he is not proud, and has a mare, and knows how to ask ground politely to be helpful.

So that is what I did with the evenings of that spring. I laid a way down my hill.

Edran let me take what I wanted off the waste heap at the quarry, broken flags and offcuts and the flat misfortunes that come off good stone when it is being made into better, a heap the size of a cottage that had been growing since his father's day. He named no price. He looked at me a long moment when I asked, in the way the valley had of looking at me that year, and then he said the heap won't miss it, and went back to his ledger, and I believe he had the whole story around the village by supper, because when I came down the next morning with Snow and the stone-boat there were two quarrymen leaning on the gate who had no reason on earth to be leaning on that gate, watching me load flags like men who had paid for seats.

The valley found it a rich entertainment, and I let them have it. The wizard was cobbling his mountain. That was the word that went round, and it was fair. A man who could light a house without a flame was hauling broken stone up and down a hill three flags at a time behind a patient mare, on his knees in the mud of an evening, setting them one against the next like a poor man's kitchen floor, and no crew, and no wages, and no sense that anyone could see. One of the quarrymen asked me, perfectly friendly, whether I meant to charge a toll. I said the toll was that he would have no excuse now for arriving at my door with dry feet and an empty cup, and he went off pleased with both of us, and I heard the exchange repeated at Lira's twice that season, improved both times.

The work itself I will not make more of than it was. It was flags and mud and the plain arithmetic of one man's back. I cut the line straighter where the old track had wandered out of laziness rather than need, and let it keep its wander where the wander was honest, around the shoulder of rock, above the bad ground. I set the flags proud where the water wanted to sheet and flush where the wind would find them. Ren came up one Saturday with his own barrow and no explanation, sent by nobody, and set the ford stones at the beck crossing in an afternoon, each one true the first time, and went home again, and neither of us made anything of it, which is the only way Ren would have it.

The one piece of wizardry in the whole business was at the elbow, the wet place where Bess had needed the thorn. There was a seep there that had been crossing the track since before the track, coming down off the high ground with nowhere better to be, and the old way of dealing with such a thing is a cut drain and a culvert and a fight you have again every autumn for the rest of your life. I did not cut anything. I went up at the dusk pulse with my hands in my pockets and stood at the elbow a good while, listening the way the hill had taught me to listen, and I found where the water actually wanted to go, which was not where it was going; it had been thrown off its old line by a fall of stone years back and had been making do ever since, the way water will, without complaint and to everyone's cost. I asked the ground for passage. Not for dryness, and not for obedience. Passage, a way through for a thing that already wanted to move. It took three evenings, and it was slow small work, more like combing a knot out of a child's hair than anything grand, and on the fourth morning the elbow was only a bend in a path, and the seep ran bright and orderly down a seam of its own choosing to the beck, and no one who had not watched me stand there would ever know the ground had been asked anything at all.

That is most of what my trade is, whatever the tales say. Finding out what a thing already wants, and getting out of the way of it earlier than anyone thought to.

Soren walked the way twice while it was building, top to bottom and back, slow, with his head down, not helping and not idling either, doing the particular thing he did that looked like a boy wasting an afternoon and was in truth a kind of surveying. At the elbow he stopped the longest. He crouched and put his hand flat on the new-set flag, the way he had once put his hand on a stone at the crown of the hill, and then he took it off and stood and said, "It likes being crossed better than being stood in," and went on down, and I wrote that sentence into my working notes that night, word for word, because it was truer than anything I had measured.

I finished at the bottom, which I had done on purpose, the way you save the last of something good. The way came down off my ground, over the beck on Ren's stones, and met the lane at the foot where the village began, and the last flag I set was the first one anyone coming up would stand on. I set it in the cool of an evening with the swifts going over, and I sat back on my heels in the mud, and a voice behind me said, "You're taking it the whole way down, then."

It was Lira. I had not heard her come, which put her in a company of very few. She was standing in the lane with her shawl about her and her arms folded, looking up the hill, and I got up and stood beside her and looked up it too, and for a while neither of us said anything. From the bottom, in that light, you could see the whole line of it, the pale flags climbing the dark hill in their long stitched seam, bending where the ground had asked them to bend, up and up to where the Study sat grey and small against the sky.

"As far as the beck, at least," I said. "The lane's the village's. I'd not presume."

Lira looked at the way a while longer. Then she looked at me, the whole of her attention, which she had given me perhaps three times in two years, and never once wasted.

"It comes to the lane," she said, in the voice she kept for the ledger. "To the village."

I did not answer it, because there was no answer that would not have said more than I was ready to say, and she did not want one anyway. She turned and went back in, and I gathered my tools, and when I came through the inn's door a quarter-hour later, mud to the knee and my hands past apologizing for, my usual place had a jug on it that I had not asked for, and my cup was full, and Lira was at the far end of the bar going through her accounts as though nothing in the room had anything to do with her.

"I'll mention it to Cael," she said, to the ledger, as I sat down.

That was all. Four words to a page of figures. I took it for the thaw it was, and did not think to wonder why, in two years of sitting at that bar, she had never once asked me a question about the hill.

I walked home up my own way in the last of the light, dry-shod the whole distance for the first time in two years. Behind me the village lamps were coming on. Ahead of me the flags held what the sky had left, going up the dark hill in a pale seam, the way a thing you have given away keeps a little of the light of the giving, and at the top of it my own lamp was burning amber in the Study window, where I had left it to come home to.

The stone had been down there under the Study through the whole of that laying, three seasons of the hill's nights on it by then, and I went past its trap door every evening of that spring with my hands too sore to hold a pen and did not lift it. I had begun to want to. Wanting to is the first sign a record is ready, and the second sign is being able to bear it a while longer, and I could still bear it.

Soren came up the next morning with the bread. I heard him before I saw him, which had never once happened in all the mornings before, because he was not picking his way and swearing his small careful oaths at the mud. He was coming up the flags at a dead run, the way a boy runs when running is free, and he arrived at my door with dry boots and the bread unsquashed, and looked back down the long stitched line of it, and then at me, and said nothing, in the particular way he had of saying nothing that was better than most men's speeches.
