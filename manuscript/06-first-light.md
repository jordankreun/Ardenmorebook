# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Six: First Light

A new room wants a light in it, and for the first little while I gave the Study the wrong ones. I stood in the door of it most evenings until the valley had gone blue, and then went in and lit a candle. I read by candles that autumn, and worked by a good brass lamp I had carried up from Vethmark, and both did the honest work a flame does: push the dark back a little way and ask nothing of the room but that it be dark to begin with. They were fine.

I remember one evening of it in particular, early in the autumn, because it was the evening the thing began. I had the day's notes to write up, half a page of nothing much: the hour of the dusk-fail, the weather standing before it, the state of the mortar at the northeast corner. It is dull work. I set the brass lamp at my left hand and three candles along the end of the bench, because one candle will not light a page a man means to write on and three will, and I sat down to it a little after seven.

The draught came in at the northeast corner. The mortar was still curing there and the wind had found some small road into the room that I never afterward located, and it worked at the candles all evening, so that the light on the page swayed as though the page were floating on water. The brass lamp wanted its wick trimmed every hour. It smoked the chimney a little each time, and the smoke laid a thin grey breath across the new stone above it, and twice I got up and opened the door to let the room clear, and each time the cold came in and the candles guttered and I lost the sentence I was in the middle of.

I got half a page written in two hours. My own hand's shadow went ahead of the pen across the page all evening, as it does, because one flame at your elbow can do nothing else and cannot help it. The room smelled of hot oil and new mortar and cold stone.

At nine I gave it up and banked the fire. The candles were not entirely to blame for the half page. They had been going since four, and there were fewer of them than there had been.

So I went down in the morning for more.

Kellard kept the dry-goods yard at the bottom of the street, and his was the counter where the village did the half of its business that wanted writing down. Two men were in ahead of me settling something about a cart going down to Wainford before the frosts, and I stood back and waited and listened and followed perhaps a third of it. One of them had the cart. The other had, so far as I could make out, a cousin, and a quantity of something in sacks, and a grievance about the last time. Kellard wrote three short lines in his book while they talked and did not look up once, and at the end of it both men went off satisfied, and I could not have said what had been agreed.

Then it was my turn.

"Candles," I said.

He gave me the look of a man who has not finished pricing his customer. "Dips or moulded."

"What's the difference to me?"

"Moulded burn clean and cost you near twice. Dips will smoke your ceiling." He said it flat and offered neither. That was Kellard being scrupulous. He would tell you the truth of both and then let you spend your own money in your own way.

"Moulded. Two dozen."

That got the first thing out of him that was not a price. "Two dozen."

"I read late."

"You read late," he agreed, and wrote it in his book in that careful upright hand of his, every letter finished before the next one was begun. Then he counted the candles out of the crate two at a time, and named the sum, and I paid it, and he wrote that down as well.

Coming back up the street I stopped at Jorin's yard, because I had the draught on my mind. Jorin was not in it. Ren was, dressing a stone with a broad chisel, and he stood up when he saw me and wiped his face with the back of his wrist.

I told him about the wind at the northeast corner and asked whether the wall wanted pointing.

He thought before he answered, sorting what he knew from what only sounded right. "It might," he said. "But new mortar sweats all its first winter. Point over a wall that's still giving off water and you trap it, and you'll be raking it out again in the spring and paying twice. I'd let it have its winter."

"And the draught?"

"Hang a bit of sacking," said Ren. "It's only wind."

I hung a bit of sacking, and left the wall to its winter on the word of a boy of sixteen with a chisel in his hand. He was right about it.

I went along to the inn after that to look at my horse. Snow had spent the summer in a spare stall at Lira's at my expense, and four months of standing about being fed had done nothing for her character. She put her head over the door when she heard my step, and looked at me a while with her ears at an angle I knew, and then turned her head away and stood looking out at the yard.

I stayed longer than I had meant to and scratched the place under her jaw that she permits, and went in.

Lira was in the taproom with her sleeves turned back and a slate in her hand, and she did not greet me. She set down a cup of the hot black tea she keeps for men who come in off the road, which I had not asked for and had not known she had marked me down as taking, and went on with her slate.

"Your mare wants exercise," she said, to the slate.

"She does."

"The boy's been walking her out along the river of an evening. I've not charged you for it." A pause exactly the length of a full stop. "I might."

"You should."

"I might," she said again, and rubbed something off the slate with the side of her thumb, and that was the whole of the conversation, and I drank the tea I had not ordered and went out into the street.

The climb took its half hour and a little more with the weight of two dozen candles on my back. But a candle in that room, on that hill, was a stranger's light, brought in from outside and burning something it had brought with it, and after a few evenings of it I did not want a stranger's light in the first room I had built in three years. I wanted a light that belonged to the place. So I set about making one.

A lumen is a lamp that does not burn. That is the whole of it, and it is harder than it sounds. Every light a man had ever made before that autumn ate something to give its glow, oil or wax or tallow or, if he was of my trade and foolish, a slice of his own strength. What I wanted was a light that fed on something already in the room and gave it back changed, the way a good window does not make light but takes what is offered and hands it on kinder. And on that hill there was something in the room to feed on, if a man knew how to ask. There was the hum.

The glass I already had, by luck and not by any foresight of mine. Among the cases I had carried up from Vethmark and set out on the bench that first week in the Study there was a flat wooden box packed with straw, and in the straw, each in its own sleeve of cloth gone the colour of weak tea, four ordinary vessels of blown glass. There had been six when I bought them, a dozen years before, for work of an entirely different kind, and I had used two. They were nothing much to look at: clear, a little green in the thick of the base, each one about the size of a pear and swelling at the middle, with a short neck and a lipped mouth. Some glassblower had made them in a shop I never saw and thought no more about them, and turned out six near enough alike to be sold as a set, and one of the four left to me had a bubble caught in its shoulder no bigger than a pin's head.

I took them out one at a time in the flat grey of a morning and stood them along the bench, and turned each one in my fingers, and found my hands remembered the weight of them. There is a particular chill a good glass has after three years in a cold box. I set the flawed one aside to be the last, on the reasoning that I would have learned something by the time I came to it, and I was quite certain by then that I would spoil at least one before I was done.

I built the body of it plainly, because the beauty of the thing was never meant to be in the body. A clean vessel of blown glass, and a collar and foot of brass that I had of Tomas at the smithy, my second small commission from him and the first I watched him make from start to finish, an education in patience. Into the glass I set a working. The working is mine and I do not give it out; on paper it reads as a list of nonsense words, which is all any working ever is to a man who cannot do it. But the shape of what it did was this. It did not command the hill's song, and it did not take it. It opened a small door and stood back from it, the way you leave a dish of milk at the edge of the yard and go back inside, and it let the hum come into the glass of its own accord and warm to a light there, and give that light back to the room as amber, the deep amber of late honey held up to a window.

The collar was Tomas's. Most smithies are a racket, and a good part of the racket is put on for the customer, the way a market-seller cries his wares; a smith who lets you watch him work is half performing, and the work suffers for it. Tomas did not perform.

His forge stood at the top of the street where the houses give out. The doors were open on a cold bright morning and there was no noise in the place at all but the fire. His stock lay racked along the wall in lengths sorted by section, round and square and flat, every cut end squared off true, and there was nothing underfoot. A smith who works in a mess is not necessarily a bad smith. A smith who keeps that kind of order is telling you something about how he thinks, and it repays the reading.

He was at the anvil when I came in, drawing a bar down for something that was going to be a hook, and he did not look up, and I did not speak. I was not going to spend his heat on a good morning. He worked the bar, and turned it, and worked it again. The hammer went at a pace I would not have called fast until I noticed that the bar was already the shape he wanted and I had not seen it get there. Then he laid it in the slack-tub, and the water spoke, and he set the hammer down on the anvil's face with the handle square to the horn, and looked at me, and that was my invitation to state my business.

When I brought him the drawing of the collar he looked at it a long while and said nothing, and then he looked at me the same way, and asked the one real question of the whole commission. "What's it to hold?"

I told him a light that did not burn. He took that in with no more surprise than if I had said a candle, and he made the thing, and it took him the better part of a week for a piece of brass a lesser maker would have knocked out in an afternoon, and when it was done it was so exactly the shape the glass wanted and no other that I could see he had made it not to my drawing but to the thing the drawing was reaching for.

I went down on the second day with a question about the neck of the glass, and found the question answered before I had asked it. He had spent that day making a former: a stub of iron forged down and filed to the exact shape of the vessel's neck and shoulder, so that he could work the brass over something that was the glass without ever putting the glass at risk. A whole day of a good man's time to make a tool to make one part of one lamp. I stood in the doorway and did the arithmetic of what that week was going to cost him against what a collar of brass fetches in any town in the country, and kept it to myself. No price had been named yet. It would be named at the end, by him, and I was not going to tell him it was too low and insult him twice.

The next time I came he had the collar roughed out and a second one lying in the scrap-tray beside it, and to my eye the two were identical. He saw me looking.

"Twist in it," he said.

I have thought about that since. There was a twist in it of some order I could not see across four feet of workshop and could very likely not have seen with the piece in my hand and a glass in my eye, and he had found it, and he had put a day's brass in the scrap-tray over it, and he had not mentioned it to me until I looked. He would not have mentioned it at all.

The rest of the week was files. He worked with the piece held in a wooden clamp lined with leather so the jaws would not mark it, and he took the brass down in strokes so light that a whole minute of them made no difference you could see, and the only sound in the place was the fire and the file and, now and then, the small dry breath of him blowing the dust out of the work. I stood a long time in that shop with nothing asked of me. The first hour I was impatient. The second I stopped composing sentences in my head. By the third I was watching a piece of metal come right, and would not have thanked anybody who spoke.

On the last day I carried the glass down the hill in a folded shirt inside my coat, watching my feet and disliking every loose stone on the track, and set it on his bench and stood back. He took the collar in one hand and the glass in the other and brought them together with no ceremony at all. It went down onto the shoulder and stopped where it was meant to stop, with no play in it anywhere and no forcing either. He turned the whole of it once and stood it upright, and then he took both hands off it and put them behind his back before he looked at it again, so as not to be touching the thing while he judged it.

He said nine words to me across the days of the making. Three of them were *twist in it*. Two were *come Thursday*. The other four came at the end and were about money.

He named a fair price and I paid it, and as I gathered it up he said one more thing, or the start of one. "That hill," he said, banking his fire and not looking at me. And then he stopped, the way a man stops at the head of a stair he has decided not to go down, and said only, "Comes on to winter early, up there. Mind it does," which was true, and was not, I thought, quite what he had begun to say. I said I would, and carried the collar home.

The first attempts came to nothing, and there were a row of them. I built the first version to draw. To reach into the hill's song and pull up a good draught of it and make a bright light, a proper light, a light to be proud of. And the hill did to that lamp what it had done to me the morning I went up the crown wanting to take something: nothing at all. The glass stayed dark. The working sat in it sound and elegant and greedy, and the hill declined to be at home to it, and I sat in the dark I had failed to lift and laughed, because I had known better and done the greedy thing anyway.

I sat there longer than there was any point in sitting. The fire had gone out at some stage of the waiting and I had not noticed, so that when I got up at last I was cold as well, and had to lay a new one on my knees in the black. That is the whole of the first failure. A man alone in a stone room at nine at night with a tinderbox and six days of work in a glass that did nothing.

Unpicking it took the better part of the next day and was worse work than the making had been: all the labor of laying a working in, and none of the pleasure, and paid for out of the same place in me. Soren came up with the bread in the middle of it and stopped inside the door with the loaf still in his hands, looking at the bench, where there was now one clean empty glass and more paper than bench.

"Is it broken?" he said.

"No. It's empty. I put something into it and it was the wrong thing, and I'm taking it back out."

He considered that. "Can you put a different thing in?"

"That's the plan."

He set the loaf down in the one clear place and mended the fire without being asked and started for the door, and stopped in it. "How many times can you do that before the glass is used up?"

It was a better question than I had an answer to, and I told him so, and he took that for an answer and went off down the track.

The second version asked instead of taking, but asked for a set measure, so much light and no less, which is only taking with better manners on, and it got one sullen flicker at the dusk-fail and went dark again, a door opened a crack and shut.

I was bent over it when it did that, nearer the glass than a careful man puts his face, and the flicker was so brief and so grudging that my first thought was that somebody had gone past the window with a light, on a hill where nobody goes past anything. I stood a long time afterward with my hand flat on the bench, waiting to see whether it would think better of itself. It did not.

That flicker taught me more than the flat refusal had. The first lamp had reached in with both hands and been given nothing at all. This one had asked, and been answered once, and not again, and the difference between nothing and once is the whole of what I had to go on.

I wrote it up in the notes as *second: one flicker at the dusk-fail, nothing after*, and sat looking at that line for a while, and then put the pen down and said out loud, to a room with nobody in it, that I was being dealt with as I deserved.

The third I overcorrected into something so timid it would not have lit in a thunderstorm.

That is the one I am least proud of. A man refused twice will overcorrect, and what I built the third time was a working so full of apology that it hardly opened the door at all. It sat in the glass across two evenings and asked for so little that nothing came. I ate a cold supper beside it on the second of them and went to bed.

There were three glasses standing empty on the bench by then, and one still in its straw in the box, the flawed one, the one with the bubble in its shoulder that I had put by to be last. I looked at the four of them for a while. Then I went out and stood on the crown in the dark until I was cold enough to sleep.

What I had left after that was one glass and no better notion than the three that had already failed, and I let it lie a full day before I put a hand to it. Pride wants sitting with, the same as a green wall wants its winter. I sat with mine and got up off it no wiser and a little easier.

Then I took the flawed glass out of its straw. A lamp that asks a little of a thing is given it; a lamp that demands a great deal gets a dark room and a lesson, and it had taken me three of them to believe it. So I built the plainest of them all: a lamp with no measure in it anywhere, that did nothing but stand open to whatever the hill was already saying and warm to it, the way a window stands open to whatever the day is doing. That was the one that woke.

I lit it, if you can call it lighting, on a still evening near the end of that autumn, at the failing of the light, one of the hill's two speaking hours. It was the first thing that hill and I ever made together.

I set it on the bench and moved the drawings out from under it, and then moved them back. My hands went on with that while the rest of me waited to find out whether three weeks had come to anything. I had not lit the brass lamp. A light should not have to compete for its first evening. The window had gone the colour of a slate roof and the wind was working at the northeast corner where the mortar was still curing, and the room was cold enough that I could see what I was breathing.

Nothing.

I counted. Counting is what I do instead of hoping. At sixty I put my thumb to the collar seating, for no reason except that it was something to do with a hand. At a hundred and twenty I began composing the line for the notes, and got as far as *the fourth also declines*, and stopped, because the floor had started.

It arrives through the boots before it arrives anywhere else. Then the footing, then the walls, then the bench under my forearms: the long low pull of it, the hill saying its one thing at the end of the day as it had said it every day I had been on it and every day for however long before I came. I had felt it a hundred times lying on the crown in the wet grass. I had never once felt it with my hand on a thing I had made.

The glass took a breath of it. The light did not catch or kindle or strike. It *drew*, and warmed, and woke to a low amber no brighter than a banked coal, and held.

It was not much light. It was enough to read a page held close, no more. But it brightened at the two hours the hill chose to speak, and dimmed to almost nothing in the flat middle of the day, so that it kept the hill's calendar without being told and burned brightest in the dark before the dawn.

I sat with it a long time that first evening. It threw my shadow up the new stone wall and lit the ordered spines of my books and reached, just at its edge, the northeast corner where the chest stood. I did not read a word. Three years before, I would not have had it in me to care about a lamp.

That first glass followed the hill the way a weathercock follows wind. It kept the hill's hours and none of mine, generous at the two speaking times, down to an ember through the flat middle of the day, and there was no more arguing with it than with the tide. For my own evenings I did not mind; I had come to like a light with weather in it. But a light at the whim of its source is a marvel and not yet a tool, and I could not draft at a bench that went dim whenever the ground fell quiet. So the later versions learned two things the first never knew. I taught the glass to hold: a second working laid under the first, a cistern under the window, that takes the hum in slow at the hours it runs strong and stands the light of it in the glass against the dry stretches, so that the burning rides level through the day on what the hill said at dusk. And I gave the collar a ring, more of Tomas's brass, that a thumb can turn to ask the glass for more of its keeping or less, so the lamp answers its keeper's hours and not only its maker's ground. Every working light I have made since has had both in it. The first lumen I never rebuilt. It sits where it has always sat, keeping the hill's hours, and I would as soon put a clock's works in a thrush.

I called it a lumen because I had to call it something and the old word for such a made light was near enough, and because *the lamp that drinks the hill* was a mouthful to say twice. The first one was never made for money. I made it because I had built myself a room and wanted, at the end of the day, to sit in it in a light that was mine.

It was the lumen, in the end, that brought Brenna up the hill.

I had met her already, in the way you meet a village, which is to say not at all and completely. Hers was the shop I had smelled that first evening riding in, the cut green and the clean bitterness beneath it, the smell of a place people go to when they are frightened for someone they love. Her cottage was at the far end of the lane by the birches, a low stone house with a drying-green always in the window, and in my first months I had bought of her the small honest things a man setting up alone needs, a salve for the hands that stone-work ruins, a tea that Lira swore by for a cough, and I had found her exactly as measured with me as the rest of them, and no more.

The first time I went in was in the middle of the summer's stone, on an afternoon when my hands had come to the end of what hands will stand. A summer of stone takes the skin off the inside of the fingers first and then off the heels of the palms, and I had reached the stage of discovering how many ordinary movements of a day require a man to close his hand. I had also been sent, after a fashion. Lira had listened to a cough I brought in off the crown at the end of a wet week and remarked, to her slate, that there was a tea for that at the end of the lane. She did not offer to fetch it. She did not offer to let it alone, either.

The shop was the front room of the cottage, and the smell of it came out the door and met you on the step, the cut green first and the clean bitterness under it. Bunches hung head-down from a rail across the window with the afternoon coming through them, so that the whole window was a green screen and the room behind it stayed cool on a hot day. Jars stood along a shelf with the names on them in a small clear hand. The doorstone had been worn into a shallow dish by one woman's feet over forty years and then by another's over thirty, and I stood on it and knocked on an open door, being a stranger.

She came through from the back and did not say good afternoon. She looked at my hands, and then at the rest of me, in that order.

"Stone," she said.

"Stone."

She took a squat brown jar down off the shelf and set it on the counter and then waited, because I had come in about two things and she knew it.

"There's a tea," I said, "that I'm told is good for a cough."

"Who told you?"

"The woman at the inn."

"Then you'll have had it in your cup twice this week already." She weighed the leaf out on a small pair of scales all the same, and tipped it into a twist of paper, and folded the paper over twice.

"She didn't say."

"She wouldn't," said Brenna, and named the price of the two together, and I paid it, and she wrote nothing down anywhere. At the door I said the salve was better than the sort I had bought in the city, having had cause to buy it there. She said, "It is." I was in her shop three or four times more before the leaves turned, on business of the same size, and she was the same each time, and that was the whole of our acquaintance.

She weighed a stranger the way she weighed her herbs, close and without apology, and gave him what he had asked for and nothing over. I liked her for it, the way I had come to like the whole of that careful valley, without yet knowing her at all.

She came up on a grey afternoon with the first real cold in it, unasked, and that was itself a thing worth marking: no one came up my hill unasked but the boy. I saw her from the Study window, coming up the track in a good plain coat with a basket on her arm and the particular unhurried climb of a woman who has walked up worse hills after harder days, and I went out to meet her because you do, and she stopped a little way off and looked at me, and then looked past me at the Study, and then she considered the whole of what she meant to say before she said any of it, and said all of it at once.

"I've brought you cuttings for that window," she said, "because a room with a light like that in it wants something living to look at, and you strike me as a man who'd not think of it himself. Rosemary, and a grey sage, and a thing my grandmother called soldier's-heart that will flower in the dark of the year if you're kind to it and ignore it, which is the trick of most things worth keeping. They'll do on a north sill, which is what you have. That's the first of my business." She set the basket down on the doorstone and straightened and looked at me. The first of her business had been the easy part. "The second is a question I've been carrying up and down that lane for the better part of a month, since the first evening your light came on in that window, and I'll ask it plainly because I'm too old to do it any other way. My grandmother spent forty years of her life listening to this hill. To this hill, and to the old oak in the north wood, which she said were talking to each other, and had been since before the village had a name. Everyone in the valley thought her a fond old fool, kindly, the way you think it of someone you love, and I half thought it myself, God forgive me, until a stranger bought the hill nobody could build on and built on it, and lit a lamp in the window that burns brightest at the two hours she always said it spoke. She left notebooks. Forty years of them. I've kept them thirty years in a dry chest and never known what to do with them, because there was no one alive who wouldn't have humored me for showing them, until now." She stopped, and let the question stand there in the cold between us without dressing it in anything. "Would you want to see them."

It was not, quite, a question, any more than the boy's had been. She had kept those notebooks thirty years in a dry chest and had not put them into anybody's hands. I did not answer at once. I looked at the woman, and at the basket of living things she had thought to bring a man she barely knew, and I thought about a hill I had been told was dead that had answered me when I asked. I had come into a valley deeper than the one I had bought.

"I would," I said. "Very much. Though I'll tell you honestly I'm not ready to read them well yet. I've been on this hill less than a year, and your grandmother gave it forty. I'd not want to open her forty years and understand a tenth of it. Give me a little time to learn the hill's hand before I try to read hers."

I do not know what she had expected me to say, but it was not that, and something in her face let go of a thing it had been holding, and she nodded, and looked at me a moment longer.

"That," said Brenna, "is the first sensible thing anyone has said to me about her in thirty years." And she picked up her empty basket, and told me to water the soldier's-heart once a fortnight and no more, and went back down the track into the failing afternoon, and left me standing in my doorway with three cuttings and the knowledge that I was not, after all, the first soul in that valley to sit still on my hill and be answered.

I did not know whether her grandmother and Cael's had been the same woman, or sisters, or strangers who happened to share a hill and a habit; the two old women who had loved this ground had come to me sideways, out of two different griefs, and it was not yet mine to ask which of them the valley's long listening belonged to, or whether it belonged to no one and happened the way weather happens, to whoever was quiet enough to notice. I let the question keep. Questions like that are not improved by asking them early, and I had cuttings to pot.

That evening I set Brenna's cuttings in three plain pots, and it took me the whole of the evening, for reasons that had nothing to do with the cuttings.

The cuttings were wrapped at the root in twists of wet sacking with moss packed round them, and the whole of it was done with the sort of care that costs twenty minutes at a kitchen table and is invisible to anybody who does not know what he is looking at. I knew what I was looking at.

I had no pots. It had not once crossed my mind in the whole building of that room that a house wants pots. What I had was three brown crocks off the shelf by the hearth: one with half an inch of lamp oil standing in the bottom, one holding nails of three sizes I had been meaning to sort since the summer, and one empty and unaccounted for. I poured the oil off. I tipped the nails out onto the bench and let them lie where they fell. Then I took the three crocks outside, and broke up a cracked one that had been sitting in the grass by the wall since the tent came down, and laid three or four curved shards in the bottom of each so the water would have somewhere to go.

For soil I went round to the north wall with a trowel, where the turf had never knitted back over the builders' scars, and cut what I wanted there. It came up black and cold and thick with small stones, and I picked the stones out with my fingers in the last of the light because it was not soil for anything I meant to keep alive with the stones left in it. I mixed a double handful of grit off the track through it. I have potted better plants since, in better soil and better pots, and I could not tell you one of them from another.

The work itself I did at the bench with the brass lamp lit for it, because the amber was never more than enough to read a page held close, and roots want seeing. Spread and not crammed. Each stem set to the depth it had been growing at before, no deeper. The soil firmed with two thumbs and not packed down, and water given until it ran, and then no more of it. The rosemary went in as though it had been waiting for it. The grey sage took longer. The soldier's-heart was a bare woody stick with three leaves on it, and I gave it no better than one chance in three.

I set them on the north sill, where the lumen's amber could just reach them. Then I sat in the light my hill had made and did not open any notebooks. Below me the valley put out its lamps one window at a time, and in one of them, down at the far end of the lane by the birches, a light I had not marked before stayed on later than the rest, and I took it for company.

It was a few days after that, with Brenna's question still working in me, that I walked out at last to look at the old oak properly. I had marked it my first morning on the crown, standing alone out of the middle of the north wood, and I had let it be ever since, out of a kind of courtesy or a kind of cowardice, and I could not have said which. It was a fair walk, out across the northeast meadow and down into the wood along the edge of my own woodland.

I went in the middle of the morning with bread in my pocket and took nothing else. There was nothing in my kit that would have listened for me.

The meadow first. Four and twenty acres of it, and nothing had been asked of that ground in a lifetime: no beast put on it, no blade through it, only the grass coming up and lying down again year on year until it had made a thick mattress of itself, so that crossing it in the cold was like walking on something that gave under you and then pushed back. The frost had been down in the night and had gone off wherever the light had reached it, and it lay on still along the north side of every tussock and in every hollow, so that the whole field was written over in white in the exact shape of its own low ground. I stopped in the middle of it, having never once in my life stood in the middle of a field that was mine, and gave the fact of it the attention it seemed to want. What I thought, standing there, was that four and twenty acres is a wide stretch of grass to be doing nothing whatever.

Then my own woodland, walked at last as a man who owned it and not as a man being shown it. Oak and ash, mostly, with the leaves down and the light coming through in a way it would not do again until spring. And where my ground runs up to meet the north wood there is a stand of larch, planted out in ranks a man can still read off the ground at forty paces.

Cael had told me whose they were in the spring, with the rows still green over us and the needles taking the sound out of the air. Old Harrow put them in, or paid a man to, having a notion he would cut them at sixty year and set his sons up on the proceeds. He had been six years in the churchyard by the time I came up that road, and his people down the coast had sold the ground to Penworth's agent without ever seeing it, and he had not lived to take a single stick out of what he planted. They stood in their rows that morning going the colour larch goes at the back end of autumn, a soft bright rust, dropping their needles onto a floor already inches deep in them and gone silent underfoot. I walked down between two of the rows, because a planted row asks you to. It is a long thing to plant, a larch. You put it in for sons who will be middle-aged before it is worth the saw, and instead a stranger walks between them one cold morning with bread in his pocket and no more notion of what to do with them than the trees have. I did not cut them. I have never cut them.

Past the larch the ground began to fall away in earnest, and the wood proper closed over, and the light went out of the morning. The wood was old and quiet in the way old woods are, holding the cold under its bare branches, and the oak, when I came to it, was older than the wood. I will not tell you it spoke to me, because it did not. But I stood under it a long while in the leaf-mould and the cold, and I put my hand flat against the great grey trunk of it the way the boy put his against stones, and I felt nothing I could name and a good deal I could not. It was not the hill's hum. It was quieter than that, and further off, and turned somehow away from me: the attention of a thing listening hard to something in another room, with no notice to spare for the man at its own door. Brenna's grandmother had called them the talking pair, the hill and the oak. Standing under the one with my hand on its bark and the other a mile behind me across the meadow, I could believe it, and could not, in the same cold breath, and I went home with the question larger than I had brought it.

The hill answered that evening. I do not have a better word for it. By then I could have set a clock by the dusk-fail pulse, and that evening it came stronger than I had ever felt it, and a full hour early, while there was still grey light in the west, as though something had happened in the day that the hill wished to remark on, and the only thing that had happened in the day was that a man had walked out and laid his hand on the old oak and come back. I lay on the crown in the last light and felt the hill say its one deep thing an hour ahead of its own long habit, and I thought about two old women who had given their lives to the listening, and a boy who heard it and thought nothing strange, and a tree turned away from me toward something I could not hear. I was standing at the foot of something and could not see the top of it. I put it aside. I could carry only so much at once, and I had a room and a light.
