# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Six: First Light

A new room wants a light in it, and for the first little while I gave the Study the wrong ones. I read by candle that autumn, and worked by a good brass lamp I had carried up from Vethmark, and both did the honest work a flame does: push the dark back a little way and ask nothing of the room but that it be dark to begin with. They were fine. But a candle in that room, on that hill, was a stranger's light, brought in from outside and burning something it had brought with it, and after a few evenings of it I did not want a stranger's light in the first room I had built in three years. I wanted a light that belonged to the place. So I set about making one, and that is how the first lumen came to be lit on the crown of Ardenmoor, though I did not call it that yet, and did not know it would be the making of me in the plain commercial sense before it was anything else.

A lumen is a lamp that does not burn. The tales have made them into everything from a holy relic to a cheap conjuror's trick, and they are neither. That is the whole of it, and it is also, I promise you, far harder than it sounds. Every light a man had ever made before that autumn ate something to give its glow, oil or wax or tallow or, if he was of my trade and foolish, a slice of his own strength. What I wanted was a light that fed on something already in the room and gave it back changed, the way a good window does not make light but takes what is offered and hands it on kinder. And on that hill there was something in the room to feed on, if a man knew how to ask. There was the hum.

I built the body of it plainly, because the beauty of the thing was never meant to be in the body. A clean vessel of blown glass, and a collar and foot of brass that I had of Tomas at the smithy, my second small commission from him and the first I watched him make from start to finish, an education in patience. Into the glass I set a working, and I am not going to give you the working, both because it is mine and because on the page it would read as a list of nonsense words, which is all any working ever is to someone who cannot do it. But the shape of what it did was this. It did not command the hill's song, and it did not take it. It opened a small door and stood politely beside it, the way you leave a dish of milk at the edge of the yard and go back inside, and it let the hum come into the glass of its own accord and warm to a light there, and give that light back to the room as amber, the particular deep amber of late honey held up to a window.

The collar was Tomas's, and the man deserves more than a word here. Most smithies are a racket, and a good part of the racket is put on for the customer, the way a market-seller cries his wares; a smith who lets you watch him work is half performing, and the work suffers for it. Tomas did not perform. When I brought him the drawing of the collar he looked at it a long while and said nothing, and then he looked at me the same way, and asked the one real question of the whole commission. "What's it to hold?"

I told him a light that did not burn. He took that in with no more surprise than if I had said a candle, and he made the thing, and it took him the better part of a week for a piece of brass a lesser maker would have knocked out in an afternoon, and when it was done it was so exactly the shape the glass wanted and no other that I could see he had made it not to my drawing but to the thing the drawing was reaching for, which is a gift I have met only a handful of times, and never in a man who spoke so little. He named a fair price and I paid it, and as I gathered it up he said one more thing, or the start of one. "That hill," he said, banking his fire and not looking at me. And then he stopped, the way a man stops at the head of a stair he has decided not to go down, and said only, "Comes on to winter early, up there. Mind it does," which was true, and was not, I thought, quite what he had begun to say. I said I would, and carried the collar home.

My first attempts at the working itself were failures, a row of them, and instructive ones, and I mention them because a story would have you believe such things come to a man whole and shining, and they do not. I built the first version to draw. To reach frankly into the hill's song and pull up a good draught of it and make a bright light, a proper light, a light to be proud of. And the hill did to that lamp precisely what it had done to me the morning I went up the crown wanting to take something: nothing at all. The glass stayed dark. The working sat in it sound and elegant and greedy, and the hill declined to be at home to it, and I sat in the dark I had failed to lift and laughed, because I had known better and done the greedy thing anyway. It is the oldest failing in my trade, and mine more than most. The second version asked instead of taking, but asked for a set measure, so much light and no less, which is only taking with better manners on, and it got one sullen flicker at the dusk-fail and went dark again, a door opened a crack and shut. The third I overcorrected into something so timid it would not have lit in a thunderstorm. So I unpicked the lot and sat a while with my pride, which wanted the sitting.

Then I built the plainest of them all: a lamp with no measure in it anywhere, that did nothing but stand open to whatever the hill was already saying and warm to it, the way a window stands open to whatever the day is doing, and that was the version that woke. A lamp that asks a little of a thing is given it. A lamp that demands a great deal gets a dark room and a lesson. I have come to think there is no working I have ever done on that hill that was not, underneath, the same working in different clothes, done over and over: the one called asking.

I lit it, if you can call it lighting, on a still evening near the end of that autumn, at the failing of the light, one of the hill's two speaking hours. It was the first thing that hill and I ever made together, and I remember the whole of that evening.

I set it on the bench and moved the drawings out from under it, and then moved them back. My hands went on with that while the rest of me waited to find out whether three weeks had come to anything. I had not lit the brass lamp. A light should not have to compete for its first evening. The window had gone the colour of a slate roof and the wind was working at the northeast corner where the mortar was still curing, and the room was cold enough that I could see what I was breathing.

Nothing.

I counted. Counting is what I do instead of hoping. At sixty I put my thumb to the collar seating, for no reason except that it was something to do with a hand. At a hundred and twenty I began composing the line for the notes, and got as far as *the fourth also declines*, and stopped, because the floor had started.

It arrives through the boots before it arrives anywhere else. Then the footing, then the walls, then the bench under my forearms: the long low pull of it, the hill saying its one thing at the end of the day as it had said it every day I had been on it and every day for however long before I came. I had felt it a hundred times lying on the crown in the wet grass. I had never once felt it with my hand on a thing I had made.

The glass took a breath of it. That is the only honest way I have of putting it, and I have tried others. The light did not catch or kindle or strike. It *drew*, and warmed, and woke to a low amber no brighter than a banked coal, and held.

It was not much light. It was enough to read a page held close, no more. But it brightened, just perceptibly, at the two hours the hill chose to speak, and dimmed to almost nothing in the flat middle of the day, so that it kept the hill's calendar without being told and burned brightest in the dark before the dawn, when a light is most wanted and least accounted for.

I sat with it a long time that first evening. It threw my shadow up the new stone wall and lit the ordered spines of my books and reached, just at its edge, the northeast corner where the chest stood. I did not read a word. Three years before I would not have had it in me to care about a lamp, and I sat there caring, and I could not have told you then what the difference was made of.

I will set down here the last of the lamp's schooling, though by the calendar it belongs to the winter, because it finishes the thought. That first glass followed the hill the way a weathercock follows wind. It kept the hill's hours and none of mine, generous at the two speaking times, down to an ember through the flat middle of the day, and there was no more arguing with it than with the tide. For my own evenings I did not mind; I had come to like a light with weather in it. But a light wholly at the whim of its source is a marvel, not yet a tool. I could not draft at a bench that went dim whenever the ground fell quiet. So the later versions learned two things the first never knew. I taught the glass to hold: a second working laid under the first, a cistern under the window, that takes the hum in slow at the hours it runs strong and stands the light of it in the glass against the dry stretches, so that the burning rides level through the day on what the hill said at dusk. And I gave the collar a ring, more of Tomas's brass, that a thumb can turn to ask the glass for more of its keeping or less, so the lamp answers its keeper's hours and not only its maker's ground. Every working light I have made since has been built on that double grammar, the open window and the cistern and the ring to govern the giving. The first lumen I never rebuilt. It sits where it has always sat, keeping the hill's hours, and I would as soon put a clock's works in a thrush.

I called it a lumen because I had to call it something and the old word for such a made light was near enough, and because *the lamp that drinks the hill* was a mouthful to say twice. I did not yet think of it as a thing to sell. But the first one was never made for money. I made it because I had built myself a room and wanted, at the end of the day, to sit in it in a light that was mine.

It was the lumen, in the end, that brought Brenna up the hill.

I had met her already, in the way you meet a village, which is to say not at all and completely. Hers was the shop I had smelled that first evening riding in, the cut green and the clean bitterness beneath it, the smell of a place people go to when they are frightened for someone they love. Her cottage was at the far end of the lane by the birches, a low stone house with a drying-green always in the window, and in my first months I had bought of her the small honest things a man setting up alone needs, a salve for the hands that stone-work ruins, a tea that Lira swore by for a cough, and I had found her exactly as measured with me as the rest of them, and no more. She weighed a stranger the way she weighed her herbs, carefully and without apology, and gave him precisely what he had paid for. I liked her for it, the way I had come to like the whole of that careful valley, without yet knowing her at all.

She came up on a grey afternoon with the first real cold in it, unasked, and that was itself a thing worth marking: no one came up my hill unasked but the boy. I saw her from the Study window, coming up the track in a good plain coat with a basket on her arm and the particular unhurried climb of a woman who has walked up worse hills after harder days, and I went out to meet her because you do, and she stopped a little way off and looked at me, and then looked past me at the Study, and then, in the way I would come to know was entirely hers, she considered the whole of what she meant to say before she said any of it, and then she said all of it at once.

"I've brought you cuttings for that window," she said, "because a room with a light like that in it wants something living to look at, and you strike me as a man who'd not think of it himself. Rosemary, and a grey sage, and a thing my grandmother called soldier's-heart that will flower in the dark of the year if you're kind to it and ignore it, which is the trick of most things worth keeping. They'll do on a north sill, which is what you have. That's the first of my business." She set the basket down on the doorstone and straightened and looked at me levelly, and the first of her business had plainly been the easy part. "The second is a question I've been carrying up and down that lane for the better part of a month, since the first evening your light came on in that window, and I'll ask it plainly because I'm too old to do it any other way. My grandmother spent forty years of her life listening to this hill. To this hill, and to the old oak in the north wood, which she said were talking to each other, and had been since before the village had a name. Everyone in the valley thought her a fond old fool, kindly, the way you think it of someone you love, and I half thought it myself, God forgive me, until a stranger bought the hill nobody could build on and built on it, and lit a lamp in the window that burns brightest at the two hours she always said it spoke. She left notebooks. Forty years of them. I've kept them thirty years in a dry chest and never known what to do with them, because there was no one alive who wouldn't have humored me for showing them, until now." She stopped, and let the question stand there in the cold between us without dressing it in anything. "Would you want to see them."

It was not, quite, a question, any more than the boy's had been. I have been asked for a great many things over the years, and given away most of them, and taken back fewer than I should have. There is a particular weight to being handed a thing that someone has carried, unwanted and unregarded, for thirty years, in the hope that it might at last have come to the one person who would not laugh. I did not answer at once. I looked at the woman, and at the basket of living things she had thought to bring a man she barely knew, and I thought about a hill I had been told was dead that had answered me when I asked, and I thought, not for the first time and not for the last, that I had come into a valley a great deal deeper than the one I had bought.

"I would," I said. "Very much. Though I'll tell you honestly I'm not ready to read them well yet. I've been on this hill less than a year, and your grandmother gave it forty. I'd not want to open her forty years and understand a tenth of it. Give me a little time to learn the hill's hand before I try to read hers."

I do not know what she had expected me to say, but it was not that, and something in her face let go of a thing it had been holding, and she nodded, slowly, and looked at me a moment longer with an attention that had a good deal of her grandmother in it, I think, though I never met the woman.

"That," said Brenna, "is the first sensible thing anyone has said to me about her in thirty years." And she picked up her empty basket, and told me to water the soldier's-heart once a fortnight and no more, and went back down the track into the failing afternoon, and left me standing in my doorway with three cuttings and the knowledge that I was not, after all, the first soul in that valley to sit still on my hill and be answered.

I did not know whether her grandmother and Cael's had been the same woman, or sisters, or strangers who happened to share a hill and a habit; the two old women who had loved this ground had come to me sideways, out of two different griefs, and it was not yet a stranger's to ask which of them the valley's long listening belonged to, or whether it belonged to no one and simply happened, the way weather happens, to whoever was quiet enough to notice. I let the question keep. Questions like that are not improved by asking them early, and I had cuttings to pot.

That evening I set Brenna's cuttings in three plain pots on the north sill, where the lumen's amber could just reach them, and I sat in the light my hill had made and did not open any notebooks, and was content, which was a word I had thought I was done with. Below me the valley put out its lamps one window at a time, and in one of them, down at the far end of the lane by the birches, a light I had not marked before stayed on later than the rest, and I chose, rightly or wrongly, to take it for company.

It was a few days after that, with Brenna's question still working in me, that I walked out at last to look at the old oak properly. I had marked it my first morning on the crown, standing alone out of the middle of the north wood, and I had let it be ever since, out of a kind of courtesy or a kind of cowardice, and I could no longer have told you which. It was a fair walk, out across the northeast meadow and down into the wood along the edge of my own woodland, and the wood was old and quiet in the way old woods are, holding the cold under its bare branches, and the oak, when I came to it, was older than the wood. I will not tell you it spoke to me, because it did not. But I stood under it a long while in the leaf-mould and the cold, and I put my hand flat against the great grey trunk of it the way the boy put his against stones, and I felt nothing I could name and a good deal I could not. It was not the hill's hum. It was quieter than that, and further off, and turned somehow away from me: the attention of a thing listening hard to something in another room, with no notice to spare for the man at its own door. Brenna's grandmother had called them the talking pair, the hill and the oak. Standing under the one with my hand on its bark and the other a mile behind me across the meadow, I could believe it, and could not, in the same cold breath, and I went home with the question larger than I had brought it.

The hill answered that evening. I do not have a better word for it, and I have looked. By then I could have set a clock by the dusk-fail pulse, and that evening it came stronger than I had ever felt it, and a full hour early, while there was still grey light in the west, as though something had happened in the day that the hill wished to remark on, and the only thing that had happened in the day was that a man had walked out and laid his hand on the old oak and come back. I lay on the crown in the last light and felt the hill say its one deep thing an hour ahead of its own long habit, and I thought about two old women who had given their lives to the listening, and a boy who heard it and thought nothing strange, and a tree turned away from me toward something I could not hear. I felt, for the first time on that hill, not wonder and not fear but the edge of a third thing I had no name for, the plain sense of standing at the foot of something whose top I could not see. I put it aside, because a man can carry only so much at once and I had a room and a light and Wednesdays coming.
