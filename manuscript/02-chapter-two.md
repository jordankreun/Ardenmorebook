# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Two: Walking the Bounds

The path up was not steep, and not truly long either, a walk of perhaps half an hour up from the last of the houses, but it was enough to take the last of the morning's stiffness out of my legs and give me back, in trade, a clean plain ache I did not mind at all. It was a cold, bright morning still, the way the early days of spring are before the sun has got any real heat into them, the dew not yet burned off the grass and the air with an edge on it that the climb was glad to have. I went up slowly. There was no one to watch me do it and no reason on earth to hurry, and I have never much trusted a man who hurries uphill; the hill is not going anywhere, and neither, if he has any sense in him, is he.

Near the top the ground gathered itself and leveled of its own accord into a broad green crown, wide as a market square and a great deal quieter. I came up onto it out of the climb the way you come up out of deep water, a little breathless and blinking at the size of the light, and stood, and turned myself slowly around once to see the whole of what I had come so far to look at.

I will not pretend to you that anything happened up there. I have promised you an honest accounting, and the honest thing is that I climbed a hill and stood on the top of it and was not, that morning, visited by anything at all: no revelation, no lifting of the heart, none of the wonders the tellers have since hung on that morning like ribbons on a tree. I had not rightly known what I expected, and so was neither let down by the want of it nor carried away by any presence in its place. What the crown of the hill gave me was a view and a great sweeping quiet, and both were worth the walk up. The whole valley lay open below me, Ardenmoor small and smoking at the bottom of it, the beck a bright wandering thread, the fields laid out in their first green like a thing only lately made and not yet finished. If there was anything at all uncommon in the place, it was only the quiet itself, which lay over the crown a little deeper than the height alone quite seemed to account for; but a tired man with a long road behind him can talk himself into and out of a great many things before breakfast, and I let the view have me instead. To the north the land folded into dark woodland, and out of the middle of that wood one great old oak stood up alone above the rest, bare-crowned and black against the morning, keeping its own counsel. I did not know then what that tree was, or whose it had been, or that it and the hill I stood on had been holding a conversation between them for longer than the village below had had a name. I only marked it, the way you mark a thing that stands apart, and thought it a fine old tree, and turned back to the valley.

I stood up there a good while. It is a failing of mine, standing and looking; I have been accused of it by people who loved me, and never troubled to mend it. The truth is that I had come a long way to a place I had no reason to be, and standing on the top of that green and silent hill in the new spring light, with the wind gone soft and the whole valley breathing below me, I felt that I did not want to leave. That was all. It does not sound like much. But I had spent three years making very sure that I wanted nothing I could be made to lose, and had grown good enough at it that the wanting of anything, even a hill, even the plain green quiet of a morning, came as a kind of alarm. I stood with it a while, the way you stand with a sore tooth, pressing on it to see how bad it is. Then I went down to find the man who could tell me whether the ground was as good as it looked, because I did not yet trust myself to say so, and because there are questions a man should not answer alone.

---

Lira had a fire going and a pot over it and the low midday hum of a room half full of people who had all come in out of the same cold morning. She looked up when I ducked through the door, took in the state of me, the wet to the knee and the color the climb had put in my face, and said only, "Well, you've been up it, then," and set a bowl in front of the stool before I had chosen it. She did not ask what I had found. I have thought since that she did not ask because she wanted to know too badly to risk the answer in a crowded room, and was saving the question, as she saved most things worth having, for when it could be asked properly. Not that day, and not in that room.

The room, that day, was worth the looking at. Ardenmoor at its dinner was Ardenmoor with its guard comfortably down, and I sat with my bowl and let it go on around me and learned more of the place in an hour of watching than a week of being told. There was a table of quarrymen arguing without heat about a horse. There was the woman with the geese, without her geese now, laughing at something over her shoulder. And there were, at the long table by the window, two men, an older and a younger, who had the look of kin.

The elder of the two I marked before I knew his trade, the way you sometimes do, because he had the particular stillness of a man who is good with his hands and has never once needed to say so. He was broad and grey and unhurried, and he ate without waste and without any part of him left over. When he set his cup down he set it down square to the edge of the table, and then squared it a second time with one finger though it had not needed it. That afternoon he was only a big grey man eating his dinner, and I was only the stranger the whole room was pretending with great courtesy not to watch.

It was Cael who did it, of course. He had come in on his stick out of the cold with the boy Soren a half-step behind him, and he lowered himself onto the bench across from me and said, without ceremony and loudly enough for the room to have it, "So. You've walked up my hill. What did you make of it?"

He had done it on purpose. I understood that later: he had asked it in the open, in front of the whole room, so the answer, whatever it was, would be public and not a matter of whispering. It was the first small piece of village governing I ever watched Cael do, and it was so quietly done that I nearly missed it for what it was.

"It is a perfectly fine hill with a fine view," I said, "and a great deal too much quiet to leave alone. I would build on it."

I had not planned to say the last of it, and the moment it was out I felt the room change. Not turn cold, nothing so plain as that; it was more that a certain number of people who had been half-listening began, without moving, to listen with the whole of themselves. Somebody down the room laughed, short and not unkind. One of the quarrymen said, to his cup as much as to me, "Build on it," in the tone a man uses for a thing he has heard tried before.

"Build on it," another agreed. "You and Cael's father both."

"Now," said Cael, mild.

"I mean nothing by it," the quarryman said, and to me, more kindly: "Only that hill's thrown off every roof anyone ever tried to put on it, friend. Forty years back and further. It's not a slight on you. It's the ground."

I said that I had heard as much, which was near enough true, and that I would take it under advisement, which was also true, though not in the sense they meant it. The moment passed, and the room let it pass, and went back with some relief to the horse and the weather. I had the shape of the thing now. The hill had a name in its own valley, and the name was *the ground nothing stands on*, and a stranger proposing to stand something on it was, to these good and practical people, a mild and familiar species of fool. I did not hold it against them, and I did not believe them either. What I heard in the warning was superstition and poor masonry in about equal measure, an old failed wall or two grown over the years into a law of nature, the sort of thing a valley will tell itself because it sits easier than allowing that a few men once built badly on a difficult slope. It did not land on me as a caution. It landed, if I am honest, as a challenge, which tells you a good deal more about the state of me that spring than it does about the hill.

All but one of them. I felt, rather than saw, that the grey man at the window had not laughed, and when I glanced that way he was looking at me, not unkindly, and not with the others' comfortable certainty, but with a flat assessing attention, the way you look at a stone you are deciding whether to set load on. Beside him the younger man, dark like him and built long where he was built square, had stopped eating to look at me too, openly, with a frank curiosity the older one would never have allowed himself. He caught me catching him and did not look away and did not color, which I liked. He only tipped his head a fraction, as though filing me somewhere for later, and went back to his bread. I knew only that a builder and his son had heard me say I meant to build, and had not laughed, and that this was worth remembering.

"That's Jorin," Cael said, low, following my look. "Best hands in three valleys, and the fewest words to go with them. If you're set on this foolishness, he's the man. He'll not tell you it can't be done, which is more than the rest of them will manage, and he'll not tell you it can, which is how you'll know he's worth his fee." He said it with the ghost of enjoyment. "But that's for later. Finish your bowl. If you're going to buy my hill, you'll want to walk the whole of it first, and not just stand on its head admiring itself. Not today, though; I'm no use to you today, I've a beast wants seeing to, and you've the look of a man who's owed a chair by a fire more than he's owed another march over wet ground. Come to me in the morning, rested, and I'll walk you the whole of it and show you what your man's money has actually gone and bought you. It's more than you think."

---

So I did not see the rest of it that day. I spent what was left of the afternoon and the long slow evening in a kind of idleness I had nearly forgotten how to keep, and slept a second night under Lira's eaves, and it was the following day, past noon and with the old man's knees at last willing, that we went to walk the bounds.

It was a great deal more than I thought. I had come up the valley with the vague idea that I was buying a hill and perhaps the skirts of it; Penworth, it turned out, had done what Penworth always did, which was to quietly acquire the elbow room before anyone thought to charge extra for it. "He didn't buy you a hill," Cael said, with real appreciation, as we came out past the last houses with Soren trailing and a keen bright wind coming down off the high ground. "He bought you the hill, which is mine to sell and worth nothing to a farmer, and you'll not make it worth more. But he didn't stop at the hill, your man. The woodland along the low road, now, the half of that was mine and the half of it was old Harrow's, who's been six years dead and whose people down the coast had let it stand and rot for want of anyone at hand to say what to do with it; your agent found them and made it worth their while to sign. The meadow off the northeast shoulder belonged to the Tolls, who never once grazed it and were glad of the coin. And the good plough-land down in the bottoms is four men's idle acres he's stitched into the one holding that'll bring you a rent, once you've found the right families to put on it. There's ground in among it, mind, that no soul in this valley ever owned at all, common strips and the odd forgotten corner the village has let lie since before my grandfather's time, and rather than leave you a snarl to argue over, your man sat down with the parish and made even those honest and yours. A stake in the mill-pond water besides. That agent of his sat at my table three evenings running with a map and a pencil, and every evening the map got bigger and the pencil got sharper. I've sold land my whole life. I never once sold it to a man who wanted the ground his ground would need before his ground needed it, nor took the trouble to square the bits nobody else had ever thought worth the bother."

"That's Penworth entire," I said. "He says a wise man buys his neighbors' patience before he has done anything to spend it."

Cael laughed at that, a short surprised bark of a laugh, and said his grandmother would have liked the sound of it, and we walked on.

We walked the whole of it, that afternoon, the long slow way round, the way you walk a thing you mean to be answerable for. Cael went at the pace his knees allowed and I was glad of it, because it was the right pace for the ground, slow enough to see. He showed me the spring that came up cold and clear out of the hill's south face and had never once, in living memory or his grandmother's, gone dry; a thing worth more than the hill on top of it, he said, and he was right, and I would have cause to bless that spring a hundred times in the years after. He showed me the woodland, good mixed timber, oak and ash and a stand of larch a former owner had planted and not lived to cut. He showed me where the grade eased along the eastern slope into a shelf of deeper, greener grass, and I felt something about that shelf that I had not the knowledge yet to name and did not mention, and Cael did not mention it either, though I caught him watching me not mention it. And all the while the boy Soren went ahead of us and to the side, quiet as weather, stopping now and then to crouch over some particular patch of ground with the total absorption of a child at his real work, and once, near the crown, he stood for a long moment with his bare hand flat on a stone, and then took it off, and came on, and said nothing, and I thought nothing of it, which is a thing I would give a good deal to go back and un-think.

It was on the crown, with the whole of the bounds walked and the light going long and golden, that Cael told me about his father.

He did not make a story of it. That was the thing about Cael; he approached the things that mattered to him sideways and set them down plainly, as if they weighed nothing, and let you find out for yourself what they weighed. "My father tried to build up here," he said, looking not at me but out over the valley, leaning on his stick. "When I was smaller than that one." A nod at Soren. "A shelter, only. A stone bothy for the high pasture, four walls and a roof, the sort of thing he'd thrown up a dozen times on a dozen other slopes and never thought twice about. He was a fair mason, my father. Not Jorin. But fair." He was quiet a moment. "It would not stand. The first wall went up and came down in the night with no wind to blame. He set it again, deeper, and it came down again. Timbers he'd cut true went in crooked, or came out crooked, or would not sit at all. Mortar that held everywhere else in the valley would not cure up here. Stayed wet, stayed soft, a fortnight, a month. He was not a fanciful man, my father, no more than I am. He was a stubborn one. He gave that hill a whole summer of his life and the hill gave him back a heap of fallen stone, and at the end of it he came down and never went up again, and he told me a thing I have kept word for word, for I was a boy then and it went in deep: *the ground won't take it, son. Some ground won't. Leave it be.*"

He turned and looked at me then, straight, an old man laying the truth on the table between us where I could not miss it. "So there it is, and you'll have it from me and not from the tavern. That's what you're buying. A hill that put my father on his knees and has thrown off every roof and wall any soul has tried to set on it in the two lifetimes I can account for. My grandmother loved it and heard God-knows-what in it and left it to me and I've kept it forty years for no reason I could ever say aloud, and now here's you, come up a bad road with a wizard's trade and a dead man's..." He stopped himself, gently, on whatever he had seen in my face when he was not looking, and did not finish it, and went on more quietly. "And a great deal of quiet you're after. I'll not cheat you, is my whole point. If you buy this hill you may spend a great many years and a great deal of your man's money learning what my father learned in one summer. I'd think less of myself if I let you sign without hearing it said."

I have been offered a great many bargains in a long life, and flattered and hurried and lied to over most of them, and I can tell you that nothing ever moved me to close a deal so fast as that old man doing his level best to talk me out of one. But it was not only the honesty, though the honesty was a great part of it. It was that he had told me the hill threw off everything set upon it, and I had stood on the crown of it that morning and felt it, for the first time in three years, take hold of *me*; and I was arrogant enough, or wounded enough, or simply wizard enough, to wonder whether the two facts might be the same fact wearing two faces. His father had asked the hill for a level floor and four square walls, had *told* it, the way a fair and stubborn mason tells any ground what it will do. I did not yet have the words for what I suspected (I would not find those words for weeks, in the margin of an old dead surveyor's book), but I had the shape of it already, standing there in the gold light, and the shape of it was this: that there is a great difference between a thing that cannot be built on, and a thing that has never once been *asked*.

"How long," I said, "would your man of affairs and mine need, to make it all proper?"

Cael looked at me a moment longer. Then something went out of his shoulders that had been in them, I think, for forty years, and he said, "You're sure, then."

"I'm sure."

"You heard what I told you."

"Every word. I'm sure."

And the old man nodded, and looked away out over the valley so that I would not see his face do what it did, and said, roughly, "Then it's yours, and God help you, and I'm glad." He put out his hand and I took it, there on the crown of the hill with his grandson watching and the light going down the sky, and we shook on it the old way, which is the only way that has ever truly bound anything: two men, and a piece of ground, and a price already fair, and nothing written down at all.

"The rest," I said, "you can leave to Penworth. He'll be desolate if he doesn't get to do the paper. It's the only thing that gives the man joy. He'll draw it up in triplicate and dun me for the fee and settle every copper with your fellow before the month's out, and he'll do it better and more honestly than either of us could manage, and enjoy every miserable line of it." I meant it lightly, and Cael took it lightly, but I meant the thing under it too: that the one part that mattered, the part only the two of us could do standing on the ground itself, was done; and that all the rest, the deeds and drafts and letters of credit, was exactly the burden a man keeps a Penworth to carry, so the promise itself could stay clean.

We came down off the hill together in the last of the light, the old man and the boy and I, and my legs by then had the honest ruin in them of a man who has walked a great deal of ground he means to answer for, and it was the best I had felt in longer than I could easily say.

That night I sat up late at the little table under the eaves at Lira's, with a borrowed candle and my own traveling ink, and wrote to Penworth. It was not a long letter; Penworth did not reward long letters, only precise ones. I told him I had seen the hill and walked the bounds and shaken on the whole of it with the seller, at his figure, and that he was to finish it, all of it, the purchase and the parcels and the water rights, and to spare no care over the water rights in particular, as I had seen the spring and would build my life around it. I told him the seller was an honest man named Cael and was to be dealt with as such. I did not tell him that I meant to build on ground that had never let itself be built on, nor that I had felt the ground take hold of me, nor any of the rest of it; Penworth would learn what he needed as he needed it, and worry over exactly the right amount, which in Penworth was a great deal. I signed it, and sealed it, and set it by for the morning post, and then I put out the candle and sat a while in the dark with the small window open a crack to the cold.

The hill was out there in the night, a darker weight against the dark, mine now in every way that a handshake can make a thing yours and the paper only catches up to later. I could not see it. But I sat with the window open until the cold drove me under the blankets, and I will tell you plainly that I did not once, that whole while, take out the letter I had carried so long and held so often. I had walked a hill instead. It is not much. But it was the first evening in three years that I had wanted the morning to come, and a man has to start his way back from somewhere, and I started mine there, at a stranger's window, over a piece of ground that everyone in the valley but me had the good sense to leave alone.
