# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter One: Ardenmoor

I came to Ardenmoor at the tail end of a long road, in the third week of spring, on a good grey mare named Snow, who had concluded some hours back that I did not know where I was going.

She had a point, though the fault was not entirely mine. I knew where I was going the way you know a thing you have been told rather than a thing you have seen. There was a hill for sale above a village of that name, and a fair breadth of ground around it, and I had come to look at what I had all but already bought. Penworth had told me to, and I had long since given up trusting my own judgment about what was good for me over his.

Penworth was my man of affairs, and the running of my tangled life had been his for something over a decade. He kept the letters, the monies, and the steady mountain of paper a working life throws off whether a man attends to it or not. He was a spare, precise, exceedingly formal gentleman out of Vethmark, near enough my own age and much tidier about the wearing of it, with a genius for finding me things before I knew I wanted them and quietly securing the ground beneath them while I was still making up my mind. His letter reached me at the turn of the year and went soft at the folds from a season in my coat. It was three close pages to say he had found a piece of country he believed would suit me, *a hill of no agricultural worth and considerable quiet*; that he had provisionally secured it, along with workable land fit for tenants; and that he would count it a personal kindness if I looked at what I had nearly purchased before he made me finish the purchasing.

There was no thread of fate in it, whatever a story would want. I did not come to Ardenmoor drawn by anything grander than an errand. I came because Penworth had an unerring eye and I had learned to trust it as my own; because it was spring, and I had spent the winter shut up in a quiet Vethmark townhouse with the spare rooms closed and sheeted, keeping out of the way of the noise and the standing crowd of small obligations a city presses on a man whether he asks for them or not; and because there was nowhere else in the wide world I would rather have been. In those days that was less a boast than a fairly bleak measure of how small my life had got. *A hill of no agricultural worth and considerable quiet.* When I read that, it sounded very nearly like the only thing left that I wanted.

The townhouse I had wintered in was my father's before it was mine, and my father had been an architect, which is to say a man who drew the houses, shops, and buildings that other men lived their whole lives inside of. He built a small respected firm about the doing of it, one that outlived him and stands yet, and I hold his share in it still. I learned drawing at his elbow as a boy and paid my way through school with drafting work at a board of my own, before I ever took up any stranger trade.

The house he kept for himself he had drawn for my mother, early, when the firm was still more hope than trade and he built as much for love as for money. It was the finest small thing he ever set his hand to, in what had then been a quiet, unfashionable quarter of the city. The quarter did not stay unfashionable. The city  grew dear and grand around it, tall bright houses rising to either side until my father's modest place sat among them like an honest word in an ornate sentence, worth many times what it had cost him and not a coin of that worth for anything he would have counted. I grew up in those rooms and was happy in them, when the house smelled of drawing-ink and my mother's baking and the windows stood open on the garden half the year. When they were gone it came to me, and after everything I came back to it: the one place that had never asked me to be anything more than I was.

I had never meant to stay long. My father's house was a stopgap, somewhere to keep myself and my boots while my own went up across the river; and my own was to be a wonder, or a warning, depending on when you asked. It was grander than anything my father would have put his name to, taller, flashier, altogether more foolish: his clean lines swollen to thrice the scale and as many times the cost, a great bright gesture ahead of a wedding. I commissioned it of my father's firm. That is the polite way of saying I went to the men who had taught me and told them what I wanted instead of asking them what was good. I drew a small part of it myself in the good years, when I still supposed I knew what I was building toward, and left them the rest of the drawing and the long patient business of putting it up. There was no wedding. I never lived in the house. For three years it stood across the water as a reminder of a life that had not happened, costing a small fortune to keep empty, and I could no more sell it than cross its threshold. Penworth spent that winter trying, with a delicacy that cost him something, to talk me round to letting it go, for the sake of my purse and, though he was too careful to say it, the rest of me.

He did it by letter, once a fortnight, all through the dark of the year, and never twice in the same way. He wrote about the roof. He wrote about the caretaker's wages, and the standing order for coal at a house nobody heated, and the fact that a chimney unswept in an empty building is a worse business than a chimney unswept in a full one. Before ever the hill was mentioned, he wrote me this.

*Sir,*

*The quarter's accounting for the property across the water is enclosed. I have set the caretaker's item at the head of the second column rather than the foot of the first, where it has sat since the house was finished, on the principle that a figure a man has learned the shape of stops being a figure and becomes part of the furniture.*

*You will observe that no single item in it is large. It is not intended to alarm you, and I would not insult you by arranging it so that it did. What I would ask you to observe is the length of the column. There is nothing in it that is wrong. There is nothing in it I could strike out and be thanked for afterward. It is simply the cost of keeping a sound house in good order that no person has any intention of entering, and it will be very much the same next quarter, and the quarter after that, and I am obliged to tell you it will go on being very much the same for as long as we both continue to be reasonable men about it.*

*I am aware you will read this as the resumption of a conversation we have already had twice. It is. I have nothing new to put into it. I have only the same three things I had in the autumn: that the house is sound, that it is empty, and that those two facts are not going to become friendly with one another through the mere passage of time.*

*One further remark and I will leave off. You have instructed me, on several occasions and in writing, to attend to those parts of your affairs you do not care to think about. I have done so faithfully and would do so for the remainder of my working life without complaint. But this is not one of those parts. This is a thing you have asked me to carry so that you need not decide about it, and there is a difference between the two, and I find I have grown less willing to pretend I cannot see it.*

*The spring, when it comes, will improve nothing whatever about the arithmetic.*

*I remain, sir, your obedient servant,*

*Penworth.*

I wrote back that I would think about it. We both understood that to be a door shut quietly rather than a door left ajar. He went on writing all the rest of that winter, once a fortnight, and set the same three things in front of me in different clothes each time, and never once observed aloud that I had done no thinking about them. Then the thaw came, and he stopped setting them in front of me at all.

What I had not understood, riding up that valley, was that Penworth had stopped waiting. Somewhere in the thaw he found a buyer willing to begin the slow lawyerly business of purchasing a house of that size and breathed not a word of it in his letter. He offered me a hill instead and asked only, in that mild and immovable way of his, that I look before he let me finish buying it. I gave him a yes to the looking and half a yes to the rest, and half a yes was all Penworth ever needed. He knew his man: give me something fresh to accept, and somewhere sensible for the money to go, and the house I could not make myself sell might consent to be sold out from under me while I stood in a far green field admiring a hill. The errand was the lever, though he was too tidy a man ever to call it so. I did not much mind being pried loose. I was tired, it was spring, and a man has to be sent somewhere. I would as soon it were a hill.

The first and easier part I came by rail: three days out of Vethmark in a carriage that rattled the teeth in a man's head and let the cold in at every seam. The railways were new, and it showed. The engine was a crude, soot-breathing thing, all iron and steam and appetite. Many called the train magical, though there was nothing arcane in it. You may put a working into a machine—I have put a good many into a good many—but a working must be fed, tended, and coaxed into existence. One large enough to shoulder a train up a grade would then have eaten more in the making than a lifetime of coal. That is the arithmetic people get backwards about my trade. There is no shortage of magic in the world; it is short of people who can work it. Coal the world has by the mountain, iron by the shire, stokers by the thousand. Of those born able to set a lasting working into iron it has a scattered handful, every one wanted in six places at once. Put them all to nursing locomotives and you might keep one railway arcane, while nothing else was warded, mended, or lit. So no sane man spends a wizard where a stoker will serve. The railways ran on fire, water, and brute iron cleverness. I spent much of those three days watching the engine labor at the watering-stops and thinking how much smaller and quieter it might have been. That was the first hard fact of my trade, and the trains were a great smoking monument to it: crude iron doing loudly what a little good artifice could have done well, had there been enough good artifice to go around.

I got down at every one of those stops. The other passengers kept their seats with their collars up and their opinions of me to themselves, and I do not blame them. There is nothing at a watering-stop but cold, cinders, and the smell of hot iron beginning to cool.

The second day's long stop came in a cut between two brown hills, with a wooden tank standing on legs above the track and nothing else in sight but a hut and a heap of sleepers. The engine drew up under the tank and stood there breathing. That is the thing nobody tells you about a locomotive. At rest it does not go quiet. It ticks and knocks and sighs. Every so often it lets off a burst of steam at the ankles of whoever has come nearest. The men who work them never flinch. Everyone else does, every time, and the crews enjoy that more than they let on.

I went up the train first to see about Snow. She had a box near the front. It had seemed a sensible arrangement in Vethmark; over two days it had become a matter she wished to discuss with me. Her ears were back and her weight was off the near hind, and she gave me a long look through the slats that I have had from her before and expect to have again.

"It is three days," I said. "Two, now."

She turned her head away and took a mouthful of hay, to show me it was not forgiveness.

Coming back down I stopped where the fireman was working. He had the tank spout swung over and had gone up on the tender to shift coal forward, and there was no wasted motion in any part of him. When he had it where he wanted it he came down and stood a moment in the cold with his forearms steaming.

"She drinks," I said.

"She does." He wiped his face with the flat of his wrist and left it dirtier than he found it. "Nine of these before Wainford. Ten if the grade's wet." He looked along the length of her as a man looks at a horse he has ridden far and is not yet done riding. "Water's the half of it. Everybody thinks it's the coal."

I said I had been thinking about the coal, and rather more about the grade. We stood and considered the engine together for a while in the companionable manner of two men who have nothing in common but a machine in front of them.

"You'll be a gentleman going out to the end of the line," he said at last. It was not quite a question and not quite an accusation.

"I'll be a wizard going out to the end of the line."

He took it better than most do. He looked at me sidelong, then back at the engine, and something went over his face that I have seen a hundred times and have never yet found a graceful way to head off.

"Could you make her not need it?" he said. "The water."

There it was. I had a sentence ready for this, worn smooth with use, and I gave it to him. "No. Not for what it would cost."

"Cost who?"

"Whoever paid me. Then the men who'd have to keep it fed after I'd gone home." I nodded up at the tank on its legs. "That fills itself out of a stream and a bit of weather. What I'd put in her would want a man's whole attention, and it would want it every day, and the first day it went without, you'd be standing here just the same. Only later, and further from a town."

He thought about it. He was not a quick man, but he was an honest one, and he stood there and turned it over where I could watch him do it, slowly, like a coin going across the knuckles.

"So there's no use in it," he said.

"There's use in it. There's no bargain in it, here." I put my hands in my pockets, because they were cold and because it was easier than saying the rest. The rest was that I could have done it, once, given a winter and a free hand and somebody else's money, and that I had spent three years not doing anything of the kind. "Shorten her grade by a foot in the hundred and you'd save more than I could."

That amused him rather more than it deserved. "Aye. Tell the company."

I have had that conversation, or a cousin of it, on a dozen platforms, and the answer never changes because the arithmetic never does. There are not enough of us. Not rare in the way people mean when they call a thing rare; there is no shortage of world to work on. There is a shortage of hands that can work it, a scattered handful across the whole Commonwealth, every one of them wanted in six places at once. Put one of those hands into an engine and you have bought an engine and lost a bridge, or a mine, or a harbour wall. Iron and fire and water never run out, and they never want tending by a man who took twenty years to make. That is the whole of why the country runs on coal and not on us, and there is no failure of anybody's imagination in it. It is a sum, and it comes out the same every time it is done honestly.

The whistle went. He was up the side of the tender like a man going up his own stairs. I walked back down past the horse-box, where Snow had her head over the door and her position unaltered, and got into the cold carriage, and we went on.

The line gave out, as such lines do, exactly where paying traffic did, at a raw and cheerful town called Wainford that once poured the country's grain and cattle onto river barges and now poured them into railway cars. There the rails stopped, and the country they had never troubled to reach began.

Wainford exists because a line stopped there, and it has the bad manners of a town that knows it. The whole place smelled of cattle and coal and wet grain. There were pens along the up side of the yard and a rank of wagons on the down side, and between the two a permanent argument was being conducted by some forty men, not one of whom appeared to be losing.

I took a bed for the night over a chophouse and spent what was left of the afternoon buying four days of road. That is a particular kind of shopping and I have always rather enjoyed it: oats, a wedge of hard cheese, twice as much bread as looks reasonable, a paper of tea. The man behind the counter had it all into a sack before I had finished asking for half of it.

"Going out west," he said.

"Ardenmoor."

He considered that with his lips pursed, as a man does over a name he has heard and never had occasion to use. "There's a road," he said, and I took that for the recommendation it was.

Behind me a drover was arguing with the counter about the price of a halter and losing on points. He broke off to tell me the road was sound as far as the second bridge and a matter of opinion after that; that if I came to a fork with a thorn tree standing in it I was to keep left; and that his brother had once done otherwise. Then he went back to the halter. Nobody in that town had time to be curious about a stranger for longer than the length of a sentence, and after three days in a carriage full of people determinedly not looking at me, I liked them for it.

I got Snow down from the horse-box, both of us glad to be quit of the noise, and went on the old way, by horse and road, four more days into the green silence.

The road to Ardenmoor had never been grand, and the last of it was humbler still: good enough packed dirt, kept carelessly and marked worse, the kind of country road that serves a place without troubling to announce it. Twice I took a fork on faith where no post stood to tell me otherwise and was proved right more by luck than judgment. The hedges had come into their first reckless leaf. The light lay long and yellow over low hills and older stone, and a stream went about its business off to my left with no interest in mine. I let Snow set the pace, which was slow, and did not correct her. She had carried me many miles and earned her opinions of the last few. It is a small courtesy to let a good animal be right about the road.

The second of those forks came on the third day, in the middle of a long afternoon, where the road divided evenly around a bank of gorse and went off in two directions with equal confidence. There was no post. There had been a post once. The stump of it stood in the grass with a nail in it and nothing hanging from the nail.

I had been told to look for a thorn tree. There was no thorn tree. There was gorse, and plenty of it, and away to the right a stand of something leafless that might have been thorn in a poor light to a man who wanted it badly enough. I found that I wanted it badly enough.

Snow did not. She had stopped square in the middle of the fork with her ears up and her weight even. In her, that is a whole paragraph.

"Right," I said.

She did not move.

"It has the wheel-ruts."

She turned her head and looked back down the road we had come up, at some length, to be sure I had taken in the alternative on offer.

There is a thing you learn riding alone, and I had learned it years before and had to learn it again about every third day of that journey. You can sit in the middle of a fork for a quarter of an hour weighing the evidence, and at the end of the quarter hour you will have exactly the evidence you began with and rather less daylight. So I looked at the ruts, which ran deeper on the right, and at the gorse, which told me nothing, and at the sky, going the colour of an old coin. Then I took the right-hand road, because carts had gone that way, and a road that carries carts is going somewhere carts want to go. The drover in Wainford had said keep left at the thorn. I had gone to some trouble to make that leafless stand into a thorn tree and then paid it no mind whatever, and I would like to tell you there was something in that beyond the ruts, and there was not.

An hour on, it brought me down to a ford, a farm, and a man mending a gate.

He straightened when he saw me and stood with the mallet hanging at the end of his arm, waiting. He was not unfriendly. He simply had no intention of speaking first to a man on a horse he did not know.

"Is this the Ardenmoor road?"

"It's a road to Ardenmoor," he said.

I have thought about that sentence since. He was not being clever. He was being exact, and exactness is a thing you meet more often past the end of a rail line than you might suppose. There were two roads. Both went there. One of them was a day longer and crossed better ground, and it was the one I had not taken.

"How far?"

"Day and a bit." He looked at Snow, then at me, and revised something. "Day, at that pace."

I asked whether there was anywhere on it to stop the night. He said there was, and told me, and then told me again with the turnings in a different order, and I thanked him and did not mention that I had stopped following after the second barn. Then he went back to his gate, and I went on, and the evening came down blue and cold over ground that had begun, without my noticing when, to fold itself into proper hills.

The place he meant turned out to be a farm with its gable to the road and a lamp burning in a window that faced nothing in particular. They put me in the hay barn for the price of my supper and asked me nothing whatever. Past the end of a rail line that is not indifference. It is a kind of manners. A man on that road is going somewhere, and if he wanted you to know where he would tell you. I ate at their table with four of them and a dog, and the talk was all of a bridge, and I contributed nothing to it and was not missed. In the morning there was tea, and the woman of the house looked at my coat and said it would rain before nine.

It rained before nine, as she had said it would, and went on until noon, and the country came out of it washed and steaming and smelling of green.

The village came into view all at once, as small places do, when the lane bent round an old oak. Ardenmoor lay below me in the last of the afternoon, and I will say plainly that it was ordinary: thatch and grey stone strung along a beck, a mill wheel turning without hurry, smoke standing straight above a dozen chimneys in windless air. Somewhere a dog was making its position known. Somewhere else a woman called two children in to supper by name, the second twice, with patience shortly to run out. I sat at the bend, felt the road go out of my shoulders, and was glad in a plain way to have arrived somewhere.

There was a hill to the north of the village and a little east of it, rounded and green and crowned with nothing but weather. *That will be the one*, I thought, and thought no more of it than that. It was a hill. I had seen a thousand hills. Whatever it had to say for itself, it was keeping to itself, and I was glad enough of the quiet.

I rode down into the village at a walk, and it looked me over the way a small village looks over a stranger, not unkindly but thoroughly, and then mostly went back to its evening. A hammer was still going somewhere among the houses, a smith at work past his hour, the ring of it carrying flat and clear across the cold. It had a shape to it that I have listened for in a hundred towns, and this one kept an even time, three light and one heavy, a man finishing a thing he had begun rather than beginning a thing he could have left. Where it came from, a door stood open on a square of red light with the black shapes of tools hung across it. Then the street bent and took it away from me.

Two boys sat on a wall and watched me the whole length of that wall without a word, turning their heads together as though they were geared to one another. A woman came out to take a cloth off a hedge, looked at me, looked at the light going, and went back in with the cloth. From a doorway a man said, "Down and left, at the beck," without my having asked him anything, and shut the door. Further on, a shop breathed out the smell of an herbalist's trade as I passed its door, cut green stems and something bitter-clean beneath it. Then it was behind me, and there was lamplight, and woodsmoke, and the good ordinary sound of a day being put away.

The inn stood where the street widened toward the beck, a broad, comfortable-looking building of three storeys, the only one in Ardenmoor to stand so tall, with a painted sign weathered past reading and lamplight spilling into the blue evening. I have stopped at a great many inns and could write a dull and honest book on the subject. This one had the one quality that matters and cannot be built on purpose: it looked like somewhere a tired man might stop being tired for a while. I got down, and my legs told me exactly how far we had come. A boy came from the inn-yard shadows to take Snow's head with the unhurried competence of a child who had been around horses since before he could walk.

He was perhaps nine. Thin, dark-haired, with a still, watchful way about him that was not shyness, though you might mistake it for that: the stillness of a child taking in more than he lets on. He looked at me with a directness most children lose by that age, or are taught out of, then took the reins and gave his whole attention to the mare, which she plainly preferred.

He was good with a horse, and for that first tired evening it was all I saw. It was enough.

"There'll be a man come to see me," I told him. "Cael. Do you know where he'd be found?"

Something crossed the boy's face, there and gone. "I can fetch him," he said.

He did not go at once. He took Snow's head and walked her through into the yard, and I went after with my saddlebags over my shoulder, because they were mine to carry and because I wanted to see how he did it.

The stable was two loose boxes and a standing stall, swept, and it smelled as any decent stable smells: hay, old leather, horse. He put her in the far box. That was the one out of the draught, and he did not ask me whether it was the one I wanted.

He had the girth undone before I got the second bag off the hook. He was not yet tall enough to swing the saddle off her as a grown man does, so he took its weight on the flat of both forearms coming down, and he neither staggered under it nor let the stirrup swing into her side. Then the bridle came off and the halter went on in one motion, and he stood a moment with his hand flat on her neck under the mane while she made up her mind about him.

She made it up quickly. She usually does not.

"She's warm," he said.

"Four days."

He nodded, as though that squared an account, and fetched a twist of hay and rubbed her down with it, chest and shoulders and along the back where the saddle had sat. He was thorough about it in a way most grown men are not. Then he looked at the bucket in the corner and did not fill it.

"She can have that in a while," he said.

I had said nothing at all. I had been standing in the door of that stable with my bags on my shoulder for some minutes, being no use to anybody, and in no hurry at all to stop.

He picked out her feet last, each one in turn, running his thumb round the frog and setting the foot down instead of dropping it. On the near fore he stopped and looked, and turned it a little toward what light there was.

"Stone bruise," he said. "Old one."

"She got it in the autumn."

"It's grown out." He set the foot down.

That was the whole of our conversation. He gave her a measure of oats and a slab of hay and shot the bolt, and checked the bolt, and went out past me into the dark at a trot. I had learned more about how he had been raised than an hour of talk would have told me.

Inside, the inn was warm and low-beamed and half full of the evening's regulars, and the woman behind the bar took one look at me and had the road, the errand, and the name before I crossed the room. Her name was Lira. She kept the inn the way a good captain keeps a ship, invisibly and completely. She was a spare, measured woman who gave a stranger nothing he had not paid for, neither warmth nor its opposite, and it was a good while before I understood that this was not coldness but economy. She kept her regard as she kept her good glasses and did not bring it out for just anyone.

She never once appeared to look up from the glass she was drying.

"Wainford road," she said.

"It is."

"Four days on it, by the boots." She put the glass on the shelf and took another off the rack. "You'll have come about the hill."

I said that I had.

"You'll be the wizard, then," she said, setting down a cup I had not asked for and immediately wanted. She said the word as another woman might have said *the man about the drains*: a trade, come to do a thing that wanted doing. I have been announced in grander rooms with more ceremony and less competence. "Room's held and paid, a fortnight since, by your man's man. Sit. Eat." A glance took the road off me in one pass. "You've the look of a man who's been eating out of his saddlebags."

I admitted that I had.

"Well," she said, as if that settled a question she had been privately turning over, and went to see about a plate.

Supper was a plain and honest business: barley soup with good bread and butter, then hard cheese and local small beer. I ate more than was strictly dignified.

The room saw to itself around me while I did it. There were perhaps a dozen of them in, and every one had come out of the same cold and sat down in the place he always sat, and the talk went over my head in the shorthand of people who have not had to explain themselves to one another in thirty years.

A man by the hearth had a ewe that had twinned and was not pleased about it. "Two," he said, "and neither of them worth the trouble of the other." A woman at the next table told him he said that every year. He said that every year it was true. That settled nothing and was not meant to.

Somebody's thatch wanted doing. That took a while. The thatch had wanted doing since before the wet spell, and the man who did thatch had a gable at the far end of the street to finish first, and there was an opinion in the room about whether the gable had needed doing at all, and then a second opinion about the first opinion. Nobody raised his voice. It had the sound of a subject they enjoyed and had no intention of concluding.

Behind me two men were settling a debt in eggs, out loud, at length, with corrections.

I sat in the middle of it with my bread and my beer and did not follow half of it, and that was the pleasure of the thing. I could not have told you who any of them were, or which of the two was owed the eggs. What I could tell was that not one soul in that room had needed to say a surname all evening. When the man with the ewe got up to go, three people wished him goodnight without turning round; the door let in a bar of cold; somebody said a thing about the door that everyone in the room had heard before, and they gave it the small worn laugh of a joke kept on purpose.

They looked at me too. I want to be fair about that. A stranger is a piece of news in a place that has not had any since the wet spell, and they took what they wanted from me over the tops of their cups: the coat, the boots, the fact that I ate like a man lately off a road. Once I heard the word *hill* two tables away, and then not again. That was manners. Once a small boy was sent past my elbow on an errand that did not require going past my elbow, looked at me the whole way, and went back to report.

Nobody spoke to me. I have been in rooms where that would have been an insult and rooms where it was a mercy, and this was neither. It was only that they had not decided yet, and saw no cause to hurry about it, and had thatch to discuss.

Lira went round the room twice while I ate and never once appeared to be going round the room. A jug came off the bar and reached the far table without anybody having asked for it. A stool was moved out of a path with her foot as she passed. At some point a coat that had been over the back of a chair was on a peg instead.

Lira did not watch, which I appreciated, and did not make talk, which I appreciated more. I had spent three years arranging my life so that no one would set a plate before me and want nothing back but that I eat it. A plain bowl of soup in a plain room undid a little of that. It was not the soup, or not only the soup, but the room. In the city a room full of people was a room full of strangers, each sealed in his own affairs and thankful to be left so. Here every soul knew every other, knew their people and troubles, whose ewe had twinned and whose thatch wanted doing. The low unshowy warmth of folk who had never needed to introduce themselves was a thing I had half forgotten was possible. I did not look closely at the feeling. Nine times in ten, one that came on so fast was grief approaching from a new direction. This was the tenth.

Cael came in on the tail of the meal, the cold still on his shoulders and the boy at his heel. He was not the man I had built out of Penworth's correspondence; I had made him older and grander in my head. He was solid, not young but a fair way from invalid, in a good plain coat, with a face that had done a great deal of weather and eyes that had lost none of their sharpness to it. He looked me over as one looks at a thing expected for months and found, on the whole, to be real. His glance caught on my face, as people's often did, and came up well short of my true count of years. I was past forty and have never worn it where a man can see. Whatever figure he settled on he kept to himself, which I liked him for.

"You'll be Emlyn," he said.

"I will," I said. "And you'll be Cael."

"Since a good while before you got here." His mouth moved toward a smile and did not quite spend it. He lowered himself onto the bench across from me with the care of a man who has come to a private arrangement with his knees, and the boy sat a little apart, near the fire, close enough to hear and far enough to be overlooked, which I would come to learn was his usual and preferred distance from the world. "Your man wrote me you'd come the first clear week of spring, and here's you, near enough to the day. Thorough sort, your man. On his account I've signed my name more times this winter than in the ten years before it."

"He's a great believer in signatures," I said. "He says a thing that isn't written down is only a rumor two men are having."

"Aye, that sounds like him." Cael took a cup from Lira with a nod that had a long friendship folded inside it. "Well. He'll have told you the shape of it. The hill, the land about it, and the price. The price is fair and settled, and most of it was never mine to haggle. What your man has been buying is dead men's fields, parish ground, other families' tired acres. I speak for the lot because somebody had to, I've been here long enough, and on the rare occasion they're held I lead our parish meetings. So we'll not bargain. That part's done." He looked at me over the cup. "I'll tell you what's not. A stranger nobody in the valley can place comes up the road and buys the better part of its idle ground in one hand. That sits ill with folk, and it ought to. So the village sent me to look at you, and here I am." He said it without heat, the way a man reports weather. "The fields want working, and they'll be glad of anyone who means to work them. The hill is the one piece I'd have kept back. It was ours, my family's, and I've had money in hand for it before now—slate men and tin men, coin as good as yours—and sent every one of them down the road. A hill is not a purse you turn out and pocket. It is a thing a man stands on, does something with, and answers for. If I sell it, I sell it to someone who means to make something of it, not carry it off in carts."

"Answers for it to whom?" I said.

He thought about that longer than the question deserved, or else exactly as long as it deserved and I had asked a better one than I knew.

"To whoever's here in twenty years," he said. "That's the whole of it. There's no court in it. There's the valley, and it has a long memory and no particular manners about the using of it." He moved his cup an inch on the table. "There's a wall down by the beck a man built badly before I was born. Nobody living ever met him. Everybody living knows whose wall it is."

"That seems hard."

"It's not hard. It's only long." He drank, and set the cup down. "You'd be surprised how few men can stand the length of it. They can stand being disliked well enough. It's the being remembered that undoes them."

I said I had come a fair way to get somewhere nobody remembered anything about me at all. He laughed at that, one short breath, and said that in that case I had chosen my valley badly.

"How long," I asked, "before a man stops being the stranger here?"

"Longer than you'll care for." He said it without any softening in it whatever, and then, because he was not a cruel man, he gave me the rest. "But it starts the first time you're wanted for something and you turn up. Not the first time you buy something. Anybody can buy something."

He turned his cup a slow quarter-turn and did not drink. "My grandmother loved that rise of ground past all sense. She went up in every weather to the year she died." He tipped his head toward the boy, who studied the fire and missed nothing. "I was younger than that one when she swore it to me. She said that on the right still evening, if you sat quiet on top, you could hear it."

"Hear what?" I asked.

"That's the thing," said Cael. "She'd never say. I don't think she rightly knew. I think she only knew it was worth the listening, and that most folk hadn't the patience, and that it would be a poor business to sell a listening hill to a man who'd never once in his life sat still." He looked at me then, plainly, an old man taking an honest measure. "You've the look of a man who's sat still. I don't say it as a kindness."

"I've had reason to," I said, which was as near as I came, in those years, to telling anyone anything.

He nodded, as if I had confirmed something, and let it lie, and did not ask the reason. I have thought since that the not-asking was the first true gift anyone in that valley gave me.

For a while after that he said nothing. He held the cup between his hands and looked into the fire, or past it, the way old men do when they are holding a thing up to the light of everyone no longer there to be asked, and I had the sense to let the silence be. The room went on around us, low talk and the settle of coals, while he turned some private matter over until it came out the shape he wanted. Then he drank one slow swallow and set the cup down with the air of a man who had signed something in his own head and did not mean to go back on it.

"Then here's what I'd say," he went on, brisker now, as if the matter were settled and only the arrangements remained. "It's full dark, and nothing worth buying ever looks like much at dusk. Take your room. Sleep. Walk up in the morning, when there's light to show you the truth of the place, and stand on it before you decide. If you still want it by noon, we'll find Penworth's man and make an honest tangle of paperwork out of it. Then I'll have sold my grandmother's hill to someone I suspect she'd have approved of, which is the most I've hoped for from this business." He got to his feet by the same slow arrangement. "A man ought to look at a hill before he owns it. That's all the wisdom I've got, but I'm fairly sure of it."

I said that suited me. "Soren," Cael said, the one word and nothing after it, and the boy rose from the fire. At the door he stopped and turned and gave me that level, unhurried look once more, as if there were a question he had decided not to ask yet but did not mean to forget. Then they went out into the dark together, the old man and the boy, and I heard the stick of the one and the quick step of the other fade off up the lane, and something in the sound of it sat oddly and warmly in my chest, and I did not, that night, know what to call it.

The room had heard the whole of that, or as much of it as mattered, and had the courtesy to go on with its own business throughout. Now it let a little of itself show. Somebody by the hearth said, to nobody in particular, that Cael had not been down of an evening since the turn of the year. Somebody else said the roads had been bad. A third voice observed that the roads were always bad, and that closed the subject as a lid closes a box.

My cup had been filled at some point during the conversation. I had not seen it done and could not have said when.

Lira gave me a candle and showed me to a good room under the eaves, the best she kept by the look of it: a proper bed with clean linen, a chair, a washstand with water already set out, and a broad window on the north side of the village. She wished me good night with the flat, practiced ease of a woman who had wished ten thousand of them and went back to her work. I shut the door and was, for the first time since the far end of the afternoon, alone.

It was a better room than I had any right to expect at the end of that road, and I stood in the middle of it a while and let it be one. The ceiling came down with the roof on the two long sides, so that a man of my height could stand upright in a strip through the middle and nowhere else, and the strip ran to the window. Whoever cut that dormer had cut it generously, and square, and had known enough to set the sill low. It is not a common thing. Most men putting a window into a roof put it where the rafters make it easy and let the man inside look at the sky.

I stood looking at the framing rather longer than a guest normally looks at framing. My father would have read that ceiling in a minute and told you what the man had been thinking while he cut it. I got as far as noticing that the collar ties were pegged and not nailed, and that somebody had cared, and left it there.

The washstand had a china basin and a jug already filled and a cloth folded over the rim of the jug. The bed was made hard and flat and the linen had been out in a wind. There was a chair, a candle, a peg on the back of the door, and a strip of rag rug on the boards for a man's feet in the morning. Nothing in the room was new. Everything in it had been chosen once by somebody sensible and then kept.

I set my bags down under the window and did not take my coat off. Then I put the candle out.

I did not sleep at once. I sat on the edge of the strange bed in the dark with my coat still on and after a while took a letter out of it, not the three-page marvel from Penworth that had carried me here, but the one I had carried far longer and never needed to open twice, because I knew every word already. It had come three weeks late, in another spring, and told me a thing I had been too far from home to prevent. I did not open it. I only held it, which was a habit I had fallen into and am not proud of, and looked out the window to where the hill stood as a darker shape against the dark, keeping whatever it kept. I thought, with no more weight on the thought than that, *I will look at you in the morning, and then I will decide.*

The boards gave a small complaint beneath my boots. A moment later the water in the basin touched the china, once, though nothing in the room had moved. I watched it settle. An inn is an old animal, and timber shifts in the cold. I had slept under enough strange roofs not to take every creak for a message.

So I got my boots off at last and set them where I would fall over them in the morning, that being where I have always set them and where I have fallen over them in a good many strange rooms. It is a poor arrangement. I have had twenty years to improve it and have not troubled.

I put the letter away and lay down under a strange roof, in a valley I had never meant to stay in, with the inn settling below me and a boy's quick step somehow still in my ears. I slept better than I had in a long while.

Morning came clear and crisp under a pale sky the scrubbed blue of fresh-washed linen. I woke before I meant to and listened to the village wake around me—the beck, the first cart, a rooster with strong feelings and no sense of pitch—then went down, where Lira set porridge, honey, and a slab of bacon before me and left me to eat in peace.

The taproom at that hour was a different room. The fire was new and small, the shutters were back, and the light came in low and level across the boards and showed up every scar in them. A carter had come and gone before me and left his plate. Somewhere under the floor a barrel was being moved with more thoroughness than tenderness.

Lira went past twice with her sleeves turned back, and the second time a further slab of bacon arrived on my plate without her breaking stride. I did not remark on it. I could see she would rather I did not.

"You'll be going up, then," she said, when I had done. It was not quite a question.

"I'll be going up."

She took that without any sign of having received it. Then she stopped, with the cloth in her hand.

"There's no road up it."

"Up the hill?"

"There's a track from the last house. It isn't a road. It'll be wet to the knee this month."

"Then I'll be wet to the knee."

"You will." She moved along the bar. "There's bread going into the oven up the lane this morning. If you want any of it, you'll want it now and not on your way back."

She wiped the same clean patch of bar she had been wiping when I came down, a thing she did with her hands while she decided whether a thing was worth the saying. Mostly, with me, she decided it was not. "Mind how you go," she said when I rose, which from her I had already learned to take for a whole speech, and she took my bowl away, and did not ask what I hoped to find up on that hill, and did not offer me her opinion of what I would, and I liked her the better for both.

I went out through the yard first. Snow had her head over the box door before I was halfway across it, and she watched me come the whole way with the particular attention of a horse who has worked out that a coat at that hour means somewhere she is not being taken.

Her bucket was full. Somebody had filled it after all, in the night or before light. There was fresh hay in the rack and the muck had been taken out and the bolt was shot and checked.

"Not today," I told her.

She put her nose against my chest, hard, and then swung her quarters over and presented me with the whole broad grey side of her disapproval. She had four days of road behind her, a box out of the draught, and a full rack, and none of that entered into the question.

I gave her the crust I had brought out for exactly this purpose, and she forgave me for as long as it lasted, and I went out of the yard into the lane.

I walked up through the waking village with the sun not yet over the eastern rise, and it went about its ordinary morning business around me, which is a thing I have always loved to watch. Near the inn a great domed oven of clay and fieldstone stood open to the lane, hot already, and a broad woman drew out the first loaves on a long wooden peel while households waited their turn with dough on boards and children waited on nothing but the smell. New bread and woodsmoke went the length of the street ahead of me and did more to recommend the place than anything the evening had shown.

I stopped, because not stopping for that would have taken a discipline I have never owned, and stood at a polite distance to watch the peel go in and come out.

The woman at the oven took my measure over her shoulder in one pass, as the whole village had been doing since the evening before, and went on with her work. She had four in and three out and a boy at her elbow whose entire office was to hold the board and not drop it.

"You'll be stopping at Lira's," she said, to the oven.

I said I was.

"Then you'll not have had this." She came round with a loaf still too hot to be carried the way she was carrying it, turned it over once in her hands, and put it against my chest so that I had to take it or wear it. It was dark, and dense, and heavier than it had any business being, and it came through my coat like a warm brick.

"I've nothing on me to pay you with."

"Did I ask you?" She was already going back for the peel. "It keeps a week. Longer, if you're not particular. Don't cut it warm."

I stood in the lane holding it. Behind her the boy with the board was watching me with frank interest, and one of the women waiting her turn said something to another woman that I did not catch and was not altogether meant to, and both of them laughed. The oven went on breathing out over the whole street.

So I went on up the street with a hot loaf under my arm and my coat smelling of it. There are worse ways to set out to look at a piece of ground.

Further along a dairymaid came the other way under two swinging pails, stepping wide of a man patching a gable with last year's reed, and a woman drove three geese ahead of her with a switch and a running commentary they ignored.

She had got as far as informing them that they were a disgrace to their mother when she caught sight of me, and she changed her audience without changing her tone.

"You'll be him, then."

"I expect I will."

"Mind the middle one." The middle goose had stopped dead in the lane and was regarding me with the flat contempt geese keep in reserve for the well dressed. "She's no respect for anybody."

I stood aside. The middle one came past me at her own pace and in her own time, with her neck out and her opinion fully formed, and the woman went after her with the switch and the commentary.

"Is it true you're for the hill?" she called back, from four houses along.

I said it was.

"Well," she said, in the tone of a woman entering a figure in a column, and went on up the street. I never did learn which way she had entered it.

A hammer started somewhere behind me, a smith making an unhurried beginning on his day. At the mill, a broad figure white to the elbows with flour stood among stacks of drying timber and watched me take the lane past the last houses, lifting two fingers from a barrel in what I chose to take for encouragement.

The mill had the beck to itself down there, and the wheel went round with the grinding patience of a thing that has never once been in a hurry and never once stopped. Sawn timber stood in ranks to dry, spaced with sticks between the courses, and the smell of cut wood came off the stacks even in that cold. Somebody had swept the yard. I have a weakness for a swept yard. It tells you somebody there is either careful or short of work, and either of those you can work with.

I did not stop, and nothing in the two fingers on the barrel suggested I was expected to. We had between us all the understanding two people can have at forty paces on a cold morning: each had seen the other, and neither meant to make anything of it yet.

Then the houses were behind me, and the lane became a track, and the track became a path worn faint into the turf by feet that had climbed it for longer than anyone living could account for, and the path ran up through the wet spring grass to the foot of the hill.

I stopped there, at the bottom of it, and stood, and looked up the long green shoulder of the thing to where it met the pale morning sky. It was, that first morning, only an ordinary hill: green, and silent, and crowned with nothing but weather and the thin gold of the new light. It was the last time it would ever be only that, and I am glad, now, that I did not know it.

Then I started up.
