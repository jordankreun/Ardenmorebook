# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter One — Ardenmoor

I came to Ardenmoor at the far end of a long road, in the third week of spring, on a good horse who had decided some hours back that I did not know where I was going.

She was not altogether wrong, though the fault was not entirely mine. I knew where I was going the way a man knows a thing he has been told rather than a thing he has seen. There was a hill for sale above a village of that name, and a fair breadth of the ground around it, and I had come to look at what I had very nearly already bought — because Penworth had told me to, and in the matter of what I ought to want I had long since stopped trusting my own judgment over his.

Penworth was my man of affairs, and had been for more years than I mean to reckon up here. He was a verbose and formal and exceedingly precise old gentleman out of Vethmark who managed the whole apparatus of my life that I could not be troubled to manage myself — the letters, the monies, the small steady mountain of paper that any working life throws off whether a man attends to it or not. He had a particular genius, honed over thirty years, for finding me things before I knew I wanted them, and for quietly buying the ground beneath a thing while I was still deciding whether I wanted the thing at all. His letter had reached me three weeks and a good many miles behind, and I had it still, folded twice and gone soft at the creases from a season in my coat. It was a small marvel of its kind: three close pages to tell me that he had come upon a piece of country he believed would suit me — *a hill of no agricultural worth and considerable quiet* — that he had taken the liberty of provisionally securing it and a good deal of the land about it against my arrival, and that he would count it a personal kindness if I would go and look at what I had nearly purchased before he made me finish the purchasing.

I have said the truth is plainer than the tellers would like it, and it is. I did not come to Ardenmoor drawn by any thread of fate. I came because Penworth had an unerring eye and I had learned to trust it above my own; because it was spring; and because there was nowhere else in the wide world I would rather have been, which in those days was less a boast than a small and sorry fact about the size my life had shrunk to. *A hill of no worth and considerable quiet.* When I read that, it sounded like very nearly the only thing left that I wanted.

The last of the road did not so much end as give up on itself — good packed dirt softening to a green lane a cart might trouble twice in a month, until near the end I was riding through grass with only the ghost of a track beneath it. The hedges had come into that first reckless leaf that has no caution left in it. The light lay long and yellow over a country of low hills and older stone, and a stream went about its business off to my left without the least interest in mine. I let Snow set the pace, which was slow, and did not correct her. She had carried me a great many miles and had earned her opinions of the last few, and it is a small courtesy, letting a good animal be right about the road. I have found it repays a man more honestly than most of the larger courtesies do.

The village came into view all at once, the way small places do, as the lane bent round the shoulder of an old oak. Ardenmoor lay below me in the last of the afternoon, and I want to say plainly that it was an ordinary place — a scatter of thatch and grey stone strung along a beck, a mill wheel turning without hurry at the near end of it, smoke standing straight up from a dozen chimneys into windless air. Somewhere a dog was making its position known. Somewhere else a woman called two children in to supper by name, and the second name twice, with the particular patience that means it is very shortly going to run out. I sat at the bend a while and looked at it, and felt the road go out of my shoulders, and was glad in a low and uncomplicated way simply to have arrived somewhere.

There was a hill to the north of the village and a little east of it, rounded and green and crowned with nothing but weather. I marked it the way you mark any rise of ground you have ridden a long way to look at — *that will be the one* — and thought no more of it than that. It was a hill. I had seen a thousand hills. Whatever else it might have had to say for itself, it kept the saying for later, and I have been grateful since that it did. I do not think I could have borne, that first tired evening, to be met by all of it at once.

I rode down into the village at a walk, and the village looked back at me the way small villages do — not unkindly, but thoroughly, filing me away against later. A big man stood at the top of the street outside a smithy, a fellow with a burn-scarred forearm and a careful face, and he left off whatever he was about and gave me a nod that was more question than greeting. I would come to know him well; his name was Tomas, and there was a great deal more to him than a smith, though neither of us had any reason to suspect it that evening. Further down, a low stone house had bunches of drying green hung in its window and a smell coming off its doorstep of cut stems and something bitter-clean beneath, the smell of a place people go to when they are frightened for someone they love. A woman stood in the doorway of it with her arms folded, watching me pass. She did not nod. She only looked, and went on looking after I had gone by, and I felt the look between my shoulder blades for a good ten strides. That was Brenna. In time she would be one of the truest friends I ever made, and she would tell me, years on and laughing, that she had disliked me thoroughly on sight and been entirely right to.

The inn stood where the street widened toward the beck — a broad, low, comfortable-looking building with a painted sign gone too weathered to read and a good deal of lamplight already spilling out of its windows into the blue evening. I have stopped at a great many inns; I could write a dull and honest book on the subject. This one, before I had so much as got down off my horse, had the one quality that matters and cannot be built on purpose: it looked like somewhere a tired person might stop being tired for a while. I got down, and my legs told me at length exactly how far we had come, and a boy appeared out of the inn-yard shadows to take Snow's head with the unhurried competence of a child who has been around horses since before he could walk.

He was perhaps nine. Thin, dark-haired, with a still and watchful way about him that was not shyness, though it might be mistaken for it — the stillness of a child who is taking in a great deal more than he lets on. He looked at me for a moment with a directness most children lose by that age, or are taught out of, and then took the reins and turned his whole attention to the mare, which she plainly preferred.

I will not pretend I knew, in that moment, what I was looking at. That is a thing the tellers add afterward, and I have let them, because it makes the shape of the story cleaner and because in a way that has nothing to do with time it is even true. But standing in that inn-yard with the ache of the road in my knees, I saw only a competent, quiet boy who was good with a horse. It would be a season and more before I understood that the child holding my mare's head was Cael's grandson, and longer still before I understood the rest — what he was, and what he would become, and how much of everything that came after would turn, in the end, on him. His name was Soren. Of all the things I found on that hill and in that valley — all the workings and the wonders and the long strange conversation with the ground itself — he is the one I would not trade for any of the others, nor for all of them together. But I am getting ahead of myself. That is the trouble with knowing the ending. You keep wanting to give it away.

"There'll be a man come to see me," I told him. "Cael. Do you know where he'd be found?"

Something crossed the boy's face — I did not read it then; I have read it a hundred ways since. "I can fetch him," he said, and that was all, and he saw the mare stabled and fed with quick sure hands before he went, which told me more about how he had been raised than an hour of talk.

Inside, the inn was warm and low-beamed and half-full of the evening's regulars, and the woman behind the bar took one look at me and knew the whole of it — the road, the errand, the name — before I had crossed the room. Her name was Lira. She kept that inn the way a good captain keeps a ship, which is to say invisibly and completely, and she had been expecting me the better part of a week. She was, I would learn, a woman of few words and no wasted motion, and every one of the words, when it came, was a kindness with its sleeves rolled up.

"You'll be the wizard, then," she said, not loudly, setting in front of me a cup I had not asked for and immediately wanted. She said the word the way another woman might have said *the man about the drains* — a trade like any other, come to do a thing that wanted doing. I have been announced in throne rooms with a great deal more ceremony and a great deal less welcome. "Room's held and paid, a fortnight since, by your man's man. Sit. Eat." A glance took the whole of the road off me in one stroke. "Cael's sent for — that'll be young Soren up the lane now, you'll not catch him. You've the look of someone been eating out of his saddlebags a week."

I confessed that I had.

"Well," she said, as though that settled a question she had been privately turning over, and went to see about a plate.

The supper, when it came, was a plain and honest thing — a barley soup with good bread and a crock of butter, and after it a piece of hard cheese and a cup of the local small beer — and I ate more of it than was strictly dignified, while Lira watched me do it with the quiet satisfaction of her kind. I had spent three years arranging my life so that no one would set food in front of me and expect nothing back but that I eat it, and I had got good at the arranging, and here it was undone by a woman with a soup pot before I had my boots off. I did not examine the feeling too closely. I had learned to be careful of feelings that came on that suddenly. They were generally grief wearing another coat.

Cael came in on the tail of the meal, on a stick, with the cold of the evening still on his shoulders and his grandson at his heel. He was not the man I had built out of the winter's correspondence; I had made him older and grander in my head, and he was only old, in a good plain coat, with a face that had done a great deal of weather in its time and a pair of eyes that had lost none of their sharpness to it. He looked me over the way you look at a thing you have been expecting for some months and are pleased, on the whole, to find is real.

"You'll be Emlyn," he said.

"I will," I said. "And you'll be Cael."

"Since a good while before you got here." His mouth moved toward a smile and did not quite spend it. He lowered himself onto the bench across from me with the care of a man who has made a private treaty with his knees, and the boy Soren sat a little apart, near the fire, close enough to hear and far enough to be overlooked — a distance I would learn was his usual and preferred one from the world. "Your man wrote me you'd come the first clear week of spring, and here's the first clear week, and here's you, near enough to the day. Thorough sort, your man. His agent was thorougher. Between the pair of them I have signed my name more times this winter than in the ten years before it."

"He's a great believer in signatures," I said. "He says a thing that isn't written down is only a rumor two men are having."

"Aye, that sounds like him." Cael took a cup from Lira with a nod that had a long friendship folded up inside it. "Well. He'll have told you the shape of it. The hill, and the land about it, and the price, which is fair — I'll not haggle a fair price, I'm too old to enjoy it. But I told his agent, and I'll tell you to your face: I'd not sell that particular hill to just any man who came with the money in his hand." He turned his cup a slow quarter-turn on the table and did not drink from it. "My grandmother loved that rise of ground past all sense. Past all sense. She'd go up it in every weather to the year she died, and she swore to me — I was younger than that one there —" a small tip of the head toward Soren, who was studying the fire and missing nothing "— she swore that on the right sort of still evening, if you sat quiet on the top of it, you could hear it."

"Hear what?" I asked.

"That's the thing," said Cael. "She'd never say. I don't think she rightly knew. I think she only knew that it was worth the listening, and that most folk hadn't the patience, and that it would be a poor business to sell a listening hill to a man who'd never once in his life sat still." He looked at me then, plainly, an old man taking an honest measure. "You've the look of a man who's sat still. I don't say it as a kindness."

"I've had reason to," I said, which was as near as I came, in those years, to telling anyone anything.

He nodded, as though I had confirmed something, and let it be, and did not ask the reason — and I have thought since that the not-asking was the first true gift anyone in that valley gave me. "Then here's what I'd say," he went on, brisker now, the matter of my soul apparently concluded to his satisfaction. "It's full dark, and nothing worth buying ever looks like much at dusk. Take your room. Sleep. Walk up in the morning, when there's light to show you the truth of the place, and stand on it a while before you decide anything. If you still want it by noon, we'll go find Penworth's man and make an honest tangle of paperwork out of it, and I'll have sold my grandmother's hill to someone she'd have approved of, which is the most I've hoped for out of this business from the start." He got to his feet again by the same slow treaty. "A man ought to look at a hill before he owns it. That's all the wisdom I've got, but I'm fairly sure of it."

I said that suited me. He gathered his grandson with a look, and the boy rose, and at the door Soren stopped and turned and regarded me once more with that level, unhurried attention, as though there were a question he had decided not to ask yet but did not mean to forget. Then they went out into the dark together, the old man and the boy, and I heard the stick of the one and the quick step of the other fade off up the lane, and something in the sound of it sat oddly and warmly in my chest, and I did not, that night, know what to call it.

Lira gave me a candle and a small room under the eaves with a bed and a stool and a window, and wished me a good night with the ease of a woman who had wished ten thousand of them and meant every one. I climbed the narrow stair and shut the door and was, for the first time since the far end of the afternoon, alone.

I did not sleep at once. I sat on the edge of the strange bed in the dark with my coat still on and, after a while, took from inside it a letter — not the three-page marvel from Penworth that had carried me here, but the one I had carried a great deal longer, and had never once had to open, because I knew every word of it already. It had come three weeks late, in another spring than this one, and it had told me a thing I had been too far from home to prevent. I did not open it now. I only held it, which was a habit I had fallen into and am not proud of, and I looked out the small window to where the hill stood as a darker shape against the dark, keeping whatever it kept, and I thought — with no more weight on the thought than that — *I will look at you in the morning, and then I will decide.*

I put the letter away, and lay down at last in a stranger's house, in a valley I had never meant to stay in, with the small sounds of the inn settling below me and a boy's quick step somehow still in my ears, and I slept better than I had slept in a long, long while.

Morning came clear and cold and washed, the way the best of spring mornings do, under a sky the pale scrubbed blue of something just laundered. I woke before I meant to and lay a while listening to the village wake around me — the beck, the first cart, a rooster with strong feelings and no sense of pitch — and then I went down, and Lira, who I do not believe slept at all, put porridge and honey and a slab of bacon before me and stood over me until it was gone.

"You'll be going up, then," she said. It was not quite a question.

"I'll be going up."

She wiped the same clean patch of bar she'd been wiping when I came down — a thing she did with her hands, I would learn, while she made up her mind whether to say a thing with her mouth. "My gran knew Cael's gran," she said at last. "Girls together. She held the same as he does, about the hill. The hearing of it." A glance, and something careful in it. "I always took it for old women's talk. Only Cael's no old woman, and he doesn't fancy things." She put the cloth away, done. "Mind how you go. And come back and tell me if it's true — I've wanted to know my whole life and never once had the nerve to go up and find out for myself."

I told her I would. It was the first promise I made in that valley, and I kept it — though not for a good while, and not in any way either of us could have guessed that morning.

I walked up through the waking village with the sun not yet over the eastern rise, and the place showed me its ordinary self going about its ordinary business, which is a thing I have always loved to watch and never tired of. Tomas the smith was already at his fire, and the ring of his hammer came steady and unhurried across the cold air — a sound I did not yet know I would come to measure my days by. A woman drove three geese ahead of her with a switch and a running commentary. Brenna was on her knees in the little walled garden beside her house, doing something exacting to a bed of low grey plants, and she looked up as I passed and held my eye a moment over the wall — still not friendly, but not quite so cold as the evening before, as though she had spent the night revising her first opinion downward from *enemy* to merely *nuisance*, which in Brenna was a considerable warming. At the mill, the miller — a broad, floury, taciturn soul named Maret, who spoke perhaps four words to me that morning and would one day trust me with a great deal more than words — stood among stacks of drying timber in the yard and watched me take the lane past the last of the houses and out toward the open ground, and lifted two fingers off the head of a barrel in what I chose to take for encouragement.

And then the houses were behind me, and the lane became a track, and the track became a path worn faint into the turf by feet that had climbed it for longer than anyone living could account for, and the path ran up through the wet spring grass to the foot of the hill.

I stopped there, at the bottom of it, and stood, and looked up the long green shoulder of the thing to where it met the pale morning sky. It was — I will say it once more, because it was the last time it would ever be *only* this, and I want the record honest — an ordinary hill on an ordinary morning: green, and silent, and crowned with nothing but weather and the thin gold of the new light.

Then I started up.
