# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter One: Ardenmoor

I came to Ardenmoor at the far end of a long road, in the third week of spring, on a good horse who had decided some hours back that I did not know where I was going.

She was not altogether wrong, though the fault was not entirely mine. I knew where I was going the way a man knows a thing he has been told rather than a thing he has seen. There was a hill for sale above a village of that name, and a fair breadth of the ground around it, and I had come to look at what I had very nearly already bought, because Penworth had told me to, and in the matter of what I ought to want I had long since stopped trusting my own judgment over his.

Penworth was my man of affairs, and had had the running of the whole tangle of my life for something over a decade by then. He had the keeping of the letters, the monies, the small steady mountain of paper that any working life throws off whether a man attends to it or not. He was a spare, formal, exceedingly precise gentleman out of Vethmark, of about my own age though a good deal tidier about the wearing of it, and he had a particular genius, honed across a long career spent in the disorder of other men's affairs, for finding me things before I knew I wanted them, and for quietly buying the ground beneath a thing while I was still deciding whether I wanted the thing at all. His letter had reached me three weeks and a good many miles behind, and I had it still, folded twice and gone soft at the creases from a season in my coat. It was a small marvel of its kind: three close pages to tell me that he had come upon a piece of country he believed would suit me, *a hill of no agricultural worth and considerable quiet*; that he had taken the liberty of provisionally securing it, together with a good breadth of the workable land about it, the sort a man could set to honest tenants and thereafter forget he owned; and that he would count it a personal kindness if I would go and look at what I had nearly purchased before he made me finish the purchasing.

I have said the truth is plainer than the tellers would like it, and it is. I did not come to Ardenmoor drawn by any thread of fate. I came because Penworth had an unerring eye and I had learned to trust it above my own; because it was spring; and because there was nowhere else in the wide world I would rather have been, which in those days was less a boast than a small and sorry fact about the size my life had shrunk to. *A hill of no worth and considerable quiet.* When I read that, it sounded like very nearly the only thing left that I wanted.

The last of the road did not so much end as give up on itself. Good packed dirt softened to a green lane a cart might trouble twice in a month, until near the end I was riding through grass with only the ghost of a track beneath it. The hedges had come into that first reckless leaf that has no caution left in it. The light lay long and yellow over a country of low hills and older stone, and a stream went about its business off to my left without the least interest in mine. I let Snow set the pace, which was slow, and did not correct her. She had carried me a great many miles and had earned her opinions of the last few, and it is a small courtesy, letting a good animal be right about the road. I have found it repays a man more honestly than most of the larger courtesies do.

The village came into view all at once, the way small places do, as the lane bent round the shoulder of an old oak. Ardenmoor lay below me in the last of the afternoon, and I want to say plainly that it was an ordinary place. A scatter of thatch and grey stone strung along a beck, a mill wheel turning without hurry at the near end of it, smoke standing straight up from a dozen chimneys into windless air. Somewhere a dog was making its position known. Somewhere else a woman called two children in to supper by name, and the second name twice, with the particular patience that means it is very shortly going to run out. I sat at the bend a while and looked at it, and felt the road go out of my shoulders, and was glad in a low and uncomplicated way simply to have arrived somewhere.

There was a hill to the north of the village and a little east of it, rounded and green and crowned with nothing but weather. I marked it the way you mark any rise of ground you have ridden a long way to look at. *That will be the one*, I thought, and thought no more of it than that. It was a hill. I had seen a thousand hills. Whatever else it might have had to say for itself, it kept the saying for later, and I have been grateful since that it did. I do not think I could have borne, that first tired evening, to be met by all of it at once.

I rode down into the village at a walk, and it looked me over the way small villages look over a stranger, not unkindly but thoroughly, and for the most part went on with its evening. A hammer was still going somewhere back among the houses, some smith at work past his hour, the ring of it carrying flat and clear across the cold. Further on, a shop breathed out the smell of an herbalist's trade as I passed its door, cut green stems and something bitter-clean beneath them, the smell of a place people go to when they are frightened for someone they love, and then it was behind me. There was lamplight, and woodsmoke, and the good ordinary sound of a day being put away. I did not meet anyone that night, or learn a single name, and that was as it should be. A village does not hand you its people all at once. You come to know them slowly, a little at a time, the way you come to know anything worth the knowing.

The inn stood where the street widened toward the beck. A broad, low, comfortable-looking building with a painted sign gone too weathered to read and a good deal of lamplight already spilling out of its windows into the blue evening. I have stopped at a great many inns; I could write a dull and honest book on the subject. This one, before I had so much as got down off my horse, had the one quality that matters and cannot be built on purpose: it looked like somewhere a tired person might stop being tired for a while. I got down, and my legs told me at length exactly how far we had come, and a boy appeared out of the inn-yard shadows to take Snow's head with the unhurried competence of a child who has been around horses since before he could walk.

He was perhaps nine. Thin, dark-haired, with a still and watchful way about him that was not shyness, though it might be mistaken for it. It was the stillness of a child who is taking in a great deal more than he lets on. He looked at me for a moment with a directness most children lose by that age, or are taught out of, and then took the reins and turned his whole attention to the mare, which she plainly preferred.

I will not pretend I knew, in that moment, what I was looking at. He was a boy who was good with a horse, and for that first tired evening that was the whole of what I saw, and it was enough.

"There'll be a man come to see me," I told him. "Cael. Do you know where he'd be found?"

Something crossed the boy's face. I did not read it then; I have read it a hundred ways since. "I can fetch him," he said, and that was all, and he saw the mare stabled and fed with quick sure hands before he went, which told me more about how he had been raised than an hour of talk.

Inside, the inn was warm and low-beamed and half-full of the evening's regulars, and the woman behind the bar took one look at me and knew the whole of it before I had crossed the room, the road and the errand and the name. Her name was Lira. She kept that inn the way a good captain keeps a ship, which is to say invisibly and completely, and she had been expecting me the better part of a week. She was a spare, measured woman who gave a stranger nothing he had not paid for, neither warmth nor its opposite, and it was a good while before I understood that this was not coldness in her but economy. She kept her regard the way she kept her good glasses, and did not bring it out for just anyone.

"You'll be the wizard, then," she said, not loudly, setting in front of me a cup I had not asked for and immediately wanted. She said the word the way another woman might have said *the man about the drains*. A trade like any other, come to do a thing that wanted doing. I have been announced in grander rooms with a great deal more ceremony and a great deal less competence. "Room's held and paid, a fortnight since, by your man's man. Sit. Eat." A glance took the whole of the road off me in one stroke. "You've the look of a man who's been eating out of his saddlebags a week."

I confessed that I had.

"Well," she said, as though that settled a question she had been privately turning over, and went to see about a plate.

The supper, when it came, was a plain and honest thing: a barley soup with good bread and a crock of butter, and after it a piece of hard cheese and a cup of the local small beer. I ate more of it than was strictly dignified. Lira did not watch me do it, which I was grateful for, and did not make talk, which I was more grateful for still. I had spent three years arranging my life so that no one would set a plate in front of me and expect nothing back but that I eat it, and I had got good at the arranging, and a plain bowl of soup in a plain room undid a little of the arrangement before I had my boots off. I did not examine the feeling too closely. I had learned to be careful of feelings that came on that suddenly. They were generally grief wearing another coat.

Cael came in on the tail of the meal, on a stick, with the cold of the evening still on his shoulders and his grandson at his heel. He was not the man I had built out of the winter's correspondence; I had made him older and grander in my head, and he was only old, in a good plain coat, with a face that had done a great deal of weather in its time and a pair of eyes that had lost none of their sharpness to it. He looked me over the way you look at a thing you have been expecting for some months and are pleased, on the whole, to find is real. His eyes caught a moment on my face, as people's often did, running the sum of my years and coming up a good way short; for I was past forty by then and have never worn it where a man can see, for reasons that belong further along in this account than they do here. Whatever figure he settled on, he kept to himself, which I liked him for.

"You'll be Emlyn," he said.

"I will," I said. "And you'll be Cael."

"Since a good while before you got here." His mouth moved toward a smile and did not quite spend it. He lowered himself onto the bench across from me with the care of a man who has made a private treaty with his knees, and the boy sat a little apart, near the fire, close enough to hear and far enough to be overlooked, which I would come to know was his usual and preferred distance from the world. "Your man wrote me you'd come the first clear week of spring, and here's the first clear week, and here's you, near enough to the day. Thorough sort, your man. His agent was thorougher. Between the pair of them I have signed my name more times this winter than in the ten years before it."

"He's a great believer in signatures," I said. "He says a thing that isn't written down is only a rumor two men are having."

"Aye, that sounds like him." Cael took a cup from Lira with a nod that had a long friendship folded up inside it. "Well. He'll have told you the shape of it. The hill, and the land about it, and the price, which is fair. I'll not haggle a fair price, I'm too old to enjoy it. But I told his agent, and I'll tell you to your face: I'd not sell that particular hill to just any man who came with the money in his hand." He turned his cup a slow quarter-turn on the table and did not drink from it. "My grandmother loved that rise of ground past all sense. Past all sense. She'd go up it in every weather to the year she died." He gave a small tip of the head toward the boy, who was studying the fire and missing nothing. "I was younger than that one there when she swore it to me. She swore that on the right sort of still evening, if you sat quiet on the top of it, you could hear it."

"Hear what?" I asked.

"That's the thing," said Cael. "She'd never say. I don't think she rightly knew. I think she only knew that it was worth the listening, and that most folk hadn't the patience, and that it would be a poor business to sell a listening hill to a man who'd never once in his life sat still." He looked at me then, plainly, an old man taking an honest measure. "You've the look of a man who's sat still. I don't say it as a kindness."

"I've had reason to," I said, which was as near as I came, in those years, to telling anyone anything.

He nodded, as though I had confirmed something, and let it be, and did not ask the reason. I have thought since that the not-asking was the first true gift anyone in that valley gave me. "Then here's what I'd say," he went on, brisker now, the matter of my soul apparently concluded to his satisfaction. "It's full dark, and nothing worth buying ever looks like much at dusk. Take your room. Sleep. Walk up in the morning, when there's light to show you the truth of the place, and stand on it a while before you decide anything. If you still want it by noon, we'll go find Penworth's man and make an honest tangle of paperwork out of it, and I'll have sold my grandmother's hill to someone she'd have approved of, which is the most I've hoped for out of this business from the start." He got to his feet again by the same slow treaty. "A man ought to look at a hill before he owns it. That's all the wisdom I've got, but I'm fairly sure of it."

I said that suited me. "Soren," Cael said, the one word and nothing after it, and the boy rose from the fire; and at the door he stopped and turned and regarded me once more with that level, unhurried attention, as though there were a question he had decided not to ask yet but did not mean to forget. Then they went out into the dark together, the old man and the boy, and I heard the stick of the one and the quick step of the other fade off up the lane, and something in the sound of it sat oddly and warmly in my chest, and I did not, that night, know what to call it.

Lira gave me a candle and a small room under the eaves with a bed and a stool and a window, and wished me a good night with the flat, practiced ease of a woman who had wished ten thousand of them, and left me to find the stairs on my own. I climbed the narrow stair and shut the door and was, for the first time since the far end of the afternoon, alone.

I did not sleep at once. I sat on the edge of the strange bed in the dark with my coat still on and, after a while, took from inside it a letter, not the three-page marvel from Penworth that had carried me here, but the one I had carried a great deal longer, and had never once had to open, because I knew every word of it already. It had come three weeks late, in another spring than this one, and it had told me a thing I had been too far from home to prevent. I did not open it now. I only held it, which was a habit I had fallen into and am not proud of, and I looked out the small window to where the hill stood as a darker shape against the dark, keeping whatever it kept, and I thought, with no more weight on the thought than that, *I will look at you in the morning, and then I will decide.*

I put the letter away, and lay down at last in a stranger's house, in a valley I had never meant to stay in, with the small sounds of the inn settling below me and a boy's quick step somehow still in my ears, and I slept better than I had slept in a long, long while.

Morning came clear and cold and washed, the way the best of spring mornings do, under a sky the pale scrubbed blue of something just laundered. I woke before I meant to and lay a while listening to the village wake around me, the beck, the first cart, a rooster with strong feelings and no sense of pitch, and then I went down, and Lira, who I do not believe slept at all, set porridge and honey and a slab of bacon in front of me and left me to eat it in peace.

"You'll be going up, then," she said, when I had done. It was not quite a question.

"I'll be going up."

She wiped the same clean patch of bar she had been wiping when I came down, a thing she did with her hands while she decided whether a thing was worth the saying. Mostly, with me, she decided it was not. "Mind how you go," she said when I rose, which from her I had already learned to take for a whole speech, and she took my bowl away, and did not ask what I hoped to find up on that hill, and did not offer me her opinion of what I would, and I liked her the better for the both of it.

I walked up through the waking village with the sun not yet over the eastern rise, and it went about its ordinary morning business around me, which is a thing I have always loved to watch and never tired of. A smith's fire was going somewhere, and the ring of a hammer came steady and unhurried across the cold air. The herb-shop had its shutters open to air the place, and the same green bitterness of the evening before drifted out of it into the street. A woman drove three geese ahead of her with a switch and a running commentary. At the mill, a broad figure gone white to the elbows with flour stood among stacks of drying timber and watched me take the lane past the last of the houses and out toward the open ground, and lifted two fingers off the head of a barrel in what I chose to take for encouragement.

And then the houses were behind me, and the lane became a track, and the track became a path worn faint into the turf by feet that had climbed it for longer than anyone living could account for, and the path ran up through the wet spring grass to the foot of the hill.

I stopped there, at the bottom of it, and stood, and looked up the long green shoulder of the thing to where it met the pale morning sky. It was (I will say it once more, because it was the last time it would ever be *only* this, and I want the record honest) an ordinary hill on an ordinary morning: green, and silent, and crowned with nothing but weather and the thin gold of the new light.

Then I started up.
