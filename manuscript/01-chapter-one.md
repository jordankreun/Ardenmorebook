# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter One: Ardenmoor

I came to Ardenmoor at the tail end of a long road, in the third week of spring, on a good grey mare named Snow, who had concluded some hours back that I did not know where I was going.

She had a point, though the fault was not entirely mine. I knew where I was going the way you know a thing you have been told rather than a thing you have seen. There was a hill for sale above a village of that name, and a fair breadth of ground around it, and I had come to look at what I had all but already bought. Penworth had told me to, and I had long since given up trusting my own judgment about what was good for me over his.

Penworth was my man of affairs, and had had the running of my whole tangled life for something over a decade by then. He kept the letters, the monies, and the small steady mountain of paper any working life throws off whether a man attends to it or not. He was a spare, precise, exceedingly formal gentleman out of Vethmark, near enough my own age and a good deal tidier about the wearing of it, and he had a particular genius, earned across a long career spent in the disorder of other men's affairs, for finding me things before I knew I wanted them, and for quietly buying the ground under a thing while I was still making up my mind about the thing itself. His letter had reached me at the turn of the year, and I had carried it about ever since, folded twice and gone soft at the creases from a season in my coat. It was a small marvel of its kind: three close pages to tell me he had come across a piece of country he believed would suit me, *a hill of no agricultural worth and considerable quiet*; that he had taken the liberty of securing it provisionally, along with a good breadth of the workable land about it, the sort you could set to honest tenants and thereafter forget you owned; and that he would count it a personal kindness if I would go and look at what I had nearly purchased before he made me finish the purchasing.

There was no thread of fate in it, whatever a story would want. I did not come to Ardenmoor drawn by anything grander than an errand. I came because Penworth had an unerring eye and I had learned to trust it over my own; because it was spring, and I had spent the whole of the winter shut up in a Vethmark townhouse with far more rooms than one man had any use for, most of them dark and under dust-sheets, and had barely marked the season going by; and because there was nowhere else in the wide world I would rather have been, which in those days was less a boast than a fairly bleak measure of how small my life had got. *A hill of no worth and considerable quiet.* When I read that, it sounded like very nearly the only thing left that I wanted.

The townhouse I had wintered in was my father's before it was mine, and my father had been an architect, which is to say a man who drew the houses that other men then lived their whole lives inside of. He had built up a small firm about the doing of it, a good and unglamorous one that outlived him and stands drawing houses yet, and I hold his share in it still, though I have not troubled it in years and it has had the grace not to trouble me. I had learned the drawing at his elbow as a boy, and paid my own way through my schooling at a board of my own, before I ever took up any stranger trade. The house he kept for himself, and had made years before for my mother, was the finest small thing he ever drew, and when the two of them were gone it came to me, and I had grown up in its cold handsome rooms and come back to them, after everything, the way a man comes back to a thing when he has run clean out of other places to be.

It was not the house I had bought in the good years. When I still had a life ahead of me and someone to spend it with, I had gone to my father's own firm and set them to draw me a grander house than my father would ever have countenanced, the same lines he had given my mother but swollen to twice the scale and three times the cost, ornament laid on ornament, a house built to say aloud a thing I did not otherwise have the words for. It stood across the river, tall and bright, my gift toward a wedding we did not in the end have. I never lived in it. I had not so much as crossed its threshold in three years, and I could no more sell it than set foot in it, and it stood there across the water full of a life that had not happened, costing me a small fortune a year in the bare keeping of a grand thing that nobody used, quietly eating money I could not make myself stop spending on it. Penworth had spent the better part of that winter trying, with a delicacy that plainly cost him, to get me to let it go, for the sake of my purse and, though he was too careful a man to say it, for the sake of the rest of me, and I had spent the winter not answering him. What I had not understood, riding up that valley, was that he had already gone and found the thing to let it go toward, and that it was a hill.

The first and easier part of it I had come by rail: three days out of Vethmark in a carriage that rattled the teeth in a man's head and let the cold in at every seam. The railways were new then, and it showed. The engine that hauled us was an early, soot-breathing thing, all iron and steam and appetite, and there was next to nothing of the arcane in it, which surprised the sort of people who supposed a wizard must approve of a train on principle. I did not, particularly. You may put a working into a machine, and I have put a good many into a good many; but a working has to be fed and tended and coaxed and renewed, and one big enough to shoulder a train up a grade would have eaten more in the keeping than the coal it saved. Magic in this world does not come by the ton. It comes by the thimble, and dearly, and no sane man spends a thimble of it where a shovelful of coal will serve. So the railways ran on fire and water and brute iron cleverness and almost no magic at all, and I spent a fair part of those three days watching the engine hiss and labor at the watering-stops and thinking, in the idle, itching way of a man who cannot let a made thing alone, how much smaller and quieter and cleverer the whole of it could have been built, if the world had only had the magic to spare for it. It did not. It never has. That is the first hard fact of my trade, and the trains were a great smoking monument to it: a mountain of crude iron doing loudly and dearly what a very little good artifice could have done well, had there only been enough good artifice to go around, which there was not, and is not, and I have stopped expecting there ever to be.

The line gave out, as such lines do, exactly where the paying traffic did, at a raw and cheerful town called Wainford that lived to pour the country's grain and cattle into railway cars and held no higher opinion of itself. There the rails simply stopped, and the country they had never troubled to reach began. I got Snow down out of the horse-box, the two of us equally glad to be quit of the noise, and from there we went on the old way, by horse and by road, four more days into the green silence.

The road to Ardenmoor had never been grand, and the last of it was humbler still: good enough packed dirt, quiet, kept carelessly and marked worse, the kind of country road that serves a place without troubling to announce it. Twice I took a fork on faith where no post stood to tell me otherwise, and was proved right more by luck than by judgment. The hedges had come into their first reckless leaf. The light lay long and yellow over a country of low hills and older stone, and a stream went about its business off to my left with no interest at all in mine. I let Snow set the pace, which was slow, and did not correct her. She had carried me many miles and had earned her opinions of the last few, and it is a small courtesy to let a good animal be right about the road.

The village came into view all at once, the way small places do, as the lane bent round the shoulder of an old oak. Ardenmoor lay below me in the last of the afternoon, and I will say plainly that it was an ordinary place. A scatter of thatch and grey stone strung along a beck, a mill wheel turning without hurry at the near end of it, smoke standing straight up from a dozen chimneys into windless air. Somewhere a dog was making its position known. Somewhere else a woman was calling two children in to supper by name, the second name twice, with the particular patience that is very shortly going to run out. I sat at the bend a while and looked at it, and felt the road go out of my shoulders, and was glad in a plain way to have arrived somewhere.

There was a hill to the north of the village and a little east of it, rounded and green and crowned with nothing but weather. I marked it the way you mark any rise of ground you have ridden a long way to look at. *That will be the one*, I thought, and thought no more of it than that. It was a hill. I had seen a thousand hills. Whatever it had to say for itself, it kept for later, and I have been grateful since; I could not have borne, that first tired evening, to be met by the whole of it at once.

I rode down into the village at a walk, and it looked me over the way a small village looks over a stranger, not unkindly but thoroughly, and then mostly went back to its evening. A hammer was still going somewhere among the houses, a smith at work past his hour, the ring of it carrying flat and clear across the cold. Further on, a shop breathed out the smell of an herbalist's trade as I passed its door, cut green stems and something bitter-clean beneath them, the smell of a place people go to when they are frightened for someone they love. Then it was behind me, and there was lamplight, and woodsmoke, and the good ordinary sound of a day being put away.

The inn stood where the street widened toward the beck. It was a broad, comfortable-looking building of three storeys, one of the few in Ardenmoor to stand so tall, with a painted sign gone too weathered to read and lamplight already spilling out of its windows into the blue evening. I have stopped at a great many inns and could write a dull and honest book on the subject. This one, before I had so much as got down off my horse, had the one quality that matters and cannot be built on purpose: it looked like somewhere a tired man might stop being tired for a while. I got down, and my legs told me at length exactly how far we had come. A boy came out of the inn-yard shadows to take Snow's head with the unhurried competence of a child who has been around horses since before he could walk.

He was perhaps nine. Thin, dark-haired, with a still, watchful way about him that was not shyness, though you might mistake it for that. It was the stillness of a child taking in a great deal more than he lets on. He looked at me a moment with a directness most children lose by that age, or are taught out of, then took the reins and gave his whole attention to the mare, which she plainly preferred.

He was a boy who was good with a horse, and for that first tired evening that was the whole of what I saw, and it was enough.

"There'll be a man come to see me," I told him. "Cael. Do you know where he'd be found?"

Something crossed the boy's face. I did not read it then; I have read it a hundred ways since. "I can fetch him," he said, and that was all. He saw the mare stabled and fed with quick sure hands before he went, which told me more about how he had been raised than an hour of talk.

Inside, the inn was warm and low-beamed and half full of the evening's regulars, and the woman behind the bar took one look at me and had the whole of it before I had crossed the room, the road and the errand and the name. Her name was Lira. She kept that inn the way a good captain keeps a ship, which is to say invisibly and completely, and she had been expecting me the better part of a week. She was a spare, measured woman who gave a stranger nothing he had not paid for, neither warmth nor its opposite, and it was a good while before I understood that this was not coldness in her but economy. She kept her regard the way she kept her good glasses, and did not bring it out for just anyone.

"You'll be the wizard, then," she said, not loudly, setting down in front of me a cup I had not asked for and immediately wanted. She said the word the way another woman might have said *the man about the drains*: a trade like any other, come to do a thing that wanted doing. I have been announced in grander rooms with a great deal more ceremony and a great deal less competence. "Room's held and paid, a fortnight since, by your man's man. Sit. Eat." A glance took the whole of the road off me in one pass. "You've the look of a man who's been eating out of his saddlebags a week."

I admitted that I had.

"Well," she said, as if that settled a question she had been privately turning over, and went to see about a plate.

Supper, when it came, was a plain and honest business: barley soup with good bread and a crock of butter, and after it a piece of hard cheese and a cup of the local small beer. I ate more of it than was strictly dignified. Lira did not watch me do it, which I was grateful for, and did not make talk, which I was more grateful for still. I had spent the last three years arranging my life so that no one would set a plate in front of me and want nothing back but that I eat it, and I had got good at the arranging. A plain bowl of soup in a plain room undid a little of that, and I did not look too closely at the feeling, having learned to be wary of the ones that came on that fast. Nine times in ten it was grief, come at me from a new direction.

Cael came in on the tail of the meal, the cold of the evening still on his shoulders and his grandson at his heel. He was not the man I had built out of Penworth's correspondence; I had made him older and grander in my head, and he was only just shy of old, in a good plain coat, with a face that had done a great deal of weather in its time and a pair of eyes that had lost none of their sharpness to it. He looked me over the way you look at a thing you have been expecting for months and are pleased, on the whole, to find is real. His glance caught a moment on my face, as people's often did, and came up a good way short of my true count of years. I was past forty by then, and have never worn it where a man can see, for reasons I will come to. Whatever figure he settled on he kept to himself, which I liked him for.

"You'll be Emlyn," he said.

"I will," I said. "And you'll be Cael."

"Since a good while before you got here." His mouth moved toward a smile and did not quite spend it. He lowered himself onto the bench across from me with the care of a man who has come to a private arrangement with his knees, and the boy sat a little apart, near the fire, close enough to hear and far enough to be overlooked, which I would come to learn was his usual and preferred distance from the world. "Your man wrote me you'd come the first clear week of spring, and here's you, near enough to the day. Thorough sort, your man. On his account I've signed my name more times this winter than in the ten years before it."

"He's a great believer in signatures," I said. "He says a thing that isn't written down is only a rumor two men are having."

"Aye, that sounds like him." Cael took a cup from Lira with a nod that had a long friendship folded up inside it. "Well. He'll have told you the shape of it. The hill, and the land about it, and the price, which is fair. I'll not haggle a fair price; I'm too old to enjoy it. But I told his agent, and I'll tell you to your face: I'd not sell that particular hill to just any man who came with money in his hand. I've had money in my hand for it before now, more than once. A fellow off the coast wanted the north face opened up for slate, and another was sure there was tin under it and meant to go down after it, and their coin was as good as yours or anyone's. I sent them both back down the road. A hill is not a purse you turn out and pocket. It is a thing a man stands on, and does something with, and answers for. If I sell it, I sell it to someone who means to make something of it, not to a man who means only to carry it off in carts." He turned his cup a slow quarter-turn on the table and did not drink from it. "My grandmother loved that rise of ground past all sense. Past all sense. She'd go up it in every weather to the year she died." He tipped his head toward the boy, who was studying the fire and missing nothing. "I was younger than that one there when she swore it to me. She swore that on the right kind of still evening, if you sat quiet on the top of it, you could hear it."

"Hear what?" I asked.

"That's the thing," said Cael. "She'd never say. I don't think she rightly knew. I think she only knew it was worth the listening, and that most folk hadn't the patience, and that it would be a poor business to sell a listening hill to a man who'd never once in his life sat still." He looked at me then, plainly, an old man taking an honest measure. "You've the look of a man who's sat still. I don't say it as a kindness."

"I've had reason to," I said, which was as near as I came, in those years, to telling anyone anything.

He nodded, as if I had confirmed something, and let it lie, and did not ask the reason. I have thought since that the not-asking was the first true gift anyone in that valley gave me.

For a while after that he said nothing at all. He sat with the cup between his two hands and looked into the fire, or past it, the way old men do when they are holding a thing up to the light of everyone who is no longer there to be asked, and I had the sense to let the silence be. The room went on around us, low talk and the settle of the coals, and he turned some private matter over until it came out the shape he wanted. Then he drank, at last, one slow swallow, and set the cup down with the air of a man who has signed something in his own head and does not mean to go back on it.

"Then here's what I'd say," he went on, brisker now, as if the matter were settled in him and only the arrangements were left. "It's full dark, and nothing worth buying ever looks like much at dusk. Take your room. Sleep. Walk up in the morning, when there's light to show you the truth of the place, and stand on it a while before you decide anything. If you still want it by noon, we'll go find Penworth's man and make an honest tangle of paperwork out of it, and I'll have sold my grandmother's hill to someone she'd have approved of, which is the most I've hoped for out of this business from the start." He got to his feet again by the same slow arrangement. "A man ought to look at a hill before he owns it. That's all the wisdom I've got, but I'm fairly sure of it."

I said that suited me. "Soren," Cael said, the one word and nothing after it, and the boy rose from the fire. At the door he stopped and turned and gave me that level, unhurried look once more, as if there were a question he had decided not to ask yet but did not mean to forget. Then they went out into the dark together, the old man and the boy, and I heard the stick of the one and the quick step of the other fade off up the lane, and something in the sound of it sat oddly and warmly in my chest, and I did not, that night, know what to call it.

Lira gave me a candle and showed me up herself to a good room under the eaves, the best she kept by the look of it: a proper bed made up with clean linen, a chair, a washstand with the water already set out, and a broad window that gave on the north side of the village. She wished me a good night with the flat, practiced ease of a woman who has wished ten thousand of them, and went back down to her work. I shut the door and was, for the first time since the far end of the afternoon, alone.

I did not sleep at once. I sat on the edge of the strange bed in the dark with my coat still on, and after a while took a letter out of it, not the three-page marvel from Penworth that had carried me here, but the one I had carried far longer and had never once had to open, because I knew every word of it already. It had come three weeks late, in another spring than this one, and it had told me a thing I had been too far from home to prevent. I did not open it now. I only held it, which was a habit I had fallen into and am not proud of, and I looked out the window to where the hill stood as a darker shape against the dark, keeping whatever it kept, and I thought, with no more weight on the thought than that, *I will look at you in the morning, and then I will decide.*

I put the letter away and lay down at last under a strange roof, in a valley I had never meant to stay in, with the small sounds of the inn settling below me and a boy's quick step somehow still in my ears, and I slept better than I had slept in a long, long while.

Morning came clear and cold and washed, the way the best of spring mornings do, under a pale sky the scrubbed blue of fresh-washed linen. I woke before I meant to and lay a while listening to the village wake around me, the beck, the first cart, a rooster with strong feelings and no sense of pitch, and then I went down, and Lira set porridge and honey and a slab of bacon in front of me and left me to eat it in peace.

"You'll be going up, then," she said, when I had done. It was not quite a question.

"I'll be going up."

She wiped the same clean patch of bar she had been wiping when I came down, a thing she did with her hands while she decided whether a thing was worth the saying. Mostly, with me, she decided it was not. "Mind how you go," she said when I rose, which from her I had already learned to take for a whole speech, and she took my bowl away, and did not ask what I hoped to find up on that hill, and did not offer me her opinion of what I would, and I liked her the better for both.

I walked up through the waking village with the sun not yet over the eastern rise, and it went about its ordinary morning business around me, which is a thing I have always loved to watch and never tired of. Near the inn a great domed oven of clay and fieldstone stood open to the lane, hot already, and a broad woman was drawing the first loaves out of it on a long wooden peel while the households with no oven of their own waited their turn with dough on boards, and a knot of children waited on nothing but the smell. That smell went the length of the street ahead of me, new bread and woodsmoke, and did more to recommend the place than anything the evening had shown me. Further along a dairymaid came the other way under two swinging pails, stepping wide of a man up a ladder patching a gable with last year's reed, and a woman drove three geese ahead of her with a switch and a running commentary they paid no mind to. A hammer started up somewhere behind me, a smith making an unhurried beginning on his day. At the mill, a broad figure gone white to the elbows with flour stood among stacks of drying timber and watched me take the lane past the last of the houses and out toward the open ground, and lifted two fingers off the head of a barrel in what I chose to take for encouragement.

Then the houses were behind me, and the lane became a track, and the track became a path worn faint into the turf by feet that had climbed it for longer than anyone living could account for, and the path ran up through the wet spring grass to the foot of the hill.

I stopped there, at the bottom of it, and stood, and looked up the long green shoulder of the thing to where it met the pale morning sky. It was, that first morning, only an ordinary hill: green, and silent, and crowned with nothing but weather and the thin gold of the new light. It was the last time it would ever be only that, and I am glad, now, that I did not know it.

Then I started up.
