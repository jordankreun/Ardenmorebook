# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Three: Under Canvas

The hill became mine in the way the world counts such things, which is to say on paper and in triplicate, a little under a fortnight after I shook Cael's hand on the crown of it. Penworth managed the whole of that from Vethmark without once setting foot in the valley, as I had known he would. A thing does not become real to Penworth until it has been written down three times and a copy lodged with some third man neither party quite trusts, and by that reckoning the hill was more real to him, a week of road and rail away, than it yet was to me standing on top of it.

His agent came up the road instead, a narrow careful young man with a satchel and a manner, come so quick behind my own letter that he must have been sent before ever it was written, which tells you what odds Penworth had laid on my answer. He and Cael and I sat an evening at Lira's over a great deal of paper and a small amount of beer, and I signed my name in all the places his finger came to rest, and Cael signed in fewer, and the credit was settled, and the thing was done. Cael did not read the papers. He watched me not read them either, and I think it pleased him. When it was finished he laid his hand flat on the topmost page a moment, the way you might lay it on the shoulder of someone leaving on a journey, and then took it away and said nothing, and that was the whole of his ceremony.

The buying of it was not nothing to me, and I did not do it out of a full purse. The land was cheap, as such things go, for two reasons. The first was that it lay about as far out from anywhere that mattered as a man could go and still be, on paper, inside the Commonwealth at all. It was a week's travelling from Vethmark, and you have made that road with me already: the rails giving out at Wainford, and four days more of horse into country the railway had never come for and, by the look of things, never would. Land the rails have reached is land somebody wants. This was the other kind. A loaded wagon needed the better part of a fortnight to make the crossing the mails made in a week.

The second reason was the first one wearing a different coat. Scarcely an acre of that ground had ever been put in the way of earning anything. I had assumed, in my ignorance, that cheap land meant bad land, and Penworth had the goodness to correct me in writing before I could embarrass myself with it aloud. Ground is not worth what it will grow, he wrote. Ground is worth what it can carry to a market, and every mile between the two comes off the price. The soil in those bottoms was as good as any I have stood on since. There was water everywhere, and timber on the slopes, and grazing on the high ground enough to keep a valley in wool. And it was worth next to nothing, because there was no road out worth the name, not a drain cut nor a fence standing over the most of it, no barns, no byres, and hardly a soul within a day's walk who was free to work it. A man who buys such a place is not buying a living. He is buying the makings of one, and the making is all still in front of him, and it is the making and not the buying that costs.

On the paper Penworth sent me the holding ran to something above five thousand acres, which sounds like the making of a lord and was nothing of the kind. Perhaps four hundred were in any sort of working order. The rest was rough country and honest enough: fell and heather and scrub-wood, old grazing gone back to bracken, and, stretching away north and west until it gave out in weather, a great slow reach of wet ground that wanted nothing in this world but draining and had never once been offered it. The hill that grew stone instead of grain sat in the middle of the whole of it, and a scatter of tired family fields lay about its skirts whose owners had long since stopped troubling over them. The great bulk of it Penworth had gathered up by the hundred acres for the price of the asking, and every acre of it would ask me for something before it gave me anything back.

Cheap, and it still took very nearly every silver I could put a hand to. That is a different thing altogether from everything I had, as I have learned since. Penworth spent the winter turning my affairs over in his careful hands to see what they would bear, and what they bore was a great deal on paper and almost nothing in coin. The river house was the most of my worth, and the river house would not move quickly; a grand house in a great city is a slow animal, and that one stood on ground that carried a debt I had signed for in a better year. When it went at last the proceeds would come to me in two lump sums, one the buyer's deposit to secure the sale and one to come at a later date out of his lender. Rather than sit and wait on it, Penworth borrowed against it: a short loan out of a bank that knew the street the house stood in and cared nothing whatever for the country I was going to, the whole of it to fall due when the sale cleared, and a sale of that size clears at the pace of its lawyers, which Penworth priced, as he priced everything, at very nearly a year.

Set beside that were the old patents, which paid their steady quarterly pittance whether I attended to them or not, and my father's firm, which went on paying its partner's share into an account I never opened. My father's house did not appear in the columns at all. Penworth knew better than to put it there, and I would not have signed the page it was on.

He ruled it out for me in a letter, in neat columns, and at the foot of the columns stood a sum aimed like a spent arrow at a hill nobody wanted, and the loosing of it left me holding five thousand acres and next to no money, a condition I had no name for then and have never since been entirely out of.

That was the mercy in the thing, and the whole of the trap. So nothing at all fell due until the place had been given a full turn of the seasons to find its feet, which was not generosity but conveyancing, and it meant that in a year I would need this hill to be earning, one way or another. The farmland in the bottoms would have to be got into the hands of tenants who would work it and pay for the working. The trade would have to come. A wiser man would have looked at that year the way you look at a lit fuse, and been frightened. I looked at it and felt, for the first time in three years, interested. I have learned to trust that in myself further than I trust my caution, though it has cost me nearly as often as it has paid.

I lodged at Lira's while the paper went back and forth. I had thought, when I took the room, that I would want it a night or two and no more; I kept it the better part of the rest of the season. Lira let me the room by the week without comment, and fed me without comment, and formed her opinion of me the way she formed all her opinions, slowly and to herself, and let it out a word at a time over the weeks, the way you let out a coat you mean to keep.

A village the size of Ardenmoor does not decide about a man quickly, and is right not to. For the first of those weeks I was served at Lira's promptly and correctly and with about the warmth of a signed receipt, which suited me well, for it asked nothing of me in return. I was the foolish and strange wizard who had bought Cael's hill, and that is a thing to be watched a while before it is a thing to be welcomed. So I did not hurry it. I paid what I owed on the day I owed it, and said good morning to men who did not yet say it back, and kept my strangeness folded up small and out of sight, and let the place look at me as long as it cared to. Once, early, one of the quarrymen leaned over from the next bench, emboldened by his cups, and asked me straight out whether it was true I was a wizard, and what that meant a man could do. I told him it meant mostly that I could ruin an evening explaining what it did not mean, and I bought him a drink, and he went away satisfied. After that I was, if not a friend, then at least a known quantity, which in a village is the first step toward the rest.

I went up the hill every day of that fortnight, whether there was anything to do up there or not, which for a good while there was not. I walked the bounds until I knew them in the dark. I sat on the crown and let the valley go about its business below me and did a great deal of the particular nothing that looks, to a busy man passing, exactly like idleness, and is in truth the slow work of coming to know a place well enough to ask it for something. Cael's grandson took to appearing at the edges of it, never on the crown itself and never quite trespassing, only there of a sudden at the foot of the track or along the edge of the woodland, watching, the way a cat will sit at the exact calculated boundary of a room it has been told to keep out of. I let him watch. A boy has a right to watch a hill his family has held forty years. I have never been one to chase off a quiet audience.

What I was truly waiting on, that fortnight, was my life to come up out of Vethmark and find me.

I had sent for it the day the handshake was dry, and there was not much of it. A man who has lived three years alone in his parents' house learns exactly how little of it he actually touches. I had left the quiet rooms of that Vethmark house as I had kept them, sheeted and still and full of the furniture of a life I no longer led, and I had sent Penworth's carters through it with a short list, and what came up the valley road was the short list and nothing more: my books, which were many; the tools and instruments of my trade, which were fewer but more particular; a bed, a chair, a chest of clothes, a particular kettle I preferred, the plain machinery of one man keeping himself alive. And one thing more, which rode alone.

The wagons came up on a grey wet afternoon, groaning and lurching along the last of the road into the little village. I went down to meet them at the door of the inn, and Soren came from wherever he kept himself and fell in beside me without being asked, and the two of us and the two drivers spent a wet and satisfying couple of hours carrying my life into the corner of the inn's stable Lira had let me for the task. The boy worked without being told and without complaint, and had a good eye for how a load wants to sit, and I marked it and said nothing, for I had already learned that saying a thing to that boy was the surest way to make him careful of it.

The books had come up to my room in crates I trusted to no one else, the better part of a lifetime's reading and a fair share of its writing. I stacked them in no order at all and knew already the long good evenings I would spend giving them one. My instruments I was more careful of. They are fewer than folk imagine and stranger, the fine-drawn things a man in my line uses to feel for what is already in a place before ever he asks it for anything: gauges and glasses and lengths of drawn wire that mean nothing to the eye and a great deal to the hand, each wrapped in its own cloth against the jolting of the road. I laid them out under cover and ran my hands over them in the failing light, taking their count, and it came to me that I had not opened most of these cases in three years. Their original destination, the grand study I had made for myself in another life, they had never reached.

Near the end, when the light was going and the last of the rain had begun to clear, the drivers brought up the last thing between them, slung on a plank because neither would carry it alone, and set it down where I told them and stepped back from it a little more quickly than its weight accounted for.

It was not large; a single man could have carried it, though the drivers had chosen not to, and I did not blame them, for there are things the body declines to shoulder alone before the mind has troubled to say why. It was old, older than anything else I owned, black wood banded in a metal that was not iron and had never rusted. It had no keyhole any locksmith would know and no lock any key would turn, because the lock on it was mine and was not the kind you open with your hands. I had made that binding myself, years before, in another life, and had not unmade it since, and did not mean to. What was inside it was mine and no one else's. I had carried it out of the wreck of everything and into that dark sheeted house, and now up next to the wet hill that would be its home.

Soren watched me set it under the canvas, apart from the rest, and did not ask about it. He was the only soul in that valley, then or for a long time after, who never once asked. I have wondered since whether he already knew, in the wordless way he knew a great many things, that it was not a thing to be asked about, or whether he was only a boy with better manners than most grown men. Either way I was grateful, and gave him a coin for the afternoon worth a good deal more than the afternoon, and he looked at it, and looked at me, and took it, which I have always thought the gracious thing to do; a lesser child would have refused it to seem fine, and a greater fool would have haggled.

The drivers took their wagons away before the light was wholly gone, glad, I think, to be quit of the tiny village and the rain and the chest, and Soren went off down the track to his grandfather's fire. And I stood in the late of the afternoon, the sun just breaking through the grey, and stared at my hill with everything I owned in the world stacked in oilcloth behind me, and found that I did not, after all, want to join my books and the rest of my possessions back at the inn.

So I gathered Snow and ventured up my hill to make a camp. I had a good tent of waxed canvas that had kept the weather off me, and I pitched it near the crown in the lee of the one shoulder of ground that offered any. I dug a fire-pit and coaxed a fire out of the dry heart of wood the rain had not yet reached, and set my kettle in the coals and made tea in the smell of woodsmoke and wet grass and canvas, which is one of the oldest and kindest smells there is. I ate bread and cheese carried up from Lira's, and sat in the door of my tent with the fire dying and the valley below me putting out its lamps one window at a time, and was as nearly at peace as I had been since a letter came three weeks late in another spring.

I had not meant to move onto the hill for good. I had a dry room a mile down the track with a woman who cooked, and only a fool trades that for wet canvas on a whim. But I stayed that night, and then the next, and inside of a week I had made the crown a kind of home. I set stones round the fire-pit and learned which way the wind came over the shoulder of the ground and shifted the tent twice before I had it right. I worked out where the water stood after rain and where it ran off clean. I came to know the hill the way you only ever truly know a place, which is by sleeping on it and waking on it and being rained on by it in the dark. Soren took to bringing my bread up of a morning so I need not go down for it, a thing neither of us ever named and both of us understood. It was, in its small and unremarkable way, the first household I had kept in three years, and I was slow to notice how much good it was doing me, the way a man is slow to notice a fever breaking.

I should have been thinking about the work. I had bought the hill to build on it, after all, and Jorin's name was already in my mind, and there was a study to raise before autumn if I meant to have a roof over my instruments before the weather turned in earnest. But I found I was in no hurry to begin, and it took me a while to understand why, and when I did I was careful not to say it aloud even to myself, because said plainly it sounded like a man making excuses for his own idleness. It was this. Every soul in the valley knew the hill had thrown off every roof and wall ever set on it, and I knew it too, from Cael, to my face. A sensible man meaning to prove them wrong would have come up his first morning with his sleeves rolled and gone at the ground the way Cael's father had, deeper and harder and more stubborn, and would have got, I do not doubt, exactly what Cael's father got. I am not always a sensible man, but I have learned a few things dearly, and one of them is that when a place has said no to every question ever put to it, the fault may lie in the questions. I did not yet know what I meant to ask that hill. I only knew I would not ask it until I understood the ground well enough to ask it rightly, and that a whole summer spent learning to ask would come cheaper, in the end, than a single wall thrown up in ignorance and pulled down again in the night. So I sat, and walked, and slept on it, and let the village think me idle, and waited to be taught.

It was some nights into that new life, and not the first, that the hill spoke to me.

The fire had gone out, and I had lain down, and it began. I did not hear anything. I want to be exact, because it matters, and because I had spent a lifetime learning the difference between hearing a thing and what people mean when they say they heard. There was no sound. Had you stood beside me on the crown that night with your own two ears you would have sworn the hill dead silent, and as far as ears go you would have been right.

But I was lying full length on the ground with nothing between me and it but a groundsheet and a blanket, and it came up into me through the bone. It was below sound the way the lowest note of a great bell is below sound, the part you feel in the chest and the back teeth a moment before the ringing reaches you, except that the ringing never came. It was steady. It was slow. It had the patience of something very large and very old turning over in its sleep, and I lay in the dark and felt the hill I had bought move once, low, against the whole length of my body, and go still, and I told myself it was the blood in my own ears, and did not believe it, and lay very still and waited to feel it again.

It came again near the turning of the night, in the coldest hour, the hour before any bird. A single slow pulse up through the ground, no louder and no less, and this time I told myself nothing at all. I lay with my eyes open in the dark of the tent, the chest a black weight at my back, and listened with the whole of myself, the way I had not truly listened to anything in three years, and the hill said its one deep wordless thing to the underside of me and fell quiet.

And I understood, lying there, that Cael's grandmother had been telling the plain truth for forty years, and that most of the valley had spent those forty years politely calling a wise woman a fool, and that I owed her, whoever she had been, an apology I could never pay.

There was a second thought under that one, smaller, and I did not look straight at it, the way you do not look straight at a mark on your own skin you have only just found and do not yet want to give a name. It was only this: that on all my daylight walks and idle sittings the hill had been silent as any hill in the world, and that it kept, it seemed, its own two hours, the failing of the light and the cold before the dawn, and that I had simply never before lain still upon it, in the dark, at an hour it chose to speak. That was all it was. I said as much to myself, more than once, in the cold, and mostly I believed it.

I did not sleep much the rest of that night, and what I got was thin. But I was not afraid. I kept waiting to be, lying there in the dark taking my own measure, and the fear never came. I had come up the valley a man who wanted nothing he could lose, and I had lain down on a hill no one had ever been able to build on, and the hill had turned in its long sleep and felt the weight of me and answered. Whatever else that was, it was not nothing, and I had, by then, a very great deal of nothing.

I lay in the cold before dawn with my hand flat on the cold ground, the way the boy had laid his flat on the stone, and I asked the hill nothing, because I did not yet know how, and because there are questions a man does not put to a thing so soon after coming to sleep in its house. I only kept it company in the dark, the way Cael kept the foot of it company from his wall, and the boy kept its edges, and half that quiet valley had been keeping this hill company the whole of their lives without ever once saying so aloud.

Below me the beck ran on. Out in the north wood the old oak stood in the rain and kept its own counsel. And on the crown of the hill, in a tent of waxed canvas, I lay awake and waited for the light, and wanted, for the first time in a long while and to my own great surprise, to see what the morning would bring.

It brought rain, mostly, and a grey ordinary daylight in which the hill was only a hill again, wet and green and patient and giving no sign of anything, so that a lesser man, or a wiser one, might have put the whole of the night down to cold and tiredness and the fancies that come to a man alone in a tent. I did not. I had lain awake through two slow beats of a buried heart and felt them in my teeth, and no amount of sensible morning was going to talk me back out of it. I made my tea. I ate the bread the boy brought up. And I set about, that day, without quite admitting even to myself that I had set about anything at all, to learn how to listen to my hill in earnest.
