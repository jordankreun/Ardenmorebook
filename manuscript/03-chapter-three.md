# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Three: Under Canvas

The hill became mine in the way the world counts such things, which is to say on paper and in triplicate, a little under a fortnight after I shook Cael's hand on the crown of it. Penworth managed the whole of that from Vethmark without once setting foot in the valley, as I had known he would. A thing does not become real to Penworth until it has been written down three times and a copy lodged with some third man neither party quite trusts, and by that reckoning the hill was more real to him, four days' ride away, than it yet was to me standing on top of it.

His agent came up the road instead, a narrow careful young man with a satchel and a manner, and he and Cael and I sat an evening at Lira's over a great deal of paper and a small amount of beer, and I signed my name in all the places his finger came to rest, and Cael signed in fewer, and the credit was settled, and the thing was done. Cael did not read the papers. He watched me not read them either, and I think it pleased him. When it was finished he laid his hand flat on the topmost page a moment, the way you might lay it on the shoulder of someone leaving on a journey, and then took it away and said nothing, and that was the whole of his ceremony.

I lodged at Lira's while the paper went back and forth, and for a good while after. I had thought, when I took the room, that I would want it a night or two and no more; I kept it the better part of the season in the end, and was glad of it, for a hill is a poor roof in the rain and I was in no hurry to learn that lesson the hard way. Lira let me the room by the week without comment, and fed me without comment, and formed her opinion of me the way she formed all her opinions, slowly and to herself, and let it out a word at a time over the weeks the way a careful woman lets out the waist of a coat she means to keep.

A village the size of Ardenmoor does not decide about a man quickly, and is right not to. For the first of those weeks I was served at Lira's promptly and correctly and with about the warmth of a signed receipt, which suited me well, for it asked nothing of me in return. I was the wizard who had bought Cael's hill, and that is a thing to be watched a while before it is a thing to be welcomed. So I did not hurry it. I paid what I owed on the day I owed it, and said good morning to men who did not yet say it back, and kept my strangeness folded up small and out of sight, and let the place look at me as long as it cared to. Once, early, one of the quarrymen leaned over from the next bench, emboldened by his cups, and asked me straight out whether it was true I was a wizard, and what that meant a man could do. I told him it meant mostly that I could ruin an evening explaining what it did not mean, and I bought him a drink, and he went away satisfied. After that I was, if not a friend, then at least a known quantity, which in a village is the necessary first station on the road to the other thing.

I went up the hill every day of that fortnight, whether there was anything to do up there or not, which for a good while there was not. I walked the bounds until I knew them in the dark. I sat on the crown and let the valley go about its business below me and did a great deal of the particular nothing that looks, to a busy man passing, exactly like idleness, and is in truth the slow work of coming to know a place well enough to ask it for something. Cael's grandson took to appearing at the edges of it, never on the crown itself and never quite trespassing, only there of a sudden at the foot of the track or along the edge of the woodland, watching, the way a cat will sit at the exact calculated boundary of a room it has been told to keep out of. I let him watch. A boy has a right to watch a hill his family has held forty years, and I have never been one to chase off a quiet audience.

What I was truly waiting on, that fortnight, was my life to come up out of Vethmark and find me.

I had sent for it the day the handshake was dry, and there was not, in the end, a great deal of it. A man who has spent three years shut up in a house too large for him learns exactly how little of it he actually touches. I had left the great rooms of that Vethmark house as I had kept them, dark and sheeted and full of the furniture of a life I no longer led, and I had sent Penworth's carters through it with a short list, and what came up the valley road was the short list and nothing more: my books, which were many; the tools and instruments of my trade, which were fewer but a good deal more particular; a bed, a chair, a chest of clothes, a kettle, the plain machinery of one man keeping himself alive. And one thing more, which rode alone.

The wagons came up on a grey soft afternoon with rain not far off, groaning and lurching along the last of the giving-up road with the drivers walking at the horses' heads over the worst of it. I went down to meet them at the foot of the hill, and Soren came from wherever he kept himself and fell in beside me without being asked, and the two of us and the two drivers spent a wet and satisfying couple of hours carrying my life up the track by hand where the wagons could not go. There is a plain honesty to carrying a thing up a hill. The boy worked without being told and without complaint, and had a good eye for how a load wants to sit, and I marked it and said nothing, for I had already learned that saying a thing to that boy was the surest way to make him careful of it.

There is a particular pleasure, and a particular grief, in unpacking the tools of a trade in a new place. The books had come up in crates I had packed myself and trusted to no one else, the better part of a lifetime's reading and a good deal of a lifetime's writing, and I stacked them under the oilcloth in no order at all and knew already the long good evenings I would spend giving them one. My instruments I was more careful of. They are fewer than folk imagine and stranger, the fine-drawn things a man in my line uses to feel for what is already in a place before ever he asks it for anything: gauges and glasses and lengths of drawn wire that mean nothing to the eye and a great deal to the hand, each wrapped in its own cloth against the jolting of the road. I laid them out under cover and ran my hands over them in the failing light, taking their count, and it came to me that I had not opened most of these cases in three years, and that I had packed them, when I packed them, the way you pack the effects of the dead, without any real expectation of wanting them again. Now here they were on a wet hill, wanting to be wanted. It is a strange thing to be outlived by your own tools.

Near the end, when the light was going and the first of the rain had begun to come down in the fine sifting way that means to keep on, the drivers brought up the last thing between them, slung on a plank because neither would carry it alone, and set it down where I told them and stepped back from it a little more quickly than its weight accounted for.

It is time I told you about the chest. Or as much of it as I told anyone in those days, which was nothing at all.

It was not large; a strong man could have carried it, though the drivers had chosen not to, and I did not blame them, for there are things the body declines to shoulder alone before the mind has troubled to say why. It was old, older than anything else I owned, black wood banded in a metal that was not iron and had never rusted, and it had no keyhole any locksmith would know and no lock any key would turn, because the lock on it was mine and was not the kind you open with your hands. I had made that binding myself, years before, in another life, and had not unmade it since, and did not mean to. What was inside it was mine and no one else's. I had carried it out of the wreck of everything and into that dark sheeted house, and now up this wet hill, and I handled it always with more care than I handled anything I owned, myself in those years included.

Soren watched me set it under the canvas, apart from the rest, and did not ask about it. He was the only soul in that valley, then or for a long time after, who never once asked. I have wondered since whether he already knew, in the wordless way he knew a great many things, that it was not a thing to be asked about, or whether he was only a boy with better manners than most grown men. Either way I was grateful, and gave him a coin for the afternoon worth a good deal more than the afternoon, and he looked at it, and looked at me, and took it, which I have always thought the gracious thing to do; a lesser child would have refused it to seem fine, and a greater fool would have haggled.

The drivers took their wagons back down before the light was wholly gone, glad, I think, to be quit of the hill and the rain and the chest, and Soren went off down the track to his grandfather's fire, and I stood in the last grey wet of the evening on the crown of my own hill with everything I owned in the world stacked in oilcloth behind me, and found that I did not, after all, want to go down to the inn.

So I made a camp. I had a good tent of waxed canvas that had kept the weather off me across country a man could not cross in a year, and I pitched it near the crown in the lee of the one shoulder of ground that offered any, and dug a fire-pit and coaxed a fire out of the dry heart of wood the rain had not yet reached, and set my kettle in the coals and made tea in the smell of woodsmoke and wet grass and canvas, which is one of the oldest and kindest smells there is. The chest I brought inside the tent and set at my back and did not look at. I ate bread and cheese carried up from Lira's, and sat in the door of my tent with the fire dying and the valley below me putting out its lamps one window at a time, and was as nearly at peace as I had been since a letter came three weeks late in another spring.

I had not meant to move onto the hill for good. I had a dry room a mile down the track with a woman who cooked, and only a fool trades that for wet canvas on a whim. But I stayed that night, and then the next, and inside of a week I had made the crown a kind of home. I set stones round the fire-pit and learned which way the wind came over the shoulder of the ground and shifted the tent twice before I had it right. I worked out where the water stood after rain and where it ran off clean. I came to know the hill the way you only ever truly know a place, which is by sleeping on it and waking on it and being rained on by it in the dark. Soren took to bringing my bread up of a morning so I need not go down for it, a thing neither of us ever named and both of us understood. It was, in its small and unremarkable way, the first household I had kept in three years, and I was slow to notice how much good it was doing me, the way a man is slow to notice a fever breaking.

I should have been thinking about the work. I had bought the hill to build on it, after all, and Jorin's name was already in my mind, and there was a study to raise before autumn if I meant to have a roof over my instruments before the weather turned in earnest. But I found I was in no hurry to begin, and it took me a while to understand why, and when I understood it I was careful not to say it aloud even to myself, because said plainly it sounded like a man making excuses for his own idleness. It was this. Every soul in the valley knew the hill had thrown off every roof and wall ever set on it, and I knew it too, from Cael, to my face. A sensible man meaning to prove them wrong would have come up his first morning with his sleeves rolled and gone at the ground the way Cael's father had, deeper and harder and more stubborn, and would have got, I do not doubt, exactly what Cael's father got. I am not always a sensible man, but I have learned a few things dearly, and one of them is that when a place has said no to every question ever put to it, the fault may lie in the questions. I did not yet know what I meant to ask that hill. I only knew I would not ask it until I understood the ground well enough to ask it rightly, and that a whole summer spent learning to ask would come cheaper, in the end, than a single wall thrown up in ignorance and pulled down again in the night. So I sat, and walked, and slept on it, and let the village think me idle, and waited to be taught.

It was some nights into that new life, and not the first, that the hill spoke to me.

The fire had gone out, and I had lain down, and it began. I did not hear anything. I want to be exact, because it matters, and because I had spent a lifetime learning the difference between hearing a thing and what people mean when they say they heard. There was no sound. Had you stood beside me on the crown that night with your own two ears you would have sworn the hill dead silent, and as far as ears go you would have been right.

But I was lying full length on the ground with nothing between me and it but a groundsheet and a blanket, and it came up into me through the bone. It was below sound the way the lowest note of a great bell is below sound, the part you feel in the chest and the back teeth a moment before the ringing reaches you, except that the ringing never came. It was steady. It was slow. It had the patience of something very large and very old turning over in its sleep, and I lay in the dark and felt the hill I had bought move once, low, against the whole length of my body, and go still, and I told myself it was the blood in my own ears, and did not believe it, and lay very still and waited to feel it again.

It came again near the turning of the night, in the coldest hour, the hour before any bird. A single slow pulse up through the ground, no louder and no less, and this time I told myself nothing at all. I lay with my eyes open in the dark of the tent, the chest a black weight at my back, and listened with the whole of myself, the way I had not truly listened to anything in three years, and the hill said its one deep wordless thing to the underside of me and fell quiet.

And I understood, lying there, that Cael's grandmother had been telling the plain truth for forty years, and that most of the valley had spent those forty years politely calling a wise woman a fool, and that I owed her, whoever she had been, an apology I could never pay.

There was a second thought under that one, smaller, and I did not look straight at it, the way you do not look straight at a mark on your own skin you have only just found and do not yet want to give a name. It was only this: that on all my daylight walks and idle sittings the hill had been silent as any hill in the world, and that it kept, it seemed, its own two hours, the failing of the light and the cold before the dawn, and that I had simply never before lain still upon it, in the dark, at an hour it chose to speak. That was all it was. I said as much to myself, more than once, in the cold, and mostly I believed it.

I did not sleep much the rest of that night, and what I got was thin. But I was not afraid, and I want that set down, because with all that came after it would be easy to make me afraid on that hill, and I never truly was. I had come up the valley a man who wanted nothing he could lose, and I had lain down on a hill no one had ever been able to build on, and the hill had turned in its long sleep and felt the weight of me and answered. Whatever else that was, it was not nothing, and I had had, by then, a very great deal of nothing.

I lay in the cold before dawn with my hand flat on the cold ground, the way the boy had laid his flat on the stone, and I asked the hill nothing, because I did not yet know how, and because there are questions a man does not put to a thing so soon after coming to sleep in its house. I only kept it company in the dark, the way Cael kept the foot of it company from his wall, and the boy kept its edges, and half that quiet valley had been keeping this hill company the whole of their lives without ever once saying so aloud.

Below me the beck ran on. Out in the north wood the old oak stood in the rain and kept its own counsel. And on the crown of the hill, in a tent of waxed canvas, with a chest I would not open at my back and a life I had thought was finished stacked in oilcloth all around me, I lay awake and waited for the light, and wanted, for the first time in a long while and to my own great surprise, to see what the morning would bring.

It brought rain, mostly, and a grey ordinary daylight in which the hill was only a hill again, wet and green and patient and giving no sign of anything, so that a lesser man, or a wiser one, might have put the whole of the night down to cold and tiredness and the fancies that come to a man alone in a tent. I did not. I have never been any good at unknowing a thing once I have known it, and I had lain awake through two slow beats of a buried heart and felt them in my teeth, and no amount of sensible morning was going to talk me back out of it. I made my tea. I ate the bread the boy brought up. And I set about, that day, without quite admitting even to myself that I had set about anything at all, to learn how to listen to my hill in earnest. But that is the next part.
