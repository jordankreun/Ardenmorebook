# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Three: Under Canvas

The morning came, and a good many after it, and what they brought up the valley road was paper. The hill became mine in the way the world counts such things, which is to say in triplicate, a little under a fortnight after I shook Cael's hand on the crown of it. Penworth did the whole of it from Vethmark without once setting foot in the valley, as I had known he would, and by the end the hill was more real to him, a week of road and rail away, than it was to me standing on top of it.

His agent came up the road instead, a narrow careful young man with a satchel and a manner, come so quick behind my own letter that he must have been sent before ever it was written.

He came in on the carrier's cart with four days of the road on his boots and his satchel across his body, and he did not set it down until he was sitting. Lira gave him the small room at the back. He took it without asking the price. An hour later I found him in the taproom with his coat still on, laying his papers out in the order he meant to use them and weighting each pile with whatever the room afforded: the salt, a candlestick, a smooth river stone some child had left on the sill and forgotten.

He stood up when I came over. He said Penworth's name once, as another man produces a warrant, and then did not say it again all evening.

"Is the seller a difficult man?" he asked.

I said he was an honest one.

"That is not what I asked," he said, and made a small note.

He and Cael and I sat an evening at Lira's over a great deal of paper and a small amount of beer, and I signed my name in all the places his finger came to rest, and Cael signed in fewer, and the credit was settled, and the thing was done.

It took the better part of four hours.

Cael came at the hour he had been asked for, with his hat in his hand and the boy behind him. He gave the table the same look a man gives a horse he has heard too much about. Then he sat down opposite me, put the hat on the bench at his side, and said, "Well."

The agent read the parcels out one at a time and made a small mark against each as he read it.

"The hill, so called," he said, "with the crown, the spring on the south face, and the ground falling away from it on every side to the bounds as marked. Held by the seller. Held before him by his grandmother, and conveyed to him at her death."

"She'd have liked hearing that read out," Cael said. "She was fond of a bit of ceremony, so long as it was somebody else's."

The agent waited to learn whether that was an objection. When it turned out not to be, he made his mark and went on.

The strip of woodland by the low road came next, half of it Cael's and half of it bought off Harrow's people down on the coast, who had held it six years without ever once coming to look at it. Then the meadow at the northeast shoulder, four and twenty acres, of a family that had never grazed a beast on it in living memory and had been glad of the coin. Then a long dull list of strips and corners and odd rods of ground that had been squared up with the parish over the winter, each with its own line and its own mark. Then the high ground, which the agent called common of pasture as enjoyed by the township, unenclosed and not to be enclosed, and which Cael called the fell.

"And a share in the water of the mill-pond," the agent finished, "the size of the share being set out in the schedule."

I read the schedule. Then I read it again, because the first reading had gone through me and out the other side without stopping.

"Your man's instruction," the agent said, "was that no care whatever was to be spared over the water. I have spared none."

He had four pages on one spring. I have drawn buildings that took less paper.

Lira set a fresh lamp at the corner of the table before the old one had begun to gutter, and took the jug away, and brought it back full, and did that twice more before we were finished, and said nothing about the hour. The evening's regulars sat where they always sat. Nobody in that room was watching us, and every soul in it knew which page we were on.

"There will be three copies," the agent said. "One travels with me. One is the seller's. The third is to be lodged with a party who is neither."

"Who'd that be, then," Cael said, "here?"

The agent looked round Lira's taproom as though some suitable institution might be standing in a corner of it.

I said that the man who kept the dry-goods yard had a book, a strongbox, and no particular reason to love either of us.

"He keeps a book," Cael agreed.

"Does he keep it well?"

"He keeps it," Cael said, "better than the parish keeps its own."

The agent wrote it down.

Then the signing, which took longer than the reading. He had his own ink in a travelling case with a screw stopper, and his own pen, and a little jar of sand, and he set his finger on each place in turn and left it there until I had put my name in it. Eleven times. He turned every sheet a quarter round to face me before I signed and a quarter back after. Cael signed in four places only, forming the letters as though he were laying stones, with his tongue between his teeth and his whole shoulder in it. It was a better hand than mine.

Halfway through, the draught off the door got at the lamp and the flame went sideways. The boy came off the settle before anybody could speak of it and stood at Cael's elbow with his hands cupped round the glass until the last of it was signed, and then took them away and sat down again, and that was the whole of his part in the purchase of five thousand acres.

No coin came out at any point of the evening. The price crossed the table as a folded paper drawn on a bank in Vethmark, and Cael put it in his inside pocket without looking at it.

Cael did not read the papers. He watched me not read them either, and I think it pleased him. When it was finished he laid his hand flat on the topmost page a moment, the way you might lay it on the shoulder of someone leaving on a journey, and then took it away and said nothing, and that was the whole of his ceremony.

The buying of it was not nothing to me, and I did not do it out of a full purse. The land was cheap, as such things go, for two reasons. The first was that it lay about as far out from anywhere that mattered as a man could go and still be, on paper, inside the Commonwealth at all. It was a week's travelling from Vethmark: the rails giving out at Wainford, and four days more of horse into country the railway had never come for and, by the look of things, never would. Land the rails have reached is land somebody wants. This was the other kind. A loaded wagon needed the better part of a fortnight to make the crossing the mails made in a week.

The second reason was the first one wearing a different coat. Scarcely an acre of that ground had ever been put in the way of earning anything. I had assumed, in my ignorance, that cheap land meant bad land, and Penworth corrected me in writing before I could embarrass myself with it aloud. Ground is not worth what it will grow, he wrote. Ground is worth what it can carry to a market, and every mile between the two comes off the price. He had put figures under it as well, in the small hand he keeps for arithmetic: what a load of barley fetched standing on the boards at the Wainford rail-head, and what the same load fetched standing in a barn four days short of it, and what the difference was called by the men who lived on it. Then he added that he had spent his working life explaining this to men who bought land for the view, and that he was glad to find me buying one for the quiet. The soil in those bottoms was as good as any I have stood on since. There was water everywhere, and timber on the slopes, and grazing on the high ground enough to keep a valley in wool. And it was worth next to nothing, because there was no road out worth the name, not a drain cut nor a fence standing over the most of it, no barns, no sheds, and hardly a soul within a day's walk who was free to work it. A man who buys such a place is not buying a living. He is buying the makings of one, and the making costs more than the buying.

On the paper Penworth sent me the holding ran to something above five thousand acres, which sounds like the making of a lord and was nothing of the kind. Perhaps four hundred were in any sort of working order. The rest was rough country and honest enough: fell and heather and scrub-wood, old grazing gone back to ferns, and, stretching away north and west until it gave out in weather, a great slow reach of wet ground that wanted nothing in this world but draining and had never been offered it. The hill that grew stone instead of grain sat in the middle of the whole of it, and a scatter of tired family fields lay about its skirts whose owners had long since stopped troubling over them. The great bulk of it Penworth had gathered up by the hundred acres for the price of the asking, and every acre of it would ask me for something before it gave me anything back.

Cheap, and it still took very nearly every silver I could put a hand to. Penworth spent the winter turning my affairs over in his careful hands to see what they would bear, and what they bore was a great deal on paper and almost nothing in coin. The river house was the most of my worth, and the river house would not sell in a hurry; a grand house in a great city is a slow animal, and that one stood on ground that carried a debt I had signed for in a better year. When it went at last the proceeds would come to me in two lump sums, one the buyer's deposit to secure the sale and one to come at a later date out of his lender. Rather than sit and wait on it, Penworth borrowed against it: a short loan out of a bank that knew the street the house stood in and cared nothing whatever for the country I was going to, the whole of it to fall due when the sale cleared, and a sale of that size clears at the pace of its lawyers, which Penworth priced, as he priced everything, at very nearly a year.

Set beside that were the old patents, which paid their steady quarterly pittance whether I attended to them or not, and my father's firm, which went on paying its partner's share into an account I never opened. My father's house did not appear in the columns at all. Penworth knew better than to put it there, and I would not have signed the page it was on.

He ruled it out for me in a letter, in neat columns, and at the foot of the columns stood the sum it would take, and the paying of it left me holding five thousand acres and next to no money, a condition I had no name for then and have never since been entirely out of.

He put it this way.

*Sir,*

*The columns are overleaf. I would ask you to read the right-hand one before the left. The left is what you have. The right is what it is going to want, and you will observe without any help from me that the two do not meet. They are not obliged to meet this year. I mention it because a man on a hill comes very quickly to reckon by the season, and a bank reckons by the quarter, and you are about to become the first while remaining, for the present, the property of the second.*

*On the house: I have not sold it. I have found a man who wishes to buy it. That is a different article and a slower one, and I have borrowed against his wishing. The term of that loan I have set down for you twice already and do not propose to set down a third time; you know it, and you did not care for it on either occasion, and I would be glad to be made a fool of by six months. You will want to know what becomes of us if the man changes his mind. He will not. His wife has been twice to measure the windows.*

*You will ask why I did not wait for the sale and buy the land out of the proceeds. I did not wait because your seller is an honest man dealing with a stranger at a fair price, and such an arrangement is not improved by being asked to stand about a twelvemonth while grander men finish their business. You would have told me so yourself, at greater length, and with a comparison to something in carpentry.*

*I have sent you a young man with the papers. He has no imagination whatever, and I did not want one who had. He will read out every parcel in the order I have given him and he will not be hurried. Let him finish. He is the only part of this business I have been able to send in person.*

*There is one item you will not find in either column. It is not an oversight.*

*I have engaged the carriers for your goods. They will want the better part of a fortnight for the last stage, the road being what you tell me it is, and I have paid them the half at this end so that the other half may be an argument you can have in person.*

*I remain, sir, your obedient servant,*

*Penworth.*

I read it twice at the low table under the eaves where I had written to him, once for the words and once for the columns, and then folded it back into its own creases and set it under the candlestick.

There was a second, shorter column under that one, and it was the one that mattered soonest. Land of that size does not change hands for the price of the land. It changes hands for the price of the land and then, at the turn of the year, for the Commonwealth's transfer duty on every acre of it, working and waste alike, levied on what a holding is reckoned to be worth, and taking no interest whatever in what it earns, which in my case was nothing. Penworth had the figure to the copper and set it down without comment, and I read past it that winter the way a man reads past a thing he has decided to be brave about, and it came due in its own time regardless.

That was the mercy in the thing, and the whole of the trap. So nothing at all fell due until the place had been given a full turn of the seasons to find its feet, which was not generosity but conveyancing, and it meant that in a year I would need this hill to be earning, one way or another. The farmland in the bottoms would have to be got into the hands of tenants who would work it and pay for the working. The trade would have to come. A wiser man would have looked at that year the way a quarryman looks at a lit fuse. It did not frighten me. I had wanted nothing at all for three years, and I found I wanted to see what that hill would do.

I lodged at Lira's while the paper went back and forth. I had thought, when I took the room, that I would want it a night or two and no more; I kept it the better part of the rest of the season. Lira let me the room by the week without comment, and fed me without comment, and formed her opinion of me as she formed all her opinions, out of sight and in her own time, and let it out a word at a time over the weeks, the way you let out a coat you mean to keep.

I asked her for it on the morning the agent went back down the road, standing at the end of the bar while she worked her way down a column of her own.

"I'll want the room a while yet," I said. "I can't tell you how long."

She finished the column before she answered me. "By the week, then."

"By the week."

"Same rate as it is." She turned the page and squared it with her thumb. "Baths are Thursdays. Your man paid a fortnight in advance and there's four days left of it. The mare's in the far stall."

I said I had not asked for the far stall.

"No," she said, and went on down the next column, and that was the end of the conversation.

The near stall, I found out later that week, has a draught along the floor that comes off the yard door and finds a horse's legs no matter where you tie her. Lira never mentioned it, then or afterward, and I never thanked her for it.

She kept a page in that book with my name at the head of it. On a Saturday she would turn the ledger round on the bar so that I could read what the week had come to, and wait while I read it, and turn it back when I had done, and go on with whatever she had been doing before. She never once said the figure aloud. In all the weeks I kept that room I never heard her ask anybody for money, and I never heard of anybody failing to pay her.

Ardenmoor took its time deciding about me, and was right to. For the first of those weeks I was served at Lira's on the instant and to the letter and with about the warmth of a signed receipt, which suited me well, for it asked nothing of me in return. I was the foolish and strange wizard who had bought Cael's hill, and that is a thing to be watched a while before it is a thing to be welcomed. So I did not hurry it. I paid what I owed on the day I owed it, and said good morning to men who did not say it back, and kept my strangeness folded up small and out of sight, and let the place look at me as long as it cared to.

Most of that looking got done across counters.

The dry-goods yard was the bank and the post and the parliament of the place all in the one low room, and I was in it three times that first week. The man who kept it had a wary courtesy about him that I came to like very much before I understood it. He served me exactly as fast as he served anybody and not one instant faster. Candles, lamp oil, a tin of tea, a whetstone, a coil of good cord, six yards of oilcloth. He weighed and cut and folded and then he opened a long book with a spine gone soft as cloth and wrote it all down in a small careful hand before he would take my money.

"You write down the ones who pay?" I said.

"I write down everything." He turned the book round so that I could read the line, and I read it, and it was right to the copper, and he turned it back. "Then there's no argument later, and no memories involved."

There were two men leaning at the far end of the counter the whole time, and they had stopped talking when I came in, and started again when they judged I had stopped being interesting. What they said was that he'd have the stone at the ford before the water came up, and that it was easy enough to say a thing like that in April. There was more feeling in it than the subject seemed to call for, and I could not have told you whose side either man was on.

I asked about the post. The man behind the counter said that anything I put in his hand before the Tuesday went down with the carrier on the Wednesday, and reached Wainford, weather permitting, and after Wainford it was the railway's business and no longer his. I said that suited me. He said it suited most people and then wrote that down too.

The oven took me longer to learn, and I learned it the hard way, standing in front of it with money in my hand.

Ardenmoor is too small to keep a baker. What it keeps instead is a clay and fieldstone oven open to the lane near the inn, and the households bring their dough to it and take their bread away again, and it is fired on certain days by whoever has the firing that week. I went down on one of those mornings because the smell of it came up the street, and I found a broad woman drawing loaves out with a peel as long as I am tall, and I asked her what a loaf cost.

She set the peel down.

"It doesn't cost," she said. "It's mine. That's my flour and my hands and my turn at the fire."

I said I had none of the three.

"No," she agreed. "You haven't."

Then she looked at me a while longer, and turned a loaf over on the board, and said that a woman she would not name had more dough than she had children to feed it to on a Thursday, and that if a certain amount of flour happened to arrive at that woman's door of a Wednesday, nobody would be able to account for how it got there, and there would very likely be a loaf too many by Thursday afternoon. Then she picked the peel back up and went at the next batch as though she had said nothing worth the hearing.

That is how I came to be fed bread in that valley for the better part of a year without ever once buying a loaf. It was the dark close-crumbed sort that will sit a week in a tin and cut thin at the end of it, and it was better than anything I had eaten in Vethmark, and I paid for the flour at the dry-goods counter and it went into the long book like everything else.

The mill was the third of them, down at the river on the hill-ward edge, and it taught me a different lesson.

I went for oats and bran for Snow, and stayed a while for the pleasure of the place. The yard smelled of wet meal and sawn oak in about equal parts. There were drying stacks under a lean-to along the length of the north side, three and four years' worth of timber ranked by size with the ends painted, and I stood and read those stacks as another man reads a shelf of books, and made a note of what I was going to want out of them before the summer was done.

A boy came out to me. He had a slate and a bit of chalk and he was not more than twelve, and he took my order on the slate and told me the price without going in to ask anybody.

"You're sure of that?" I said.

"It's the price," he said.

I asked whether they had seasoned oak enough to frame a small building, if a man wanted it later in the year. The boy went in through the mill door. Beyond it I could see a thin grey figure with his back to the yard, doing something to a shaft with a mallet, and the boy said his piece to that back, and the back said four words I did not catch, and the boy came out again.

"He says how small."

I said I did not know.

"He says come back when you do."

So I paid the boy and shouldered the sacks and carried them back along the river to Lira's yard, and it was two seasons and more before I stood face to face with the man who had cut the timber I built my life out of. Nobody in that yard thought there was anything unusual in that. Neither, after a while, did I.

Once, early, one of the quarrymen leaned over from the next bench, emboldened by his cups, and asked me straight out whether it was true I was a wizard, and what that meant a man could do. I told him it was a trade like his own, only with fewer men in it, and I bought him a drink.

He did not go away at once. He put his elbows on my table first and worked his way through the question of what a wizard might be for, and two of his fellows came over to help him with it, so that by the end there were four of us at a table meant for two.

Could I make it rain?

No man alive can make it rain, I said, and anybody who tells you different is after your money.

Could I find water, then? Sometimes. A good well-digger will find it cheaper and be less trouble about it afterward.

Could I do anything about the ford on the low road? I said the ford wanted a load of stone and two days of five men, and that nothing I knew how to do would carry stone.

That got the table's whole attention, and the biggest of them, a slow-spoken man with a thumb bound up in rag, said that he would carry stone anywhere in this world for a gold at the week's end, and uphill for a gold and a half, and that this was the entire science of the thing and always had been. The rest told him to sit down. He sat down and looked pleased with himself for some while after.

Could I stop a man's back aching? I said I could not, and that the herbalist up the street could, and would charge him less than I would.

"What's the use of you, then," said the one with the thumb, and meant no harm by it whatever, and the table laughed, and I laughed with them. It was a fair question, fairly put.

I told them I had spent twenty years drawing buildings and making things that had to work afterward. That was true, and they found it far more respectable than sorcery, and the talk went off toward roofs.

It came back once. Somebody down the far end wanted to know when I meant to start on the hill.

"When I know what I'm doing," I said.

That answer went round the room and was found wanting in about the way I expected. The one with the thumb observed that a man usually finds out what he is doing by doing it. A quieter man at the end of the bench said that a man usually finds out what he is doing about a fortnight after the wall comes down. Then somebody's opinion about a horse got loose at the next table and carried the whole company off with it, and I was left to my beer.

After that I was, if not a friend, then at least a known quantity, which in a village is the first step toward the rest.

I went up the hill every day of that fortnight, whether there was anything to do up there or not, which for a good while there was not. I walked the bounds until I knew them in the dark. I sat on the crown and let the valley go about its business below me and did a great deal of the particular nothing that looks, to a busy man passing, exactly like idleness, and is the slow work of coming to know a place well enough to ask it for something.

I paced the crown out, corner to corner and then across, and got one number on the Monday and a different number on the Tuesday, and could not make the two agree. Nothing up there is square and nothing up there is flat, whatever it looks like from the lane. A crown that reads as a table from below turns out, walked, to be a slow shallow dish with a lip on the west and no lip at all on the east, and that is the difference between where the wind finds you and where it does not.

On a wet morning I took a spade up and cut one turf on the north slope and laid it back on its face. There were four fingers of good black soil and then stone, and not a bedded, honest, quarryman's stone either but a rubble of it, close-packed and every piece of it lying whichever way it pleased. I put my hand down on it and it was cold, and it was stone, and it had nothing whatever to say to me. I put the turf back and trod the edges in and went on.

The heap was on the north side, a little under the crown and out of sight of anybody standing on it, and I came on it on the third or fourth day because Snow found it first and would not go past it. It was a low tumble of dressed stone gone green on the shaded faces, forty years down and more, settling into the grass, and it had been four walls once and a man's whole summer. I sat on it for an hour with my hat off. The stone was good. The dressing was good, better than good, square and true and done by somebody who knew exactly what he was about. There was old mortar still in some of the joints and it came away between my finger and thumb like dry cheese, and it had never once, in all those years, set as mortar sets.

I did not touch anything else. His son was alive and down in the village, and I had not the first notion yet of what I would have done in his place.

Other days came to nothing anybody could have pointed at. I lay on my back on the crown in the middle of a Thursday with a book on my chest that the wind kept opening at the wrong page, and gave up on the book, and watched a pair of buzzards work the updraught off the west face for the better part of an hour without once beating a wing. I walked the ground after rain and marked where the water stood and where it ran off clean and where it went in and did not come out again. I ate my lunch on a different stone every day for a fortnight, on the theory that a place shows you a different face from every seat in it.

Cael's grandson took to appearing at the edges of it, never on the crown itself and never quite trespassing, only there of a sudden at the foot of the track or along the edge of the woodland, watching, the way a cat sits at the exact edge of a room it has been told to keep out of. I let him watch. A boy has a right to watch a hill his family has held forty years.

The second or third time, I put my hand up to him across the length of the crown. He put his up back, and stayed where he was.

The time after that I called down that he could come up if he liked.

"I know," he said, and did not come.

So I let it be, and went back to my pacing, and he watched me pace, and after a while he went home. That was the first week.

On the Friday he came as far as the foot of the track while I was coming down it, so that we met. He stood aside to let me by and then did not let me by.

"Whose is the track?" he said.

"Mine, I'm told."

He thought about that. "Grandda walks it on a Sunday. He's walked it always."

"Then he'll go on walking it."

"You'd not stop him."

"I'd not stop him."

He nodded once, slowly, and I thought that was the end of it, and I had my foot on the next stone down when he said, in exactly the same flat voice, "Can I?"

I never heard him ask permission for anything again, in all the years after.

"You've been coming up here since before I ever heard of the place," I said.

"That's not the same now."

"No," I said. "It isn't." And then, because he was standing there waiting for the true answer and not the kind one, I said, "Come up whenever you like. Sundays and every other day. If I'm working and you're quiet you'll not bother me, and if I'm not working you'll not bother me either."

"All right," he said, and went off down the track, and did not come up for two days.

When he did come, he came about forty paces short of the crown, to a flat-topped stone half sunk in the turf on the east side, and sat down on it. He came to that stone the next day and the day after that. It became his in the way things become a boy's, without a word said by anybody, and for the rest of that fortnight, and a good while after it, our whole acquaintance was that I worked on the crown and he sat on his stone, and now and then one of us said something and the other one answered it.

What I was truly waiting on, that fortnight, was my life to come up out of Vethmark and find me.

I had sent for it the day the handshake was dry, and there was not much of it. A man who has lived three years alone in his parents' house learns how little of it he touches. I had left the quiet rooms of that Vethmark house as I had kept them, sheeted and still and full of the furniture of a life I no longer led, and I had sent Penworth's carters through it with a short list, and what came up the valley road was the short list and nothing more: my books, which were many; the tools and instruments of my trade, which were fewer but more particular; a bed, a chair, a chest of clothes, a particular kettle I preferred, the plain machinery of one man keeping himself alive. And one thing more, which rode alone.

The wagons came up on a grey wet afternoon, groaning and lurching along the last of the road into the little village. I went down to meet them at the door of the inn, and Soren came from wherever he kept himself and fell in at my shoulder, and the two of us and the two drivers spent the next two wet hours carrying my life into the corner of the inn's stable Lira had let me for the task.

The two drivers had come the whole fortnight of the last stage and had opinions about it. The older one told me, before he told me anything else, that the last four miles were not a road, whatever the map called them, and that he had walked at his horses' heads for the most of it. The younger one said nothing at all the whole afternoon.

We worked out of the wagons and in under the stable eaves and back again, and the rain came down steady and grey and got into everything that was not oilcloth. Lira's yard turned to a brown soup inside the first quarter hour. The stable corner she had let me was dry and swept and had a chalk line down the flags where her own tack ended and my life was to begin.

I had Penworth's list. Penworth had itemized my possessions as another man itemizes a cargo, in a hand so small and even that I had to hold it to the light, and every crate had a number chalked on two sides so that a man could read it whichever way it came off the tail. I stood in the wet with that list and called the numbers and the drivers called them back, and not one thing on it was missing. After a fortnight of that road a full tally is not luck. It is somebody else's care, and I knew whose.

Snow had the far stall and did not care for any of it. Two strange geldings in the yard, a stranger's boots on the flags, and her own bedding trodden about by men who did not consult her. She put her head over and laid her ears back at the whole proceeding at intervals through the afternoon, and once, when the younger driver went past her with a crate on his shoulder, took hold of the corner of his collar and let it go again.

The books were the worst of it. Books are the heaviest thing a man owns and the least willing to be carried, and there were fourteen crates of them, and the third one from the tail had shifted somewhere in that long fortnight out of Wainford and cracked a board, so that we went the rest of the way with it held together by a rope and my hand under it. Nothing was spoiled. Some of the books in the top layer had gone soft at the edges with the damp and dried later with a wave in the paper.

The kettle came out of a nest of straw in a crate of kitchen things, and I took it out and looked at it and set it aside from the rest, on the ledge above the chalk line, where I could see it.

Lira came out twice. The first time she brought a jug of something hot and set it on a barrel-head and went in again without a word. The second time she stood in the stable door for a moment with her arms folded and said, "That wall sweats in a wet spell. Stack a hand's width off it." Then she went in again. We shifted everything we had already stacked and did it her way, and she was right, and the wall sweated all that summer, and nothing of mine ever took a mark.

The boy worked without being told and without complaint, and had a good eye for how a load wants to sit, and I marked it and said nothing, for I had already learned that saying a thing to that boy was the surest way to make him careful of it.

The books had come up to my room in crates I trusted to no one else, the better part of a lifetime's reading and a fair share of its writing. I stacked them in no order at all and knew already the long good evenings I would spend giving them one. My instruments I was more careful of. They are fewer than folk imagine and stranger, the fine-drawn things a man in my line uses to feel for what is already in a place before ever he asks it for anything: gauges and glasses and lengths of drawn wire that mean nothing to the eye and a great deal to the hand, each wrapped in its own cloth against the jolting of the road. I laid them out under cover and ran my hands over them in the failing light, taking their count, and it came to me that I had not opened most of these cases in three years. I had packed them for a study in another life, and they had never got there.

Near the end, when the light was going and the last of the rain had begun to clear, the drivers brought up the last thing between them, slung on a plank because neither would carry it alone, and set it down where I told them and stepped back from it a little more quickly than its weight accounted for.

It was not large. One man could have carried it. The drivers had chosen not to, and I did not blame them. It was old, older than anything else I owned, black wood banded in a metal that was not iron and had never rusted. It had no keyhole any locksmith would know and no lock any key would turn. The lock on it was mine, and was not the kind you open with your hands. I had made that binding years before, in another life, and had not unmade it, and did not mean to. What was inside it was mine. I had carried it out of the wreck of everything into that dark sheeted house, and now up a bad road to a wet hill.

Soren watched me set it under the canvas, apart from the rest, and did not ask about it. He was the only soul in that valley, then or for a long time after, who never once asked. I gave him a coin for the afternoon worth more than the afternoon. He looked at it, and looked at me, and took it.

The drivers took their wagons away before the light was gone, glad, I think, to be quit of the village and the rain and the chest, and Soren went off down the track to his grandfather's fire. I stood in the late of the afternoon with the sun just breaking through the grey and everything I owned in the world stacked in oilcloth behind me, and looked at my hill, and did not want to go back down to the inn.

So I fetched Snow and went up my hill to make a camp. I had a good tent of waxed canvas that had kept the weather off me, and I pitched it near the crown in the lee of the one shoulder of ground that offered any. I dug a fire-pit and coaxed a fire out of the dry heart of wood the rain had not yet reached, and set my kettle in the coals and made tea in the smell of woodsmoke and wet grass and canvas, which is one of the oldest and kindest smells there is. I ate bread and cheese carried up from Lira's, and sat in the door of my tent with the fire dying and the valley below me putting out its lamps one window at a time. I had not sat still like that, on purpose, since a letter came three weeks late in another spring.

I had not meant to move onto the hill for good. I had a dry room a mile down the track with a woman who cooked, and only a fool trades that for wet canvas on a whim. But I stayed that night, and then the next, and inside of a week I had made the crown a kind of home. I set stones round the fire-pit and learned which way the wind came over the shoulder of the ground and shifted the tent twice before I had it right.

The first pitch was too near the lip on the west, where I had taken the shoulder of ground for a shelter it did not turn out to be, because the view from there was the best on the hill and I am as much a fool as anybody. The wind came over the shoulder in the small hours, got under the weather flap, and worried at it all night like a dog with a rag. Somewhere past two it took a peg clean out. I went out in my shirt in the dark to put it back, could not find the peg in the wet grass, drove my own good knife in instead, and lay the rest of the night listening to it take the strain.

The second pitch was in the proper lee, twenty paces down and east. It was quiet as a church. It also stood in the run of every drop of water that came off the crown. I learned that in one wet night and moved again in the morning without any argument with myself.

The third pitch was right. Out of the wind, out of the water, with the doorway looking east so that the first of the light came in and woke me before I had time to decide against it. I cut a shallow trench round the uphill side with the spade and turfed the spoil against the canvas, and after that I could lie in there in a downpour and hear it going past me on both sides.

The fire-pit I dug a stride from the door and ringed with stones I carried up from the scree on the north slope, twelve of them, chosen for a flat face and a bit of belly, and it took me three goes before I had a ring that would sit a kettle level and shelter a flame at the same time. That kettle was the one thing I had brought across the world that I could not have told you a sound reason for. It was black past cleaning, and the lid had wanted a wire to hold it since before my father died. It boiled fast and it poured clean and it had made tea in rooms I would not stand in again. Set on stone above a fire I had built with my own hands, it looked at home. I did not, yet.

I worked out where the water stood after rain and where it ran off clean. I came to know the hill the only way anybody comes to know a place: by sleeping on it and waking on it and being rained on by it in the dark. Soren took to bringing my bread up of a morning so I need not go down for it, a thing neither of us ever named and both of us understood.

It started on a cold bright morning at the end of that first week, with the kettle just off and the fire not yet worth the name. I heard him on the track before I saw him. He came up over the last of it with a cloth bundle held against his chest in both arms and his breath going in front of him, set the bundle down on the flat stone by the fire, and stood back from it.

"Bread," he said.

It was still warm through the cloth. I said that was a long way to carry a loaf uphill before breakfast.

"It's on my way."

It was not on his way. Nothing on that hill is on anybody's way. I did not say so, and he did not offer any further account of himself, and the bread sat between us on the stone going cold.

I poured him tea in the tin cup, since I had only the one good one and was using it, and he took it and drank it standing. He looked at the tent, and at the trench I had cut round it, and at the ring of stones.

"You moved it."

"Twice."

"It's better there."

"It is."

He finished the tea and set the cup upside down on the stone, so the rain would not get into it. That had not once occurred to me in twenty years of camps. Then he went off down the track at a good pace, and did not say where to.

The next day he came again, and the day after. I offered him a coin for it the third time. He looked at the coin, and then at me, and did not take it, and did not explain. He had taken the coin for the wagons quick enough. Work was work and could be paid for. This was something else, and it took me some while to catch up to it, and he offered me no help at all.

It was the first household I had kept in three years, and I was slow to notice how much good it was doing me, the way a man is slow to notice a fever breaking.

I should have been thinking about the work. I had bought the hill to build on it, after all, and Jorin's name was already in my mind, and there was a study to raise before autumn if I meant to have a roof over my instruments before the weather turned in earnest. But I found I was in no hurry to begin, and it took me a while to understand why, and when I did I was careful not to say it aloud even to myself, because put into words it sounded like a man making excuses for his own idleness. It was this. Every soul in the valley knew the hill had thrown off every roof and wall ever set on it, and I knew it too, from Cael, to my face. A sensible man meaning to prove them wrong would have come up his first morning with his sleeves rolled and gone at the ground the way Cael's father had, deeper and harder and more stubborn, and would have got, I do not doubt, exactly what Cael's father got. I am not always a sensible man, but I have learned a few things dearly, and one of them is that when a place has said no to every question ever put to it, the fault may lie in the questions. I did not know what I meant to ask that hill. I only knew I would not ask it until I understood the ground well enough to ask it properly, and that a whole summer spent learning to ask would come cheaper than a single wall thrown up in ignorance and pulled down again in the night. So I sat, and walked, and slept on it, and let the village think me idle.

It was some nights into that new life, and not the first, that the hill spoke to me.

The fire had gone out, and I had lain down, and it began. I did not hear anything. I want to be exact, because it matters, and because I had spent a lifetime learning the difference between hearing a thing and what people mean when they say they heard. There was no sound. Had you stood beside me on the crown that night with your own two ears you would have sworn the hill dead silent, and as far as ears go you would have been right.

But I was lying full length on the ground with nothing between me and it but a groundsheet and a blanket, and it came up into me through the bone. It was below sound the way the lowest note of a great bell is below sound, the part you feel in the chest and the back teeth a moment before the ringing reaches you, except that the ringing never came. It was steady. It was slow. It had the patience of something very large and very old turning over in its sleep, and I lay in the dark and felt the hill I had bought move once, low, against the whole length of my body, and go still, and I told myself it was the blood in my own ears, and did not believe it, and lay very still and waited to feel it again.

It came again near the turning of the night, in the coldest hour, the hour before any bird. A single slow pulse up through the ground, no louder and no less, and this time I told myself nothing at all. I lay with my eyes open in the dark of the tent, the chest a black weight at my back, and listened with the whole of myself, the way I had not listened to anything in three years, and the hill said its one deep wordless thing to the underside of me and fell quiet.

And I understood, lying there, that Cael's grandmother had been telling the plain truth for forty years, and that most of the valley had spent those forty years calling a wise woman a fool, and being polite about it, and that I owed her, whoever she had been, an apology I could never pay.

There was a second thought under that one, smaller, and I did not look straight at it, the way you do not look straight at a mark on your own skin you have only just found and do not yet want to give a name. It was only this: that on all my daylight walks and idle sittings the hill had been silent as any hill in the world, and that it kept, it seemed, its own two hours, the failing of the light and the cold before the dawn, and that I had never before lain still upon it, in the dark, at an hour it chose to speak. That was all it was. I said as much to myself, more than once, in the cold, and mostly I believed it.

I did not sleep much the rest of that night, and what I got was thin. But I was not afraid. I kept waiting to be, lying there in the dark taking my own measure, and the fear never came. I had come up the valley a man who wanted nothing he could lose, and I had lain down on a hill no one had ever been able to build on, and the hill had turned in its long sleep and felt the weight of me and answered. Whatever else that was, it was not nothing. I had a great deal of nothing by then.

I lay in the cold before dawn with my hand flat on the ground, the way the boy had laid his flat on the stone, and I asked the hill nothing, because I had not learned how, and because there are questions a man does not put to a thing so soon after coming to sleep in its house. I only kept it company in the dark, the way Cael kept the foot of it company from his wall, and the boy kept its edges, and half that quiet valley had been keeping this hill company the whole of their lives without ever once saying so aloud.

Below me the river ran on. Out in the north wood the old oak stood in the rain. And on the crown of the hill, in a tent of waxed canvas, I lay awake and waited for the light, and wanted, for the first time in a long while and to my own surprise, to see what the morning would bring.

It brought rain, mostly, and a grey ordinary daylight in which the hill was only a hill again, wet and green and patient and giving no sign of anything, so that a lesser man, or a wiser one, might have put the whole of the night down to cold and tiredness and the fancies that come to a man alone in a tent. I did not. I had lain awake through two slow beats of a buried heart and felt them in my teeth, and no amount of sensible morning was going to talk me back out of it. I made my tea. I ate the bread the boy brought up. Then I went out into the rain with my hands empty, and began.
