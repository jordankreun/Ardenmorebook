# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Thirty-One: The Letter

On the eve of the solstice the valley went quiet early, the way it does when the cold means it, smoke standing straight from every chimney and the snow squeaking underfoot, and I banked my fire in the Study and found that the evening had left me alone with the drawer.

I had known the letter was in the house since the front of the winter. It had come up with the ordinary post in the first hard week of December, before the fire, in a hand I knew the moment the boy put the bundle in mine: a woman's hand, old-fashioned, upright, the hand that had written the labels on a hundred jars of preserves in a city larder I had raided as a welcome guest in another life. Her mother. I had put it, unopened, in the lower right drawer of the desk, the sticking one, with the callipers and the two unread pages of the inventory and, this last week, Cecily's report, and I had told myself the winter's honest excuse, which was the fire and the ash and the hundred mendings of a burned-out man's days, and the winter had let me keep the excuse, and now the year had run down to its shortest evening and the excuse was spent.

Cecily is the somebody discreet, and she is good, and her report had come up a week past under Penworth's cover, in the dark of December. It was short. The two who had signed their names were traced and were what such men always are, a licensing agent overreaching and a collector of other men's methods, and had been warned off with the particular finality Penworth's office reserves for such warnings, and have not troubled me since.

The unsigned hand she could not trace. The letters had come through three different posting towns, none twice; the paper was common; the hand itself, she wrote, was disciplined enough to be nobody's natural hand at all. And they had stopped. Not dwindled. Stopped, back in the high summer, the month her inquiry began, the way a light goes out when a door down the corridor opens. Her last lines before the courtesies I will give as she wrote them, because they are not mine to soften.

*Whoever they are, they were not taught your notation from any paper now under seal; the seals are sound and the storage unbreached, and I have verified both. But you have told me the rooms stood four years before the crating with no watch on them but a housekeeper's monthly airing, and I am obliged to tell you that a hand this trained did not learn from stolen glances. It learned at leisure, at a desk, with time to practice. I would look, when you are able to look, at who had leisure in that house.*

The thought you have just had, I had it that same night, with the ash smell still in everything I owned. Half a year silent, and a week of roads distant, and a barn gone up at the loft end in a dry month. I heard the thought out, the way you hear out a witness who was not there, and let it go; a flue and green hay and a gusting wind ask less of the world than a hand that patient walking four days past the end of the railway to burn a man's hay. But I did not file her report in the shallow drawer with the licences, where the office's letter stood about being interested. It went into the sticking one, which is where I keep the things that are not yet ready to be true, and that drawer was becoming a crowded parish.

But it was the other letter I had come to the drawer for.

Everything I owned that winter smelled of the fire. That is not a figure. Smoke had gone into the blankets and the books' top edges and the good wool of my coat, and airing in the cold only rested it, and I had stopped noticing it weeks since except at moments when I noticed nothing else, and this was one: I opened the drawer, and the drawer stuck and argued and came, and the smell of my burned house rose off everything in it, and under the smell, when I lifted the letter out, was the letter's own smell, which was clean paper and a faint dry ghost of lavender, a larder smell, a linen-press smell, the smell of a house where things are kept properly because keeping is a form of love. I sat with it a while at the desk. Then I did not read it at the desk. I took it to the chair by the fire, because some letters are documents and some letters are guests, and I had kept this guest standing a fortnight and more in a cold drawer already.

I am not going to describe breaking the seal, because I do not remember it. What I remember is that the letter was short, one side of one sheet in that upright hand, and that I had braced myself the way you brace for grief and found I had braced in the wrong direction entirely, and that is the thing about the letters we cannot open. We seal them ourselves. We fill them, in the dark of a drawer, with everything we cannot carry, and then we are astonished to open them at last and find only what was put in.

What was in it was this. I will give it whole, because it is not mine to summarize.

*Emlyn.*

*Penworth writes that the house is sold at last, and that her things are crated under his seal, and safe, and that you have built something on a hill a long way off. He is a careful man and tells me little else, and I do not ask, because I would rather have it from you.*

*I am writing to ask you to come in the spring. Not for a visit. For the crates. It is time they were opened, and I find I cannot do it, and I have understood at last, this autumn, that I have been waiting because it is not work for one person. It never was. It is work for the two people who loved her most, at one table, with the whole of a day to fail at it and start again.*

*Come in the spring, then, and bring whatever of her still lives in your keeping. I know there is something. There is always something; I have her mother's spoons. You have carried yours alone these six years, and I have carried mine, and I think we have both learned everything the carrying has to teach.*

*The garden has gone on without her, which I did not forgive it for the first year, and do now. Come and see it. Come in the spring, Emlyn, and we will open the crates, and I will feed you until you are sensible.*

*With my love, which you have always had, and my patience, which you have tested,*

*Hesper.*

I sat with it a long time. I would be lying if I said I sat quietly the whole of it, and that is as much as I will say; a man's chair by his own fire on the longest night of the year is one of the few courts left where he may be as he is, unwitnessed, and I was, for a while, as I was.

Six years I had treated my grief as a thing that had to be defended, a keep to be held, walls and a locked gate and me on the one wall, garrison of one. Every unopened thing in my life, the letter, the crates, the inventory, the chest twelve feet under my chair, I had held the way a besieged man holds a gate, certain that what was outside the walls was loss in its final form, the kind you do not survive, and that the holding was the surviving.

You do not tell a grief what to be. I had built my whole late life on the ground's version of that sentence and had never once, in six years, thought to walk it back up my own stairs and in at my own door. You ask. And you get out of the way earlier than you think you ought.

The fire had burned down to its steady heart. Outside the window the valley lay black and white under no moon at all, the solstice dark, the year's floor, and one by one, as I sat there, the last late lights of Ardenmoor went out, Lira's amber last of all, until the only light burning in the whole of that valley was the lumen at my elbow, keeping the hill's hours, dimming toward the deep of night the way it does, waiting as it had always waited for the hour before the birds, and I sat in the last light in the valley with a letter on my knee and listened to my own house tick and settle, and somewhere below the floor, patient beyond every patience I have ever failed to learn, the ground I lived on gathered itself toward the one hour of the year when its long word came almost right.

I did not decide anything. I want that written plainly. Deciding is a daylight word, and what happened that night was not a decision, it was a door coming to the end of its argument. I stood up, at some hour I could not name, with the letter still in my hand. I banked the fire out of forty years of habit. I took the lumen off its shelf, and it came with me the way it had come with me through every dark of three winters, throwing my shadow huge up the Study wall and then folding it down the stairwell ahead of me.

And I took the key.

I have called it a key from the first and I will not stop now. It was never metal. The lock I had set on that chest in another life answers no iron. It answers a question, the same one question, asked with the whole of a man, and for six years I had carried the question the way you carry a key to a house you cannot bear to visit, knowing its exact weight in the pocket, never once taking it out. *Are you ready to be answerable for what you keep?* That is the whole key. It had been cut the day I sealed the chest, and I had carried it every day since, and it had never once fit, because I had never once, until a woman's letter on a solstice eve, been able to hold it steady.

The stairs took me down the way they always did, cold coming up to meet warmth going down.

The cellar was black and still and mine, and the corner was where the corner had always been, and I set the lumen on the floor beside the chest, and knelt, and laid the letter down beside the lamp where it could see, if letters see, and put both my hands flat on the old black wood for the first time in three years, and above me the house was quiet, and around me the hill was quiet, the deep quiet before its deepest hour, the whole patient ground of my life holding its breath in the dark, and I asked.
