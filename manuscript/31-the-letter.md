# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Thirty-One: The Letter

On the eve of the solstice the valley went quiet early, when the cold means it, smoke standing straight from every chimney and the snow squeaking underfoot, and I banked my fire in the Study and found that the evening had left me alone with the drawer.

The Study had been my workroom for two years and more and had been the whole of my house since the second week of December. I had come into it as a man comes into a boat, with what was in my hands. The cot stood along the north wall under the window, borrowed, narrow, and I had not stopped thinking of it as somebody else's; I made it every morning out of a habit my mother put in me, and it never once looked made. My coat hung on a peg Jorin had set for aprons. The kettle sat on the hearth-stone within reach of the chair, black as the inside of a flue, which is very nearly what it had lately been in. My books stood in stacks along the east wall in the beginning of an order, which is a thing I have been beginning most of my life and have never yet finished, and that beginning had got three stacks along and stopped there and had not moved in a month.

A fortnight had gone into working out where things live in a room never built to hold a life. The bread and the cheese lived down the stair with the rest of the winter, so that supper was a small expedition with a lamp in it. Water stood in a covered pail by the door and took a skin of ice most nights before I slept. I ate at the desk with a board laid over the papers, and washed in what the kettle would spare, and hung the towel on the chair-back where the fire could reach it, and everything else I did in that room I did cold. There was one chair. I had stopped resenting the arrangement somewhere about the middle of the month and had got as far as a dry respect for its arithmetic.

The cold that night was the sort that has made up its mind. The fire's reach ended about a yard out from the hearth and the rest of the room kept the room's own temperature, which was the hill's: in December, the temperature of a cellar. Ink thickens at that temperature. The inside face of the window carried a grey bloom of ice that had been coming on since the middle of the afternoon, and I had watched it come the way you watch a slow tide, doing nothing about it, because there was nothing to do about it.

The dark had come down a little after three. It had been coming down earlier every day for a month and this was the day it stopped coming earlier, the floor of the year, and past the window there was nothing between the glass and the sky. The lumen stood on its shelf at my elbow keeping the hill's hours as it has kept them since the summer I first set it there, warm through the dusk-fail, easing down through the middle of the night. It made a circle on the desk about the size of a dinner plate and left the rest of the room to the fire, and between the two of them the walls got a little light and the corners got none.

I had known the letter was in the house since the front of the winter. It had come up with the ordinary post in the first hard week of December, before the fire, in a hand I knew the moment the boy put the bundle in mine: a woman's hand, old-fashioned, upright, the hand that had written the labels on a hundred jars of preserves in a city larder I had raided as a welcome guest in another life. Her mother. I had put it, unopened, in the lower right drawer of the desk, the sticking one, with the callipers and the two unread pages of the inventory and, this last week, Cecily's report, and I had told myself the winter's honest excuse, which was the fire and the ash and the hundred mendings of a burned-out man's days, and the winter had let me keep the excuse, and now the year had run down to its shortest evening and the excuse was spent.

The mendings were real. I want to be fair to them, because a man's excuses are generally made out of true things. Half the valley came up the second morning without being sent for, and stayed the day.

They came up in the dark end of it, before there was light enough to work by, and I heard them a long time before I saw them: boots on stone far down the hill, and then nearer, and then the particular knock a bucket makes against a leg on a slope. Nobody came to the Study door. There was nothing to come about. By the time I was out with my coat half on there were lanterns strung along the flags from the elbow up, and the first of them were already standing at the edge of the black ground with their hands under their arms, looking at it, letting their eyes get used to what the daylight was going to show them anyway.

The frost had been hard in the night and the ash had frozen where the buckets left it. It came up in plates for the first hour and then, once the sun had been on it, in a wet grey putty that stuck to everything and dried pale on the backs of hands. The middle of the barn's ash was still warm at that depth and they left that part of it alone another day, without anybody deciding to, in the manner of people who have all grown up around fires. Jorin set two trestles out of his own saw pit at the top of the yard and laid boards across them, and that was where things went. Kellard's cart stood at the head of the flags all day with its shafts down. Somebody had brought a flour sieve out of a kitchen, and it was the best tool on the hill and went round all morning without one word of ceremony about it.

Half a valley is half a valley, and the half that came were the ones with a free day and the right boots, and a good many of them I never saw arrive and never saw go. Tomas raked one end of the barn out for three hours with his coat off in a frost, and said nothing to anybody at all, and drank what was put into his hand, and went down. Bess Wray had the Study hearth going before seven and something on it all day, and fed people in relays, and would not sit. Soren and two of the Wray children were put on the carrying, because carrying is the safe work and there was a good deal of it, and they went between the trestle and the cart all morning until their faces were the colour of the ash. Cael came up on his stick and sat on the paddock rail out of the road, in his coat, next to my horse, and stayed there the best part of the day, and I knew what he was doing there and left him to do it. Brenna went through the glasshouse first, being the only person on that hill with living stock to lose, and then came up and worked the trestle, and it was Brenna who decided what could be washed, because somebody had to decide and nobody else wanted the job.

There was no ceremony about any of it and there was very little talk. What there was was the noise of a rake on stone, and a full bucket set down, and the small flat sound of an iron thing put on a board beside other iron things. I was off the hill for an hour of that afternoon on an errand of my own, and came back up and found that the work had not so much as noticed. At dusk they went down the way with their lanterns in ones and twos, in the exact reverse of the night of the fire, and I stood at the top and did not call anything after them.

After that there was a fortnight of the work a fire leaves behind, which nobody warns you about because everybody assumes the burning is the hard part. Digging in wet ash for what the ash was holding. Laying out on a trestle in the yard the things that had come through, and looking at them, and dividing them into what could be washed and what could not. A coat relined. Boots ordered off a fortnight of road, and borrowed boots meanwhile that were a size out. Glass for a window and a hinge for a door and a list, twice written, of what a man needs in order to go on being a man in a room.

Digging in ash is not much like digging. The rake takes the top of it and then you are down on your knees with your hands, because a rake breaks what you are hunting for, and because after the first hour you can feel through a glove what you cannot see. What the ash was holding was mostly iron. Nails, in rows, standing up where the boards had lain, so that a burned floor gives you back its own plan if you are patient with it and do not walk over it first. Hinges. The stove's door, off its pintle, eight feet from the stove. A knife with the scales burned off the tang. Glass gone into shapes glass has no business making. And crockery everywhere, in pieces the size of a thumbnail, because crockery neither burns nor survives, and I picked bits of my own plates out of that ground for a fortnight and could not have told you one meal I ever ate off them.

The trestle was the harder part, and it was hard in a direction I had not braced for, because it was not loss. It was arithmetic. You lay a thing on a board and look at it and put it in one of two places. Left went iron, stone, glass, the brass foot of a lamp, the flat-iron, anything with nothing in it to hold a smell. Right went wool and paper and leather and anything with glue in its making, and there is no sense in arguing about the right-hand board, and the arguing is what you have to watch in yourself. I argued twice. Once over a wooden box of no value whatever, and once over something else, and both times somebody at my elbow let me finish and then quietly put the thing where it belonged, and I was grateful for it by the afternoon and am grateful for it yet.

There is also a third place. Nobody sets it out and everybody uses it. It is the far end of the board, and what goes on it is what came through whole and has no use left in the world, and I took two things off that end and could not have defended either of them to a court, and have not thrown them out since.

A coat relined is four words. What it was, was two evenings with the coat over my knee and the old lining out of it in strips on the floor and my thumb sore from pushing the needle with it instead of with the thimble I did not own. The wool of the outside was sound; smoke does not eat wool, it only lives in it. The lining had taken the heat somewhere in that quarter hour of going in and out at the north door, and had gone the colour of weak tea and the texture of a moth's work, and it came away in my hands like something long buried. I cut the new one out of a shirt past mending and set it in with a running stitch no tailor would show to anybody, and the seam under the left arm is a disgrace to this day, and that coat kept me warm for another four winters.

The borrowed boots were a size out, and a size out in a boot is a whole education. You walk differently. You go down a slope with your toes braced and up it with your heel loose, and by evening you have been using muscles that have no business in a day's work, and you get them off at the fire and find you have been walking about as somebody else since morning. A twist of wool in each toe helped and did not settle it. The new ones came off a fortnight of road and were four days late on top of the fortnight, and were the plainest boots I have ever owned, and I do not believe I have ever been better pleased with anything I paid money for.

The glass and the hinge were not fire at all, and they belong in the list because they belong to the same fortnight and the same discovery. It showed itself in small faults I had walked past for two years without seeing them. The window over the cot had a pane starred corner to corner from some knock long before my time in it, and it had never once mattered, and the first night I slept under it, it mattered every hour. The door had dropped a hair on its lower hinge. A workroom door is a thing you go through; a house door is a thing you shut behind you, and it turns out you find that out in one night. The glass came off what the glasshouse had left over, cut down. The hinge came up the hill from the street in a few days, and I hung it myself on an afternoon, and neither job was difficult, and the evening after it that room was a different room.

The list I wrote twice, and the difference between the two is the only honest measure I have of that fortnight.

The first I wrote on the third evening at the desk, with the ash still under my nails, and I wrote it the way writing goes when writing is the only orderly thing within reach. It ran down one side of a sheet and onto the back. It had a bed on it and a chair and a table and a press and a lamp and a second lamp, and blankets, and a jug and a basin, and a pot and a pan and a kettle I already owned, and it had, further down, where the hand had begun to go long and loose, a fair number of things I did not need and had never in my life owned and had put down because putting them down was the work and I did not want the work to stop.

The second I wrote a week later, standing up, on the back of a bill, and it had four things on it. Boots that fitted. A coat with a lining in it. Something to sit on that was not the bed. And firewood enough not to have to think about firewood.

What happened between the two lists was that I found out I had been living in the room for a week already. I had eaten in it and slept in it and got up in it and gone out of it to work and come back into it in the dark, and it had held me, with no bed in it, no press, no second lamp. Whether that is a comfort or an indictment I never did settle with myself, and I have stopped needing to.

Four evenings of that fortnight I spent copying out of my head what the fire had taken out of my notebooks, which is a thing worth doing and is also a thing you can do for as many evenings as you have.

I did it with a new book on the desk and the lumen pulled over, ruling the margin first out of an old habit, because a ruled margin makes a page believe it is going to be filled. What comes back easiest is the arithmetic; numbers keep in a head. What comes back after that is a shape, an order, my own notation and the way I lay it out, so that I could rebuild a page and be certain of the page and not certain of one word on it. What does not come back at all is the day. A working note is half a note and half a weather report and half a record of what my hands were doing wrong that particular morning, and the first two of those are recoverable and the third is not, and the third is generally what sends you back to the book in the first place.

So I left gaps. I ruled a short line where a line was missing and put a small mark in the margin against it, and by the fourth evening the marks outnumbered the entries on some pages and I stopped counting them. I did not afterwards fill in one of those gaps out of the plausible, and I am prouder of that than of the copying.

I wrote to Penworth about matters that could have waited until Candlemas and he answered by return, which tells you what he made of the letters. My hands smelled of ash for a week under the nails whatever I did to them. And through the whole of it the drawer sat under my elbow at the height of my knee, and I knew to the inch where it was, the way you know where a bruise is without touching it.

Cecily is the somebody discreet, and she is good, and her report had come up a week past under Penworth's cover, in the dark of December. It was short. The two who had signed their names were traced and were what such men always are, a licensing agent overreaching and a collector of other men's methods, and had been warned off with the particular finality Penworth's office reserves for such warnings, and have not troubled me since.

The unsigned hand she could not trace. The letters had come through three different posting towns, none twice; the paper was common; the hand itself, she wrote, was disciplined enough to be nobody's natural hand at all. And they had stopped. Not dwindled. Stopped, back in the high summer, the month her inquiry began, the way a light goes out when a door down the corridor opens. Her last lines before the courtesies I will give as she wrote them, because they are not mine to soften.

*Whoever they are, they were not taught your notation from any paper now under seal; the seals are sound and the storage unbreached, and I have verified both. But you have told me the rooms stood four years before the crating with no watch on them but a housekeeper's monthly airing, and I am obliged to tell you that a hand this trained did not learn from stolen glances. It learned at leisure, at a desk, with time to practice. I would look, when you are able to look, at who had leisure in that house.*

The thought you have just had, I had it that same night, with the ash smell still in everything I owned. Half a year silent, and a week of roads distant, and a barn gone up at the loft end in a dry month. I heard the thought out, the way you hear out a witness who was not there, and let it go; a flue and green hay and a gusting wind ask less of the world than a hand that patient walking four days past the end of the railway to burn a man's hay. But I did not file her report in the shallow drawer with the licences, where the office's letter stood about being interested. It went into the sticking one, which is where I keep the things that are not yet ready to be true, and that drawer was becoming a crowded parish.

That desk came up the valley with my books in the first summer, and it is not a fine piece, only oak and heavy and honest. The lower right drawer has never in its life pulled clean. The runners are oak on oak, fitted by somebody in a dry season, and the wood has been getting the better of the fitting ever since. There is a trick to it that I could do half asleep. You take the pull in both hands and lift the front of the drawer a quarter inch against its own weight, and ease it left, and then draw it straight, and if you do that it comes grudging and complaining and comes. If you hurry it, it locks itself like a wheel in a rut and you may stand there and swear at it as long as you like. In wet weather it does not come at all.

The trick works and the hurrying does not, and I have hurried it a few hundred times, generally at the end of a long day and nearly always for something I could as well have fetched in the morning. It jams at about an inch and a quarter out, canted, the front standing proud on the left, and at that point there is nothing to be done but push it home and begin again, and beginning again is exactly what somebody in a hurry has decided not to do. I have stood over that drawer in a temper on a wet evening and heard myself doing it, and gone and stood at the window until I was fit to come back and lift it properly.

I had told myself for years that it wanted twenty minutes and a plane. I owned a plane. I had twenty minutes on most days of my life.

The plane hung on its nail at the end of the bench where it has always hung, six feet from the drawer, and sharp, because I keep my edges sharp whatever else I let go. Twenty minutes is an honest estimate and may be generous. It is a quarter inch off the underside of two runners and a rub of candle wax along them after, and I have done work of that size for other people at the end of a long day and thought nothing of it, and been pleased to be asked.

I understand well enough now what I was about, and I understood some part of it then, in the way you understand a thing you have arranged not to look at directly.

But it was the other letter I had come to the drawer for.

Everything I owned that winter smelled of the fire. That is not a figure. Smoke had gone into the blankets and the books' top edges and the good wool of my coat, and airing in the cold only rested it, and I had stopped noticing it weeks since except at moments when I noticed nothing else, and this was one: I opened the drawer, and the drawer stuck and argued and came, and the smell of my burned house rose off everything in it, and under the smell, when I lifted the letter out, was the letter's own smell, which was clean paper and a faint dry ghost of lavender, a larder smell, a linen-press smell, the smell of a house where things are kept properly because keeping is a form of love. I sat with it a while at the desk. Then I did not read it at the desk. I took it to the chair by the fire, because some letters are documents and some letters are guests, and I had kept this guest standing a fortnight and more in a cold drawer already.

I took the banking off the fire first and fed it two lengths of dry ash out of the basket, which was an extravagance at that hour of the night, and which I committed deliberately, and which I would commit again. The wood took hold with that small settling crack they give, and the light came up on the ceiling beams, and the room went from cold with a fire in it to warm in one place. I stood in front of it a while with my hands out to it until they were fit to hold paper.

The basket had been filled off the stack along the south wall, and I had not bought a stick of that stack, and I burned two lengths of it that night with both hands and no arithmetic in me at all. Ash is the wood that will burn green and pays you back double for being dry, and those two were two winters dry and went up clean with hardly any smoke in them, and I was glad of that out of all proportion to the fact.

My hands took longer to come back than they should have. A fortnight of that room had got the cold into the joints of them, and it came out slowly and in the order it always goes: the backs first, then across the knuckles, then the last two fingers of the left hand, which are always last. I turned them over and back in front of the fire, palms and backs, and watched the light come up red through the webs, and thought about nothing whatever.

Then I sat, and put the letter on my knee, and looked at the outside of it, which I had not once done properly in a fortnight.

My name on it, and the valley, and by way of Vethmark, in that upright hand that never in its life leaned on anything. The stamps of three posting offices, one of them smudged past reading, the last of them Wainford. A softness along the top edge where it had ridden in a bundle. A thumb of ink in the corner that was not hers and was, by the size of it, the post boy's. She had sealed it with wax, which nobody does any more, and had pressed the wax not with a seal but with something that came to hand, the end of a spoon handle by the look of the mark, and I have thought since that the spoon was probably the nearest thing to her.

The hand had not altered by a degree in six years. She wrote upright and small and even, the letters standing separate more often than joined, so that a line of it looked less like writing than like something set out in rows, and she had done my name across the middle of that cover in the same size she would have used on a jar. I have two of her labels in my head still in that hand, the black plum and a damson she made one year and never made again. Nothing about the outside of that letter had been done in a hurry. There was not a mark on it she had not intended, the post boy's thumb excepted, and I found that I liked the thumb.

The paper was heavier than people trouble with now, and the fold was square. The softness along the top edge was the only thing about it that had happened to it rather than been done to it, and I ran my thumb along that edge two or three times sitting there, for no reason I could give then or give now.

I turned it over twice. I held it up so the fire was behind it and could see nothing but one sheet, folded once, and the shadow of writing on it that could have been any writing in the world. I sat with it a good deal longer than a man needs to sit with one sheet of paper.

I am not going to describe breaking the seal, because I do not remember it. What I remember is that the letter was short, one side of one sheet in that upright hand, and that I had braced myself the way you brace for grief and found I had braced in the wrong direction entirely, and that is the thing about the letters we cannot open. We seal them ourselves. We fill them, in the dark of a drawer, with everything we cannot carry, and then we are astonished to open them at last and find only what was put in.

What was in it was this. I will give it whole, because it is not mine to summarize.

*Emlyn.*

*Penworth writes that the house is sold at last, and that her things are crated under his seal, and safe, and that you have built something on a hill a long way off. He is a careful man and tells me little else, and I do not ask, because I would rather have it from you.*

*I am writing to ask you to come in the spring. Not for a visit. For the crates. It is time they were opened, and I find I cannot do it, and I have understood at last, this autumn, that I have been waiting because it is not work for one person. It never was. It is work for the two people who loved her most, at one table, with the whole of a day to fail at it and start again.*

*Come in the spring, then, and bring whatever of her still lives in your keeping. I know there is something. There is always something; I have her mother's spoons. You have carried yours alone these six years, and I have carried mine, and I think we have both learned everything the carrying has to teach.*

*The garden has gone on without her, which I did not forgive it for the first year, and do now. Come and see it. Come in the spring, Emlyn, and we will open the crates, and I will feed you until you are sensible.*

*With my love, which you have always had, and my patience, which you have tested,*

*Hesper.*

I sat with it a long time. I would be lying if I said I sat quietly the whole of it, and that is as much as I will say; a man's chair by his own fire on the longest night of the year is one of the few courts left where he may be as he is, unwitnessed, and I was, for a while, as I was.

I had eaten at that woman's table perhaps forty times in my life and never once got out of the house without something wrapped in a cloth to take with me. The larder was three steps down off her kitchen and was colder than the yard, and had a stone shelf the length of the wall, and on the shelf the jars stood in ranks with the labels in that same upright hand and the year written small in the corner of each. She fed people the way other men argue, by instinct and to win. She had opinions about my coat, my hours, my colour, and the state of my cuffs, and delivered them while putting food in front of me so that there was nothing to be done about any of it.

That house kept things, and kept them properly, and the keeping went all through it and was not confined to the larder. Cloths came back. I never once carried food out of that kitchen without a cloth round it, and never once returned a cloth without seeing it go straight into a drawer with a dozen others, all of them ironed. There was a shelf in the passage with string on it, and beeswax, and a paper of pins, and a box of corks she had been adding to since before I knew any of that family, and every cork in it had come out of a bottle in that house and gone into that box, because a cork is not rubbish until somebody has decided it is.

The garden ran along the south side and down, and it was not a fine garden and had no intention of being one. It was a working garden: beans up the fence, a plum against the wall, and a great deal of ground given over to what was going into jars in September. She weeded it standing up, badly, with a fork, in whatever she happened to have on, at any hour of the day, and then came in and washed her hands and was an entirely different woman in that kitchen ten minutes later.

I was brought into the house the first time as a guest of her daughter's, and was fed as though I had been fetched in off a step, and that never altered afterwards. It did not alter when I stopped being a young man of promise and became a man with a house of his own on a river. It did not alter in the year everything altered.

I had watched her sit on those larder steps once with the door open at her back and take a spoonful out of a jar of the black plum and put the lid back and say nothing, and her daughter knew, and let her.

The last time I stood in that kitchen there were people in the front rooms in their black coats and I could not go in to them. Hesper found me by the larder door with my hat still in my hand. She put her palm flat on the back of my neck a moment, as you would to a horse, and then went back in to her guests, because they were her guests and she was who she was. That was the whole of what passed between us. I wrote to her three times in the six years after and posted twice, and both of those were about the house.

Her things were in a storehouse off the Vethmark road, crated and numbered in chalk under Penworth's seal, and I had paid the storage every quarter for years as a man pays for a thing he has no intention of ever going to collect.

I read the letter again. Then I read the middle of it a third time, the part that says it is not work for one person.

Six years I had treated my grief as a thing that had to be defended, a keep to be held, walls and a locked gate and me on the one wall, garrison of one. Every unopened thing in my life, the letter, the crates, the inventory, the chest twelve feet under my chair, I had held as a besieged man holds a gate, certain that what was outside the walls was loss in its final form, the kind you do not survive, and that the holding was the surviving.

You do not tell a grief what to be. I had built my whole late life on the ground's version of that sentence and had never once, in six years, thought to walk it back up my own stairs and in at my own door. You ask. And you get out of the way earlier than you think you ought.

The fire had burned down to its steady heart. Outside the window the valley lay black and white under no moon at all, the solstice dark, the year's floor, and one by one, as I sat there, the last late lights of Ardenmoor went out, Lira's amber last of all, until the only light burning in the whole of that valley was the lumen at my elbow, keeping the hill's hours, dimming toward the deep of night, waiting as it had always waited for the hour before the birds, and I sat in the last light in the valley with a letter on my knee and listened to my own house tick and settle, and somewhere below the floor, patient beyond every patience I have ever failed to learn, the ground I lived on gathered itself toward the one hour of the year when its long word came almost right.

I did not decide anything. I want that written plainly. Deciding is a daylight word, and what happened that night was not a decision, it was a door coming to the end of its argument. I stood up, at some hour I could not name, with the letter still in my hand. I banked the fire out of forty years of habit. I took the lumen off its shelf, and it came with me the way it had come with me through every dark of three winters, throwing my shadow huge up the Study wall and then folding it down the stairwell ahead of me.

And I took the key.

I have called it a key from the first and I will not stop now. It was never metal. The lock I had set on that chest in another life answers no iron. It answers a question, the same one question, asked with the whole of a man, and for six years I had carried the question the way you carry a key to a house you cannot bear to visit, knowing its exact weight in the pocket, never once taking it out. *Are you ready to be answerable for what you keep?* That is the whole key. It had been cut the day I sealed the chest, and I had carried it every day since, and it had never once fit, because I had never once, until a woman's letter on a solstice eve, been able to hold it steady.

I went through the workroom side of that room with the lumen held low. My instruments lay along the long bench under their cloths, shapes I could name in the dark by their shoulders, and the cloths made them look like a row of small sleeping things, and I did not stop. I had the letter in my right hand. I did not put it in my pocket. I have no account to give of that beyond the fact of it.

The stair door is Jorin's oak, hung true, and it fits close enough that the cellar keeps its own air on the far side of it. The latch was cold past the point where cold is a temperature; I felt it through the callus of my thumb as a kind of pressure. It lifted with the small clack it has always had, and the door came in toward me, and the cellar breathed up into the room, and it was the smell it has always been down there, cut stone and turned earth and the mineral flatness of a place that has never in its whole existence been warm, with the apples under it somewhere, sweet and far off.

The stairs took me down the way they always did, cold coming up to meet warmth going down.

Eleven steps. I have never once gone down them without counting, and I did not decide to count them that night any more than I decided the rest of it. The middle of the fourth tread has a hollow worn into it already, which is not the work of a solemn occasion but of the ordinary traffic of turnips, and my boot found the hollow, as it always does.

I went down slowly, holding the lumen out ahead and a little low so the light went where my feet were going, and my shadow went up the wall behind me and swung across the vault as I came, huge and then less. The dressed stone slid past a hand's breadth from my shoulder. At about the fifth step the air changed the way water changes when you wade past your knees, and after that every breath came back to me visible and hung there and went. Cold got in at my cuffs first, then at the collar, then everywhere at once. On the lower courses the stone had begun to take frost out of the air, not thick, only a whitening in the joints where the mortar sits back, and the lamp put a little amber in it as it passed.

At the bottom I stopped with my hand on the last of the wall and stood a moment. The hill was doing nothing at all. Not the dusk-fail, long gone by then, and not the other; only the flat waiting quiet of a ground that is somewhere in the middle of its own night, and I stood in it and let my eyes take the room.

The cellar was black and still and mine, and the corner was where the corner had always been, and I set the lumen on the floor beside the chest, and knelt, and laid the letter down beside the lamp where it could see, if letters see, and put both my hands flat on the old black wood for the first time in three years, and above me the house was quiet, and around me the hill was quiet, the deep quiet before its deepest hour, the whole patient ground of my life holding its breath in the dark, and I asked.
