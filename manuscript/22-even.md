# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Two: Even

The lamp business, which had begun as one lamp on a sill, had grown by that third spring into a trade with paper of its own, and the paper said that I owed Tomas a visit.

The Series lamps went out in housings, brass collar and foot to my drawings, and every housing that had ever left that valley had come off Tomas's bench. He had made the first as a favor, the second as a commission, and the following fifty in the way a river takes on a tributary, without either party signing anything, because between the two of us paper had always come last if it came at all. But fifty is a number with opinions. The spring patents had been granted at the turn of the year, the licences were beginning to pay the way Penworth had said they would, slowly and then steadily, and a man whose work is in half the housings between Ardenmoor and Wainford is owed better than a neighbor's arrangement. So I went down to the forge on a soft grey morning with a paper in my coat, to put my smith on a proper footing, and to give him a thing I had been meaning to give him for half a year.

The forge was quiet, as it always was. I have been in smithies where the noise is the product and the iron is incidental. Tomas's was the other thing entire: coals ticking, the file going in long patient strokes, the hammer only when the hammer had something true to say. He was at the bench when I came in, finishing an edge on a hinge, and he looked up and tipped his chin at the second stool, and I sat and held my peace until the file had finished its sentence, because interrupting Tomas at an edge is a thing you do once.

"Twenty sets," I said, when he set it down. "For the summer. Same drawings, but the collar goes wider by a hair; I'll mark it. And this time it's written." I put the paper on the bench. "Commissioned proper, against the patent money as it comes in. Penworth drew it. It says marks and quarter-days and what happens if either of us dies, which he tells me a proper paper must, and I apologize for it."

Tomas wiped his hands and took the paper and read it the whole way through, which not one man in ten does, and nothing in his face moved while he did it. Penworth had put the sum where a sum goes. It was a fair figure and slightly better than fair, which was as far as I could push it before Tomas would push back.

"Twenty," he said at last.

"Twenty."

"By harvest."

"Before it, if the freight behaves."

He set the paper down, squared it to the bench edge with one finger, and that was the signing, though he signed it too, in a hand like his hammer work, small and heavy and without one wasted line. And that would have concluded the morning's business, if I had let the morning stop being business, which I did not intend to.

The bundle was in my pack, wrapped in sacking against the road. I set it on the bench between us and unwound it, and the Series lamp stood up out of the cloth into the forge's dim, glass and brass, one of the best of the winter's making, and I put my thumb to the collar and woke it. Amber. The coals ticked. The lamp sat on the scarred black bench in that dark workmanlike room and burned its steady burn that owes nothing to any fire, and Tomas looked at it and did not say anything for a while.

"I'm not selling it," I said. "It's not in the paper and it's not against anything. Every housing you've made me came back off this bench better than the drawing asked. That has a worth, and I've been carrying it, and I don't care to carry it into a written arrangement. So it's yours, and we start the paper clean."

I had thought about how to say it a good deal on the walk down, and that was the best I had managed, and it was too many words for the room I said them in. Tomas looked at the lamp. He did not touch it, the way I had seen him not touch a thing before, which in him was not reluctance but a kind of respect for the moment when he would.

"The wife's been wanting one," he said.

"I know it. Brenna told me."

"She'd not ask."

"I know that too."

He nodded, slowly, the way a beam settles onto its post. Then he put one finger to the brass foot, and turned the lamp a quarter-turn on the bench, so that the collar's seam faced away, which is how a maker sets a thing when it is his, and he said, "Even."

One word, and the whole ledger of two years closed in it, every favor and overrun and underbilling on both sides of that bench, weighed off against a light that would burn on his wife's table long after both of us were done arguing about invoices. I have signed papers worth ten times less than that word, and taken longer doing it.

We talked the drawings after that, or I talked and he asked his four questions, each one landing on the exact rivet where the work would want deciding, and the morning went the way good mornings at that forge went, warm and slow and mostly quiet, with the rain starting soft on the roof and neither of us minding. I was gathering my coat and the sacking, and the business was done and the visit was done, and I was one step short of the door when he spoke behind me.

"Four days?"

I stopped. I will be honest and say that for a breath I did not know what he meant, and then I did, and the knowing arrived with a small cold clarity, like stepping from a warm room into a spring night.

At the turn of the year, on some black evening with the snow coming down and the two of us watching his fire, I had said, once, in passing, the way you say a thing to fill a silence you trust: that the stones under my Study had been listening a long while now, and that when the spring dark came, the new moon nearest the thaw's end, I meant to go down and hear the whole of what they had gathered. One remark, one evening, five months gone. I had needed my almanac, that very week, to fix the night myself.

Tomas had not needed an almanac. He had carried the date the way he carried the hum itself, silently, exactly, all winter, all spring, under the hammer and the hinges and the twenty sets and the lamp, and he had let me get one step from his door before he asked, because that was as much asking as his dignity would permit.

"Four days," I said.

He nodded, and turned back to the bench, and took up the file, and the long patient strokes began again behind me as I stepped out into the rain, keeping their even count, the way they always had, a man at his work, counting along with the ground beneath him.
