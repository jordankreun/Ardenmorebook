# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Two: Even

The lamp business had begun as one lamp on a sill. By that third spring it was a trade with paper of its own, and the paper said I owed Tomas a visit.

Paper of its own means what it says. There was a book now with the runs entered in it and the housings numbered against them. There were licences, and paper to prove the licences. There was a man in Vethmark who wrote to me about all of it. None of that had been anywhere in my head in the winter I carried the first one down the lane to Brenna's door, and came back up again with no plan in me beyond making a second.

The Series lamps went out in housings, brass collar and foot to my drawings, and every housing that had ever left that valley had come off Tomas's bench. He had made the first as a favor, the second as a commission, and the following fifty in the way a river takes on a tributary, without either party signing anything, because between the two of us paper had always come last if it came at all. Fifty housings is not a favor. It is a season of a man's bench. The spring patents had been granted at the turn of the year, the licences had begun to pay the way Penworth said they would, slow at first and then steady, and a man whose work is in half the housings between Ardenmoor and Wainford is owed better than a neighbor's arrangement. So I went down to the forge on a soft grey morning with a paper in my coat, to put my smith on a proper footing, and to give him a thing I had been meaning to give him for half a year.

I walked it, and took my time. The way was new that spring and I had not got over the pleasure of using it, the flags dark with the night's wet, the beck up and loud on my left hand, the whole valley smelling of turned ground. It was the part of spring that cannot make up its mind. Mist stood off the bottoms to about the height of a man, with the tops of the Wray hedges out of it like something drawn in and not yet coloured, and the light came down grey and even with no sun anywhere in it.

The way took me down in something under a quarter of an hour. I counted flags for the first hundred and then stopped. The stones went dark and then darker where the wet lay in the dips, and my boots made a sound on them that no boots had ever made on that slope before, flat, with a little grit under it. At the elbow, where the seep comes under, the flag edges had greened already, a fortnight of moss got in, and I stood and looked at it and found I did not mind. At the beck, Ren's ford stones stood a hand clear with the water shoving hard at them and getting nowhere. He had bedded them on their broadest faces and left the beck its gaps to go through. I crossed dry.

At the foot the last flag sat down into the lane's own mud, the first stone laid of the whole business and the one that gets walked on most, worn pale in one corner already where the lane's traffic clips it. I turned round and looked back up. From the bottom the way is not much to see, a stitched line going up through the green and gone where the shoulder rounds off, and above it the glasshouse, five long panels of that flat morning. The paper was in the breast of my coat. In the pack, done up in sacking, were the two things I had come down with, and I had been thinking about both of them on and off since the middle of the winter.

The street was up before me. Maret's wheel was turning at the low end and somebody had the bread oven open at the top, and the smell of it came the length of the street and made me slower than I had any business being. Nell Wray went by with a bucket and said good morning before I could, which two springs earlier would have wanted arranging in advance.

She had it two-handed and was leaning off it as a person of seven must with a vessel meant for a grown woman, and she went by and up the wash-house step without slowing, her business being water and not me. Two carts stood nose to tail outside Kellard's with their tailboards down and a man on each. A dog lay in the one dry patch on the whole street. The mud was that good spring mud that has finished being winter's and has not yet been trodden into summer's ruts, and I put a boot well into it, and thought better of the flags behind me than I had thought of them at the top.

Ned Pardon had me at the trough and kept me there.

He had come out of his gate as I came level with it. That can happen by accident once. He had his bucket with him as evidence, and he set it on the trough's lip and left it standing there through the whole of what followed, and it never once got filled.

"Now then," he said. "These lamps."

"They are."

He had rehearsed it. I could hear the rehearsal in it, a lane's length of getting the first sentence right, and I had learned by then to stand still and let him arrive at his own business in his own order, and not to help him to it.

"They'd be no use in a byre."

"They would, as it happens."

He took that in with his whole face, the way he took in anything I said. "Only a ewe's a nervous creature at lambing. She'll not have a flame near her, and I'll not have a lantern in with her, so I lamb in the dark like my father and I lose what I lose."

"There is no flame in it, Mr Pardon."

"Aye, well." He looked off down the street. "That's the part I can't get hold of."

He turned it over another few paces' worth without moving his feet.

"It'd hang on a nail, then," he said. "Nothing in it to spill."

"Nothing in it to spill."

He looked at the trough a while.

"What's it cost, then."

"A half-gold."

He whistled, and told me that was a deal of money for a light, and went off down the street pleased with the pair of us. He had it round four kitchens before noon. I know, because Brenna gave it back to me a fortnight later, word for word.

That is how the valley's post ran. A thing said at that trough was in Brenna's shop inside two days, and it came up my hill after that in her mouth, entire, in the order she had decided it wanted. She never passed a thing on hot. She held it until she had the shape of all of it, and then she set all of it down at once, and if a piece was missing she named the piece, and if she took the view that the piece was mine to supply she looked at me while she named it. She was the only person in that valley I could not be vague at. I learned more about my own trade out of what came back to me through that shop than out of any letter I ever wrote.

The forge stood at the top of the street. I heard it before I came to it, by which I mean that I did not hear it at all.

There is a sound a smithy makes at a hundred paces and it is not the hammer. It is the fire: a low steady draw, no louder than a kettle two rooms off, and under the draw the small tick of coal going about its business. You come up on Tomas's forge through a gap in the street's noise, and the gap is the announcement. Somebody at the top is working. The street has known it all morning and has not once had to raise its voice about it.

The forge was quiet, as it always was. I have been in smithies where the noise is the product and the iron comes second. Tomas's was the other thing entire: coals ticking, the file going in long patient strokes, the hammer only when the hammer had something true to say. He was at the bench when I came in, finishing an edge on a hinge, and he looked up and tipped his chin at the second stool. I sat and held my peace until the file had finished its sentence.

There was a good deal to look at from that stool and I had two years of practice at looking at it. The forge was one room, dark at the corners and never cold, with the fire kept small at the back of a hearth that could have taken three times the coal it ever got. The anvil stood on its elm stump where the light out of the doorway fell on it, and the face of it was worn to a soft shine in one place about the size of a hand, where the work goes down. Along the wall behind him hung the tongs, thirty pair of them, each one made for a single shape of iron and useless for any other, and under the tongs the swage block, and under that the slack tub, a hollowed log with the water gone black and a skin of scale floating on it.

Thirty pair is not a boast. It is an admission. Every one of them was a shape he had needed twice and had got tired of holding wrong the first time, and they hung in the order of how often they came down, and that is not the order a stranger would have hung them in and is the only order I have ever found sense in. Their reins were pale where his hands went and black everywhere else. He could take one off the wall behind him without looking round, and did, all morning, for years, and I never once saw him take the wrong one.

The swage block stood under them on its own low stub of oak, a foot of iron bitten all round its edges into hollows and half-rounds and vees of graded sizes. Three of those hollows had a shine on them. The rest had none at all, and you could have read Tomas's whole working life off that block and been about right: what a valley wants made, over and over, and what it has wanted made once in fifty years and may want again.

The slack tub stood where a slack tub stands, near enough to the anvil that a turn of the body reaches it. The water in it was black past seeing into, with the scale floating in a skin on top and a coppery bloom on the skin where the daylight out of the door came down into it. I have never known a smith to change that water. I asked him about it once, in my first year. He looked at the tub, and then at me, and went back to his work.

What I liked best was the pattern wall. Anything Tomas made twice he made a pattern for, a strip of soft iron bent to the shape or a scrap of board cut to it, and hung it on a nail with the rest, and there were a great many nails. Low down at the end, where a hand would find them without looking, hung mine: the collar profile, the foot, the bracket arm, three small pieces of iron on three nails with the years of their alterations scratched into them small.

I had gone the length of that wall once, one nail at a time, in my first winter, when he was out at a horse and had told me to wait. It was the nearest thing to a book that man owned. A scrap of board with the sweep of a wheel hoop marked on it. A strip of soft iron bent to the throat of a hook that somebody's grandmother had wanted a copy of. A tapering key of a shape gone out of use in the valley before I was born, hanging where it had always hung, because the door it opened was still on a byre somewhere and would want a key again one day. Nothing on that wall carried a name. Everything on it carried a date, small, cut in with the corner of a scribe, and where a thing had been altered the new date went under the old one and the old one stayed.

He had hung my three pieces at that height himself. I had not asked him to and he had not mentioned it, and I did not understand what it meant until I had watched him work for a year: on that wall, height is how often. Mine hung level with his hinge patterns and his hooks, in among the things his hand went to without his eyes, and their scratches ran three and four deep, and if you followed them down you had two years of me learning what to ask a smith for and a smith declining to say so.

The whole place smelled of coal smoke and hot scale and the sweetish burn of a hoof, though there had been no horse in it that morning; a forge keeps its smells the way a kitchen does. The file went on. It was the fine one, the last of the four he would have used on that edge, and by then he was not so much cutting the iron as agreeing with it.

"Twenty sets," I said, when he set it down. "For the summer. Same drawings, but the collar goes wider by a hair; I'll mark it. And this time it's written." I put the paper on the bench. "Commissioned proper, against the patent money as it comes in. Penworth drew it. It says marks and quarter-days and what happens if either of us dies."

Tomas wiped his hands and took the paper and read it the whole way through, which not one man in ten does, and nothing in his face moved while he did it. Penworth had put the sum where a sum goes. It was a fair figure and a little better than fair, which was as far as I could push it before Tomas would push back.

He had put it down in marks, sixty of them, marks being how money talks when it lives in an office.

Tomas read it twice. Then he set a finger under the line and turned the paper round on the bench so that it faced me, which was as near as that man ever came to raising his voice.

"Three silver the set."

"It is."

"Two and a half's the price."

"It was the price when you made them in twos and threes and I paid you out of my coat pocket. It is not the price for twenty at a fixed date with your season blocked out to do them in."

He said nothing to that. He was a hard man to argue with, because he did not argue. He left the wrong thing lying where you had put it until you either defended it or took it back.

So I sat in it. The coals ticked. Out on somebody's ridge tile a blackbird went through everything it had, twice, and I came to the end of my patience a long way before Tomas came to the end of his.

"Tomas. If you make me twenty sets at two and a half, and the freight goes wrong, and you sit here in August with brass on the bench and no other work booked, who carries that?"

"I do."

"You do. And you'd not say a word about it, and I'd hear of it in December from Brenna. I would rather pay the half now than find it out then. Take the three. It is what a man in Wainford would ask me and get, and I would pay it there, and the work here is better."

He did not move. I put my hand flat on the bench, on the wood and not on the paper, because I did not care to be a man pushing paper at another man across his own bench.

"And there's a thing in it for me," I said. "Paid the three, you'll block the season and turn other work away, and I'll get twenty sets by harvest. Paid at two and a half you'll fit them round whatever comes in the door, and I'll get fourteen by harvest and six in October with an apology folded in with them. I am not being generous with you. I am buying a date, and a date costs more than brass."

He looked at the figure a while longer. Then he turned the paper back round to himself, which was the whole of his surrender, and read on down to the bottom, where Penworth had disposed of both our deaths in one sentence eleven lines long.

"What's that."

"That's what happens if I die."

He read it again. "Long way of saying it."

"He is paid by the hour," I said, "and he is worth every hour of it. I have told him twice that no man in this valley ever needed eleven lines to say he would keep his word. He tells me that is just the sort of thing men say in the year before they go to law."

He read the eleven lines a third time. Then he did what I have watched him do over a weld, good or bad, and did nothing at all for as long as it took. The light out of the doorway had gone a shade colder while we sat. It framed the wet street and a corner of the trough and nobody in either.

"Twenty," he said at last.

"Twenty."

"By harvest."

"Before it, if the freight behaves."

The paper wanted a pen, and finding one took longer than agreeing to it had. There was one in the drawer under the bench with the punches, and it had not been out since the last time somebody made him sign for a delivery, and the ink was a stone bottle with a crust on the neck of it. He worked the crust off with a thumbnail, and dipped, and wiped the nib twice on the lip, and then went at the paper with his elbow well out from his side. The name took him about four seconds.

He set the paper down, squared it to the bench edge with one finger, and that was the signing, though he signed it too, in a hand like his hammer work, small and heavy and without one wasted line. That would have been the morning's business done. I had not come down only for business.

The bundle was in my pack, wrapped in sacking against the road. I set it on the bench between us and unwound it, and the Series lamp stood up out of the cloth into the forge's dim, glass and brass, one of the best of the winter's making, and I put my thumb to the collar and woke it. Amber. The coals ticked. The lamp sat on the scarred black bench in that dark workmanlike room and burned its steady burn that owes nothing to any fire, and Tomas looked at it and did not say anything for a while.

It is a small object. It stands about a hand and a half high and there is nothing in the look of it to tell you what it is. I had wrapped it myself the night before on my own table with the fire down and my coat still on, and I had made a poor job of the knots and a fair job of the padding, and it had come the length of the way down on my back without so much as a bloom on the collar.

The light of it found the anvil first and lay along the horn in a long bright line. Then it found the tongs and gave each pair a small gold hook, and it went down into the water of the slack tub and did not come back out. A forge is built to be seen by firelight. Every surface in it has been arranged across fifty years, by men who never once thought about it, to answer firelight. This did not flicker and it did not breathe. The shadows it threw stood where it put them and stayed, and the room looked strange in it, and looked well, the way a face you know looks in a doorway you have never seen it stand in.

It came to the pattern wall last. Fire makes a wall like that into one crowded shape with the edges running together; this picked the patterns apart and stood each one on its own nail with its own small shadow laid off to one side, and none of them moved. The scratched dates came up out of the iron. The old tapering key threw a key onto the plaster twice its own size and held it there.

Out on the wet street a cart went by. Neither of us turned round.

"I'm not selling it," I said. "It's not in the paper and it's not against anything. Every housing you've made me came back off this bench better than the drawing asked. That has a worth, and I've been carrying it, and I don't care to carry it into a written arrangement. So it's yours, and we start the paper clean."

I had worked at that on the walk down, and it was still too many words for the room I said them in. Tomas looked at the lamp. He did not touch it.

"The wife's been wanting one," he said.

"I know it. Brenna told me."

"She'd not ask."

"I know that too."

He nodded. Then he put one finger to the brass foot and turned the lamp a quarter-turn on the bench, so that the collar's seam faced away. That is how a maker sets a thing when it is his.

He said, "Even."

One word, and the ledger of two years closed in it. Every favor, every overrun, every hour on both sides of that bench that neither of us had ever put on paper.

We talked the drawings after that, or I talked and he asked his four questions, each one landing on the rivet where the work would want deciding. The rain started soft on the roof. Neither of us minded.

The four questions were these.

I had the sheets out flat by then with the tea tin on one corner and a hammer on the other, and I had brought a piece of the new glass up in my pack, wrapped in the same sacking as the lamp, a disc the size of my palm out of the winter's last case. That was the whole trouble sitting in one object. The house in the city that had cut my glass for two years had sold up at the turn of the year, and the cutter who had it now cut cleaner and cut a shade thicker, and a shade is nothing at all until you seat it in a collar bored for the old stuff. Then a shade is everything, because the brass grips it cold, and the frost gets in behind the grip, and in February you have a customer with a cracked lamp and a good opinion of you gone sour.

I put the disc on the bench.

"Whose glass," Tomas said.

"A new house out past the yards. Better work, half a hair thicker, and they'll not be talked out of it. I have tried twice by letter."

He waited, so I gave him the rest of it.

"They answered both times and they were courteous both times, and both times they told me the new cut is the better cut and that they'll not set their people back to a worse one for one customer in a valley they have never heard of. They are right. That is what makes it difficult."

He picked the disc up and did not look at it. He held it at the edge between finger and thumb, turned it once, and set it down, and I have watched men measure with instruments and be less certain when they had finished.

"Seat or the collar."

"The seat. The outside dimension stays where it is, or the brackets are all wrong, and every housing you have ever made me becomes a housing that will not hang square off my own arm."

He nodded the once. That was the answer he wanted. He had asked in order to hear me say the second half of it without prompting.

"How much."

"There's the difficulty. A hair is a word and not a measure, and I cannot draw a hair."

Tomas got off the stool. He went to the scrap box under the bench and came up with a strip of thin iron about the length of a finger, put it in the vice, and took a file to the end of it, and began working a slot in. It took him perhaps four minutes. Twice he stopped and tried the glass in the slot and went back to the file. He never once went near a rule. He filed until the disc would go into the slot under its own weight and stop there with a whisper, and then he gave it one stroke more, and handed the strip across to me.

"Gauge," he said.

I put my thumbnail into the slot and felt the daylight in it, that hair I could not draw, sitting in a piece of scrap iron where any fool could find it, myself included.

Four minutes, and no hurry in any of them. I had watched every part of it from the stool and could not have reproduced one part of it. The strokes were long and used the whole length of the file, out and back, out and back, and every third or fourth he took his thumb across the slot to learn what his eyes would not have told him for another minute yet. He blew the swarf off twice, away from me both times. He never turned the work toward the door for a better light. There was nothing in it to see. The whole of it was under his thumb.

"Make two," I said. "One here and one up the hill. And scratch the year on them, because there will be another glass house in five years and we will want to know which of these belonged to which."

He took it back and cut the year into the flat with a scribe. Then he made the second strip, and cut the year into that one before he had finished filing its slot, out of the order he did everything in, and he saw it, and said nothing, and neither did I.

That was three. The fourth came a good while after, when we had been through the foot and the bracket arm and the small chamfer he had put on the collar lip in the autumn without mentioning it, which I had found in November and said nothing about and which now stood in my drawing as though it had always been mine.

By then the doorway had gone flat and grey at its edge and the rain had settled into the steady kind. He had gone back to the hinge somewhere in there and taken up the fine file again. His questions came out of the work and not out of any conversation. That is how he talks. A thing goes under the file for an hour and comes up finished, and you are handed the finished thing, and you are not shown the hour.

"The old fifty," he said.

"They'll take the new glass in every house in this valley and crack in about nine of them the first hard winter, and the nine will be on north sills." I had chewed that on the walk down and I did not like it any better out loud. "I'll write to Kellard and have the name of every man he sold one to. Any that comes back, we ream the seat and it goes on me. Not on the customer and not on you."

He did not ask me the next thing, so I said it for him.

"Fifty seats reamed is fifty housings off your bench and back onto it, and you'll not be paid for one of them, because they're paid for already. That's what I'm putting to you and I've not found a way round it. I could offer to pay you for the reaming. You'd not take it, because to your mind a housing that comes back is a housing that went out wrong, and none of these went out wrong, and being paid to put right a thing you did right is worse to you than doing it for nothing. So I'll not offer, and you'll not refuse, and we'll both keep the ten minutes."

The file went on. Two strokes, three.

"They'll not all come back in one winter," I said. "It'll be over two, and the nine will be the north sills, and the north sills are the ones nobody looks at until February. When I write to Kellard I'll put in the letter that the change is mine and the drawing is mine, in those words, because he reads his letters out to whoever's standing in his yard, and I'd sooner the yard had it right the first time than half right for five years."

Tomas thought about it for the length of one good breath.

"Fair," he said. I was gathering my coat and the sacking, and the business was done and the visit was done, and I was one step short of the door when he spoke behind me.

"Four days?"

I stopped. For a breath I did not know what he meant. Then I did.

At the turn of the year, on a black evening with the snow coming down and the two of us watching his fire, I had said something once, in passing, the way you say a thing to fill a silence you trust. One remark, one evening, five months gone. I had needed my almanac that very week to fix the night myself.

I had gone down that afternoon for a bracket and stayed, because a man does not walk up a hill in that. The snow had been coming down all afternoon in the fat slow flakes that mean it intends to stay.

It had come on about two in the afternoon and by four there was a hand of it on his window sill and no wheel mark left anywhere in the street. I stood in his doorway with the snow going past the opening in a slow sideways drift and the cold coming off my own coat, and Tomas looked past me at the weather, and then at me, and said nothing at all, and stood out of the way of the door. That was the invitation.

He had done his day and banked nothing. He had put on what you put on when you mean to sit, not a heap of it but coal enough to go a long time, laid back and drawn forward as it took, and it changed the room. The corners came in. The tongs stopped being tools and became a row of hooks with fire in them. The anvil sat out of the light with nothing but its horn catching. In the slack tub a red coal went out and came back and went out again, and there was never any coal in the tub.

We got the two stools round. He put mine on the warm side and said nothing about having done it. Our boots steamed, mine the harder for having come down a hill in it, and after a while I got them off and set them side by side on the hearth stone with the toes in, and he looked at that and did not say whether it was welcome, and I took it that it was. The wet came out of my coat in the same slow way and the room took on the smell of it, wool and coal and the hot iron smell that never leaves a forge in any weather. We said almost nothing for the better part of an hour.

He sat with his forearms on his knees and his hands hanging and looked at the fire, and every so often he leaned forward and moved one coal about an inch and a half with the poker and leaned back again. Somewhere in the first half of it he got up and put the kettle on the bar and made tea in a pot with a mended handle, and put a cup in my hand without a word, and sat down. The snow went on. The street had stopped being a street and become a white gap between two rows of roofs, and once somebody went along it with a lantern and the light of it crossed the floor and went out at the far wall.

At some point I said, into the fire, that the stone under my Study had been listening a good while now and had a great deal in it, and that I would go down and read the whole of it at the new moon nearest the end of the thaw, a reading wanting the quietest night in the year and that being the quietest there is.

Tomas said, "Mm."

That was all of it. The fire went on. We talked afterward about a mare of Cael's, and about whether Maret would ever mend his sluice or only go on mending his wheel, and I walked up through the snow in the dark with a bracket in my coat and thought no more about any of it.

It had stopped by the time I came out of his door, and the sky had opened over the valley in the hard scoured way it does after, and the whole white bottom of it lay there with no mark on it anywhere but my own going up. There were no stones on that slope then. I went up by the old track, by guesswork and by the shape of the ground under a foot of snow, and stopped twice, and was not sorry either time to be standing still in it. The bracket lay cold against my chest the whole climb. At the top I knocked my boots off on the doorstone and set the bracket on the bench and went to bed, and slept like a stone myself.

Tomas had not needed an almanac. He had carried the date all winter and all spring, under the hammer and the hinges and the twenty sets and the lamp, and he had let me get one step from his door before he asked for it.

"Four days," I said.

He nodded, and turned back to the bench, and took up the file. I stepped out into the rain. The strokes began again behind me, long and even, a man at his work counting along with the ground beneath him.
