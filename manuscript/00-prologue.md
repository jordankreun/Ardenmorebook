# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Prologue

I have reached the age at which a man begins to set things down. Not because the memory is going — mine has never gone, and I have had a great deal longer than most men to put it to the test — but because there comes a point in a long enough life when writing a thing down is simply what one does with it, the way a man mends, at last, the gate he has been stepping over for years.

And there is this, besides. A great many stories are told about me now. I have sat quiet in the corners of inns and heard my own life told back to me by people who never came within a hundred miles of it, growing stranger and grander with each year and each retelling, until the man in the story is someone I would not know to nod to in the road and, if I am honest, do not much care for. I hold the tellers no grudge — a good story is a kind of coin, and it is meant to be spent — but when there are enough of them, and they agree on so little, an honest accounting starts to look like the least a man can do: if only so that somewhere, in one place, the thing is written the way it happened. Or near enough. I will not pretend the years have warmed no words nor softened any faces; fondness does that, and I was fond of very nearly all of them, and they are gone now, every one of them, but me. Still — the shape of it is true, and the shape is the thing that matters.

I will tell you only this much plainly at the outset, because the tales do love to begin with a wonder, or with a boy, and it was neither. I was a man. Only that: a man at the tired end of a bad few years, with rather more skill than sense and nowhere in the wide world he particularly needed to be. That is who this story is about. Be gentle with him. He did not yet know how any of it came out — which is the one advantage I hold over him, and very nearly the only thing I own that I would give back, if the giving-back of such things were ever allowed.

So. We begin where it began: on a road, in the spring, a very long time ago.
