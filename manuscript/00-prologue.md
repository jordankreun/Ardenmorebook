# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Prologue

I have reached the age at which a man begins to set things down. Not because the memory is going; mine has never gone, and I have had a great deal longer than most men to put it to the test. It is rather that there comes a point in a long enough life when writing a thing down is simply what one does with it, the way a man mends, at last, the gate he has been stepping over for years.

And there is this, besides. Once in a great while, in some inn a long way from anywhere, I will hear a story told that I half recognize, the way you might recognize your own coat on a stranger three sizes too large for it. There are not many of them. I am not, whatever you may have been led to expect, a man they sing about; I took a good deal of quiet care, over the years, that it should stay so. But a few tales got loose all the same, and time and retelling have warped them so far out of true that the man in them is half myth and half plain nonsense, and I would not know him to nod to in the road, and, if I am honest, do not much care for him. I hold the tellers no grudge; a good story is a kind of coin, and it is meant to be spent. It only seems a poor thing to leave nothing behind but those few, and so, if only so that the truth of it should exist somewhere, in one place, I mean to set down what actually happened. Or near enough. I will not pretend the years have warmed no words nor softened any faces; fondness does that, and I was fond of very nearly all of them, and they are gone now, every one of them, but me. Still, the shape of it is true, and the shape is the thing that matters.

I will tell you only this much plainly at the outset, because such stories as get told of me do love to begin with a wonder, or with a boy, and it was neither. I was a man. Only that: a man at the tired end of a bad few years, with rather more skill than sense and no particular reason to be anywhere at all. That is who this story is about. Be gentle with him. He did not yet know how any of it came out, which is the one advantage I hold over him, and very nearly the only thing I own that I would give back, if the giving-back of such things were ever allowed.

So. Enough of an old man clearing his throat. We begin a very long time ago, with a younger and a good deal more foolish version of myself, a man who had the whole of this in front of him and had not the first idea that it was coming.
