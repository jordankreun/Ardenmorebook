# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Prologue

*From one of the later journals*

I heard the story of myself last night, told across a crowded taproom by a man who had no notion he was telling it to the one it belonged to, and I have been turning it over ever since, the way you turn a stone you picked up without meaning to and now cannot quite bring yourself to set down.

I did not know him, the man in the story. That is the plain truth of it, and plain truth is very near all I have left that is worth the giving. They had made me into someone in those tales: a figure, a wonder, a man who walked through the whole of his life already knowing the shape of it. They gave him a boyhood to match, too: a strange, bright, destined boy, marked from the cradle, working wonders at an age when the true article is mostly falling out of trees. The tellers are fond of such boys. No wizard in a taproom tale is ever allowed to have been merely a child. And the man they made is, if I may say so of my own legend, a bit of an ass. Sure of himself. Untouched by anything. Always the right word ready on his lips and never a wrong night lying behind him. I would not cross a room to shake his hand, and I have yet to hear the tale told in a way that made me wish I would.

Not that the real one was any great prize. I will not sit here in my old age and pretend the man I was in those days was wiser or gentler or one whit less of a fool than the figure in the story. God knows I had a great deal more to learn than I yet had the sense to know I was missing, and I learned the most of it the slow way, and late, and at a cost to better people than myself. But he was a man, at least, and not a monument. And if I am to leave one true thing behind me when I go, I would sooner it were him than the bright bronze figure the years have cast up in his place.

Perhaps I will set the true thing down one day. The wanting to has come over me lately, quiet and from behind, the way most of my worse ideas have arrived. And when I turn my mind to how it would have to go, I find I know at least where it would not begin. Not on a wonder, and not on a boy. There is no boy in it, whatever the tellers would prefer, and no wonder either, not at the start of it. I think I would begin with the man himself, such as he was in those days: at the tired end of a bad few years, with far more skill than he had sense and nowhere in all the wide world that he needed to be. And I think, before I let him take a single step, I would ask you to go easy on him. He did not yet know how any of it would turn out.

I do, and that is the one advantage I have ever held over him, and the one I would most gladly give back.
