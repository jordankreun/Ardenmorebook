# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Fourteen: The Well Incident

It happened on an ordinary market morning, a fortnight into the stone's thirty days, and an ordinary morning is the only kind such things ever happen on. The extraordinary ones are all braced for. It is the day you have gone down to the village for nothing more urgent than bread and a spool of waxed thread that the ground reminds you the world is not as settled as you had let yourself believe.

It was warm before seven. I came down the hill on foot, the errand not being worth a horse, and Snow watched me go from the barn door with her chest against the top board and the whole of her weight on it. She had been idle three days. A mare idle three days develops views, and the view she had settled on was that the door was a thing to lean upon and I was a thing to be blown at. I told her that a horse who leans all evening might have the grace to be caught in the morning. She blew at me. I went on down.

The track takes half an hour walked honestly, and I did not walk it honestly, because the light was still low and long across the bottoms and the bottoms were worth stopping for. My own barley stood on the near side, green and a good deal shorter than it ought to have been, in ground I had bullied a plough into that spring and had no business expecting much from. Beyond it, down where the beck ran, there was black ground turned that had not seen a share in years, squared off at the hedge and open to the sky. Cut thistle lay grey in windrows the whole length of it. A thread of smoke stood straight up out of the old stone farmhouse into air that had no wind in it at all. I stood on the track a minute and looked at that, and then went on, bread being a thing you can be late for.

Ardenmoor on a market morning is the one morning in the week the village is louder than its own water. There were carts drawn up wheel to wheel along the north side of the square and more of them backed into the lane, butter under wet cloths, a trestle of early beans, three cheeses on a board with a boy set to watch them and no clear notion of what he was watching for. The air had the smell a place gets when everybody in it has been up since four. Horse, and crushed grass, and hot clay, and over the top of all of it, bread.

The oven had its mouth open to the lane and eight or nine women about it, standing in the loose ordered way of people who all know exactly whose turn it is and could tell you to the minute. I had been watching that queue for a year and a half. I had never once seen it written down, nor heard it disputed, nor found anybody who could be got to explain it to me. A woman would step in front of another woman and the other woman did not mind. Somewhere behind that there was a reckoning of firings and firewood and whose sister had been ill in the spring, and it was kept where I could not get at it, and it came out even at the end of every week.

I had no dough, having no ambitions in that line, so I waited on the second drawing and bought a loaf off a woman who had one over. It was one of the dark ones. I have never in my life bought the other sort, and by that summer nobody in Ardenmoor had to be told so twice. She turned it out of its cloth and knocked the bottom of it with a knuckle and listened to it, the way I have watched a cooper listen to a barrel and a smith listen to a weld, and pronounced it sound. She took two coppers off me and gave me one back. The oven had been sulky since the wet week, she said, and she had put the small loaves nearest the mouth on account of it.

I said the small loaves seemed to have come to no harm.

"They've come to no harm," she agreed, in the tone of a woman who was not going to be flattered out of a grievance she had spent all morning building, and went back to her board.

That left the thread. I wanted a spool of waxed thread for the plain reason that the strap of the satchel I carry my glass and my files in had gone at the stitching, and I had been making do with a bootlace for a fortnight, which works, and looks exactly like what it is. Kellard keeps waxed thread in the dry-goods yard. Kellard, that morning, was not in his yard. He was in the square with his cart and his tailgate down, and it seemed to me that a fellow standing about in that condition can be asked for a spool without anybody having to open a shop.

So I had the bread and had the thread still to get, and was crossing the square toward the inn with the loaf under my arm and the crust of it still ticking as it cooled, when Kellard's near horse went up.

I never learned what did it. A dog, most likely, or the flap of somebody's washing, or nothing at all, horses being the only creatures in the world more committed than a cat to having strong opinions about very little. The beast threw its head and came round hard in the shafts and the cart slewed, and for about four seconds the square was a small disaster: Kellard hauling at the bridle and swearing in a low steady stream, the egg-woman snatching her basket up off the cobbles, two men off the oven wall and running, everybody's morning suddenly full of half a ton of unhappy horse.

I have no particular quarrel with horses. I keep one, and she has never once done anything half so dramatic without a reason she declined afterward to give me. But there is a thing a horse in shafts does when it decides to be somewhere else, and it is not a rearing or a bolting so much as a sudden enormous reconsideration of the entire arrangement. Everything strapped to it has then to reconsider as well. The offside horse, who had no opinion in the matter whatever, was obliged to acquire one at speed, and behind the pair of them a loaded cart discovered that it had opinions of its own.

Soren was at the well. He was there most mornings, drawing a bucket for his grandfather's house and one for the inn, a chore he had been trusted with since he was small enough to need both hands on the crank, and he had just landed the full bucket on the stone coping and had not yet got the rope off it. The cart's wheel came round and clipped the well's stonework, not hard, a knock you would forget by supper, and the bucket went off the coping.

It went the way a full bucket goes. Straight down and slightly out, tipping as it fell, ten pounds of water and wood on its way to the cobbles, and the boy turned and put his hand out for it, late, the way you always are.

And the bucket came to his hand.

Not far. That is the thing I want to be exact about, because a story would make it lift and hang and shine, and what it did was move perhaps four inches, sideways, against the line of its own falling, in the quarter-second before his fingers closed on the rim. Then he had it, and the water slopped over his boots and down the front of him, and he swore, in the mild inherited way of a boy who has learned his swearing off his grandfather, and set the bucket on the coping and shook out his wet sleeve.

That was all. That was the whole of it.

I want to be plain about how invisible it was. Ten paces off, with a horse in the air and a woman's eggs at stake, there was nothing to see. A bucket had gone over and the boy had caught it. Boys catch things. If I had not been standing where I was standing, and if I had not spent twenty years being paid to notice exactly the sort of thing that ought not to have happened, I would have seen a lucky grab and thought no more of it than anyone else in that square did, which was nothing whatever. The cart came round. Kellard got the horse's head down. The egg-woman said something unrepeatable about carters. The morning closed over the moment the way water closes over a dropped stone, and not one soul there had the least idea that anything had happened at all.

Except that the bucket had moved four inches sideways, against the fall, into the boy's hand.

I went over. There was nothing else to do with my body, and standing still would have looked stranger than moving. I made a business of the windlass, because a man wants a reason to be at a well, and I put my hands on the frame and shook it and said something to Kellard about the ironwork being loose in the stone and worth a look before it dropped a bucket on somebody's foot, which was true enough of most old wells and entirely beside the point of this one. And while I said it I looked at the boy's hands.

They were a boy's hands, wet, holding a rope. Nothing in them.

"You'll be soaked," I said.

"It's warm out." He wrung his sleeve. He was not troubled. He was not anything. He was a lad who had nearly dropped a bucket and hadn't, and the whole business had cost him a wet arm and the small annoyance of having to draw the inn's water twice, and he was already turning back to the crank.

I have thought about that a great deal since. He did not know. There was not the smallest flicker in him of a boy who has felt a thing go strange under his hands, no startle, no covering, none of that quick sideways look children give the world when it has just behaved improperly. Whatever had reached out and taken four inches off that bucket's fall had not passed through anything Soren knew about himself. It had come out of him the way breath does. He had wanted the bucket. The bucket had come. If he registered any of it, he registered it as having caught the thing, which he had, and boys are not in the habit of interrogating their own luck.

I was still standing there with my hand on the windlass frame when I understood that Lira had not been watching the horse.

She was in the doorway of the inn with a cloth in her hand and a table half-wiped behind her, and every other face in that square was turned to Kellard and the cart, which is where a sensible face goes when there is a horse loose in a market. Hers was turned to the well. I could not tell you what she had seen or whether she had seen anything. I only know that when the whole square looked one way, she looked the other, and that when she caught me noticing it she gave me the ordinary nod, measured and neutral as a line in a ledger, and went back to her table without hurry. Anyone else would have said something. *Near thing, that.* Something. She only finished her table and carried the cloth inside, and I marked it the way you mark a quiet where you had braced for a noise.

What I made of it, with my hand still on the windlass, was that the valley was closer than I had given it credit for. Ardenmoor did not talk about things it had decided not to talk about, and I was pleased to have found that out, in the small proprietary way you are pleased when a place you have chosen turns out to hold the virtues you hoped for. I put her quiet down as weather. It cost me nothing to have, and I did not ask what it cost her to keep, then or after.

The square put itself back together the way squares do, slowly, and with no shortage of commentary.

By then the horse was standing with its head down and its sides going, being talked to by one of the men off the oven wall. He had a hand flat on its neck and was informing it, in a low unhurried monotone, that it was a fool. The other man had gone down on his heels and was running a palm the length of each leg in turn, front and back, taking his time about it. He stood up and said there was nothing. Kellard said aye. Neither of them looked satisfied. Somebody lately frightened by an animal likes very much to be able to point at something.

The egg-woman had her basket up on the coping and was counting into it. She got to the end and started again, the way you do.

"Three," she said.

"Put them on my book," said Kellard.

"I'll not put three eggs on your book."

"Then I'll pay you for three eggs."

"You'll pay me for two. One of them was cracked before that horse was born." She said it without any particular warmth and without any particular grudge. The whole negotiation took less time than it has taken me to set it down, and ended with two coppers going out of his hand into hers, and both of them satisfied, and her opinion of carters left standing exactly where she had put it.

Then the well.

The ironwork did precisely what I had wanted it to do. It gave the square something to be right about.

The big fellow off the oven wall came over and put his own hands on the frame and shook it exactly as I had shaken it, and said his father had said that same thing about that same well, and his father was nine years dead. A second man said it was not the ironwork at all, it was the rope. The rope had run through the sheave so long it had cut its own groove in the wood, and a rope in a groove pulls out of true, and there was your looseness. A third held for the coping, which had been the trouble in his view since before either of them was born.

I had introduced nothing. Every one of those positions was there already, finished and furnished and lived in, and had been there for a generation; my one sentence had done no more than open the cupboard door on it. I have been more careful since about what I hand a village.

They put it to me in the end, as the wizard, which happens perhaps twice a year and is never once about anything I would call my trade. I said I was no cooper and no smith, that the frame wanted a man with a hammer rather than a man with a book, and that if somebody drew the pin and looked in the socket he would very likely find the answer had been sitting there in plain sight the whole time. This satisfied everybody. It awarded the argument to nobody, and it gave them all a next step that not one of them had the smallest intention of taking.

And the entire while, at my elbow, a boy of ten wound a full bucket up out of the dark and set it on the coping without spilling a drop, and not one of those men looked round at him once.

Then Kellard wanted a hand with the tailgate, and I let it go.

The tailgate of that cart was a board of elm the better part of two inches thick, hung on two pins. When the cart came round against the stonework the offside pin had taken the shock and gone stiff in its socket. Kellard had the board half up and could not get it the rest of the way, and he was never going to say so out loud.

"Lift your end," I said.

We lifted. It came up an inch and stopped.

"It's the pin," he said.

"It's the pin."

We set the board down again, and he went round to have a proper look at it, and I stood at my end with my hand flat on the warm elm and my back to the well. That is where I let it go.

I could hear the crank the whole time I stood there. The turn, and the catch of the pawl, and the turn again, and then the small sound of a wet bucket coming over stone. There was a board of elm in my two hands and a man swearing quietly at a pin four feet off and a boy at my back drawing the inn's water for the second time that morning, and somewhere between the first lift and the second I stopped intending to say anything at all, and did not notice that I had stopped.

He went off to hunt out a bit of iron to knock the pin round with.

It was Cael who made it hard.

He came across the square while I was helping Kellard, in no hurry, in the way he had of arriving somewhere a good while after he had decided to, and he stood by the well and looked at the wet cobbles and at his grandson's soaked sleeve, and I saw, in the particular set of his face, that he had been closer than I had thought. Not close enough to have seen it. Close enough to have seen something.

He was not left in peace. The big fellow off the oven wall was on him almost before he had stopped walking, wanting a ruling about the well, and laid it all out again from the beginning. The frame, the rope, the sheave, his father who was nine years dead and had held the same view.

Cael heard him to the end of it. He had a way of hearing a man out that gave away nothing and cost him nothing.

"It'll keep till Wednesday," he said.

And it did keep till Wednesday, and I believe it kept a good many Wednesdays after that one, and the man went off perfectly contented, because he had never wanted the well mended. He had wanted somebody with standing in the parish to agree that it was a disgrace.

Then the square went about its business and left Cael and me alone at the coping, and he said nothing for a while. The spilled water had run out from the foot of the well to the low side of the cobbles in a dark tongue, and it was going off already at the edges in that heat. We watched it dry. Somewhere behind us the crank went round and the rope came in, and neither of us turned to look at it.

"Bucket went over," he said.

"It did. The wheel caught the stonework."

"Aye." He looked at the coping, and at the ground below it where the water had gone, and I watched him measure a distance with his eyes, the way any man who has spent sixty years watching things fall would measure it, and arrive at a small wrongness in the arithmetic. He was quiet for a moment longer than the subject called for.

Then he said, "Boy's quick."

And that was it. That was the whole of it, and it was said in a voice that closed the subject the way you close a gate you have decided not to walk through, and I understood that Cael had come to the edge of a thought about his daughter's son and had turned round and walked back from it, deliberately, with his eyes open.

I could have opened it. That is the thing I have turned over most, since. I had the whole of it in my mouth: that the bucket had moved, that I had seen it move, that his grandson had a thing in him that would one day be worth more than this valley, and that a boy like that wants watching over by somebody who knows what he is watching. Two sentences and it would have been said.

"He is," I said. "He's got good hands."

"Gets that off his mother." Cael put his own hand on the boy's shoulder as Soren came past with the refilled bucket, and shook him once, the rough affectionate shake old men give boys they are proud of, and said, "Mind you're not late with that," and Soren said he wasn't ever late and Cael said that was a lie of long standing, and the two of them went off up the street bickering comfortably, and that was the end of the well incident, and nobody in Ardenmoor ever spoke of it again, because as far as Ardenmoor was concerned nothing had occurred.

Kellard got his bit of iron under the pin at last and knocked it round, and it went, and we put the board up and he dropped the far pin through and gave the whole thing a slap with the flat of his hand. Men do that to a mended article they do not yet entirely trust.

"You wanted something," he said.

"Waxed thread."

He went to the box under the seat and came back with a spool and looked at the spool rather than at me while he named the price. It was the price it had always been, and no better for the lifting. Then he took the stub of a pencil out from behind his ear and made an entry, three words at the outside. I have no idea to this day whether he set down the thread, or the eggs, or the general state of his own morning.

I went into the inn afterward, because that was where I had been going when the morning happened.

It was cool inside and dark after the square, and empty, everybody being out at the carts. Lira was at the far end with her back to me, doing something to a shelf. She turned round and looked at me, and went and drew a mug of small beer, and set it on the good corner table without my having asked her for it, which is as near as that woman ever came to saying good morning.

I sat down.

"Beans are early," she said. "They'll be dear again inside the month, and then there'll be talk about it."

I said there generally was.

"There generally is." She wiped the next table along and put the cloth over her arm. "There's a jug on your slate from the week before last."

I paid her for the jug and for the beer, and she counted it and put it away. Nothing whatever was said about the well. There was no earthly reason for anything to be said about the well. I drank the beer. It was cold out of her cellar, and the day outside was getting up toward the sort of heat that makes a hole in the ground look like good sense. Then I set the mug back on the counter and went out.

I stood in the square a while with my bread going cold under my arm.

I will not pretend the reason I said nothing was a noble one, or that I had worked it through and arrived at wisdom. Some of it was simple cowardice, of the particular flavour a man gets when he sees a thing that will be difficult and can put off the difficulty for the price of one small silence. Some of it was Cael. He had looked at the arithmetic and set it down and named it quickness, and a man is entitled to decline a piece of knowledge about his own family, at least for a while, at least until it will not be declined any longer; and if I had forced it on him that morning in the square with his hand still on the boy's shoulder, I would have taken something off him he was not ready to give up, and I would have done it in public, on cobbles, next to a wet bucket.

But under both of those was the true reason, and the true reason was this. I knew exactly what happens to a child who is found early. There are hands in this world that reach for a talent the way a man reaches for a dropped tool, briskly, as a matter of course, because it is useful and it is there, and the child at the end of that reach is not asked a great deal about it. I had my own opinions on the subject, arrived at the slow way, and paid for by better people than me. It is enough to say that I had spent a good part of my life learning what it costs to be the most interesting thing in somebody else's room, and that I would not have that begin for Soren on an ordinary Tuesday, in a market square, over a bucket, before anyone had so much as asked the boy what he wanted.

He did not know. That was the great gift of it, and the great fragility. As long as he did not know, there was nothing in his face for anyone to read, and no story going round the valley, and no letter going anywhere, and the boy got to go on being a boy for another season, doing his chores and thinking his luck was luck.

I had that last item down as a certainty and would have argued it in front of anybody. There was nobody in that square with a reason to write and nobody to write to. The egg-woman had her eggs, Kellard had his horse, and Cael had walked back from the thought with his eyes open and would go on walking back from it, being the kind who could. I went over the morning's faces the way you go over a column you already know adds up. It added up.

So I decided to say nothing, and to watch him, and to be near enough that if it happened again, I would be the one standing there.

That was the decision. It was made in a market square with a cold loaf under my arm and no ceremony about it whatsoever. I am still not certain whether it was the right one or only the one I could bear.

I caught him up on the track home, because he was going my way as far as his grandfather's.

He began with the horse, and he had plainly been holding it in since the square.

"He hung on the bridle," he said.

"He did."

"You're meant to go to its head. Not hang on it." He walked a few paces on that, to give me time to get there myself. "If you hang on the bridle you're one more thing pulling at its mouth, and it's already got a thing pulling at it, which is the cart. So now it's got two, and one of them is shouting."

"Where did you get that?"

"Grandda." He said it the way another man cites a book. "A horse that's had a fright wants standing with. You go to its head and you say nothing at all and you let it be frightened until it's finished being frightened. Shouting doesn't do it. It only puts you inside the fright along with the horse."

I said that was better sense than I had heard out of men who dealt in horses for a living. He took the compliment the way he took everything, turning it over first to see whether it was true and accepting it only after.

We went on past the last of the gardens. The heat was coming up off the road by then and the beck ran low and loud in its stones, and my loaf had gone from warm to merely heavy.

"It wasn't his fault, though," he said.

"Kellard's?"

"The dog's."

I had not been aware we had got as far as the dog. We had got as far as the dog.

"Nobody knows there was a dog," I said.

"There's always a dog."

He stopped in the middle of the track to work it out, being at that time a boy who could not walk and think at the same time once the thinking got steep.

"If it barked," he said, "then it did what a dog is. You can't blame a thing for being the thing it is."

"That seems fair."

"And if it went at the horse and bit it, that's the dog's, and he ought to be tied up."

"Also fair."

"But it didn't bite it. It only might have." He held his hands out flat, one for each half of the business, the way I had watched his grandfather hold a hurdle up to see whether it sat square. "So what he's being blamed for is the might have. And you can't blame anything for a might have. There's no end to them. You'd be blaming the whole valley all day and never get your water drawn."

I asked whether that held for people as well.

He thought about it the better part of the way up the rise.

"No," he said.

"No?"

"A man knows he might." He was not pleased about it. It had come out of the sum and he had no choice but to take it. "He's had a go at the thing in his head before ever he does it. So he can stop, and a dog can't stop. That's what makes it his."

"That's harder on men."

"Well," said Soren. "They know more."

I walked on a while and did not say anything.

A dog barked somewhere across the beck, a long way off, two notes and then nothing.

"That one," said Soren.

"That one is a mile off and has been all morning."

"He could have come back."

I said that a dog who could get from the square to the far bank in the time it took Kellard to tie a horse was a dog worth putting money on. Soren allowed that this was probably so, and gave the animal up with visible regret. He had been at that dog since the last of the gardens and would have gone on being at it if there had been any of it left to be at. Nothing ever got put down by him half looked at.

Then he wanted to know about the rope.

"Is it true it's cut a groove in the wheel?"

"I'd say it's likely. The same rope running in the same place for as long as that will cut a groove in most things."

"Then the bucket doesn't come up straight." He was ahead of me now and pleased to be. "It comes up leaning. And if it comes up leaning it wears the groove deeper, and then it leans worse."

"Yes."

"So it gets worse by itself." He weighed how large a thing he had just said. "Everything that's wrong gets worse by itself."

I said that was true of a fair number of things, and that most of the trades I knew of lived on it, mine included.

At the turn where the path bends up toward the hill he stopped, and looked at his still-damp sleeve, and laughed.

"Grandda says I've good hands," he said, in the tone of a boy repeating a compliment he does not quite trust.

"You have."

"I nearly had that bucket on my foot."

"But you didn't," I said.

"No," he said, pleased, and went on up to his grandfather's smoke, and I stood at the turn and watched him go, carrying a thing he did not know he had, in a valley where nobody had noticed, and I let him.
