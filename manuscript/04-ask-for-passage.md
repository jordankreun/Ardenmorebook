# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Four: Ask for Passage

I have said I meant to learn the hill before I asked anything of it, and for those first weeks before summer I did nothing else. It is not a thing you can hurry, and it is not, to a man watching from the lane below, a thing that looks like work at all. I walked the ground in the early part of the day and the late. I lay on it at the hours it chose to speak and let the two slow pulses come up into me, the one at the failing of the light and the one in the cold before the birds, until I could have told you when they were coming the way you can tell a familiar step on a stair. I set out the instruments of my trade on a folding table under the canvas, the gauges and the glasses and the lengths of drawn wire, and I used them the way I had been taught to use them, which is to say to listen with, and not to command. A man in my line spends the better half of his life learning that the second thing is a good way to get himself killed, and the other half forgetting it.

What I was after, under all the walking and the lying still, was a plain enough thing, and it was this: I meant to build, and you cannot build on ground that will not hold. That was the whole of Cael's father's trouble, and the whole of the valley's forty years of certainty. It was not that the hill was steep, for the crown is flat as a table and always has been. It was that nothing set on it stayed set. Foundations that went down true came up crooked in a season. Mortar that cured hard everywhere else in the country stayed soft here for a month and then let go. Walls came down in the night with no wind to blame. I had heard it from Cael on the crown in the gold light, and I had felt, in the weeks since, exactly the quality of ground he meant. It was not dead ground. Dead ground is honest; it takes what you give it and keeps it. This ground was awake, and it had been throwing off everything set upon it for longer than the village had a name, the way a sleeping man throws off a blanket he never asked for.

I learned things in those weeks that no book of my trade had ever taught me, because no book of my trade had ever been written about a hill like this one. I learned that the two pulses were not always the same size. On a still fair evening the dusk-fail pulse was a small thing, hardly there, a low word spoken in another room. But on the evening before weather it came up stronger and a little sooner, and stronger again the harder the weather meant to be, so that after a while I could stand on the crown at the failing of the light and tell you, with more confidence than the sky gave me any right to, whether the next day would be wet. I got a name in the village for it before I ever understood it myself. Maret's boy, who watched the hill from the mill-yard the way boys watch anything strange, told his mother the wizard could smell rain three fields off, and it went round from there, and I did not correct it, because it was near enough true and because a man in a new place takes what small coin of goodwill he is given.

I learned, too, that the hill would not be hurried, and that it knew the difference between attention and appetite. There was a morning early on when I went up impatient, wanting to have got somewhere, wanting the thing to declare itself so I could be about the business of it, and I lay down on the crown and reached for the pulse the way you reach for a cup in the dark, and there was nothing there at all. Not silence. Absence. The particular flat nothing of a room a person has just left. I lay there a good while feeling like a fool, which is a feeling I was getting a great deal of use out of that spring, and eventually I understood that I had come up wanting to take something, and that the taking had been felt, and that the hill had simply declined to be at home to it. I went down and did not go up again until I could go up wanting nothing, which took me the better part of two days, and when I did the pulse was there again at the failing of the light, unbothered, patient, as if it had never gone anywhere, which of course it had not. I had. That was a lesson worth more than the whole surveyor's compendium, and the hill charged me nothing for it but my pride, which I could well afford.

So I sat down one grey afternoon with the rain ticking on the canvas and drafted the working that would make it hold.

I want to be plain about what I did, because it matters to everything after. I drafted it the way I had drafted a hundred workings before it, the way any competent man in my trade would have drafted it, which is to say I drafted it to win. A reshaping ward is a large and intricate thing, and it does what its name says: it takes hold of the deep layer of a place, the slow shifting stone under the soil that no spade ever reaches, and it tells that layer what shape to keep. You set your anchors at the corners and you feel down for the deep grain and you bind it, and thereafter the ground holds the footing you have given it because it no longer has any say in the matter. It is a fair-mason's working, if a mason could reach that far down. It is exactly what Cael's father did with his four square walls and his stubbornness, only I could reach the layer his mortar never could, and I did not doubt for a moment that I could make it obey.

I had the whole of it laid out on paper by the time the rain stopped, elegant and sound, and I sat and looked at it in the last of the light and found that I did not want to cast it.

I put it down to tiredness at first, and then to the strangeness of the place, and then, because I am an honest man on paper if nowhere else, I put it down to the truth, which was that I was not sure it would work. Or rather I thought the working would work exactly as well as such things always work, which is completely. I would bind the layer, and the footing would hold through the building of it, and I would raise my walls true, and for a year, or two, or five, they would stand. And then some night with no wind the hill would remember that it had a say after all, and it would shrug, the way it had shrugged off every wall in two lifetimes, and I would come down in the morning to a heap of good stone and the particular silence of a valley that had told me so. I could not set the village folk's warning out of my mind.

It was that evening, or one very like it, that I went looking through my books for a better idea, and found one.

Among my books, long forgotten yet held for exactly this rare moment in which it would be required, was a surveyor's compendium a good deal older than I was, a fat foxed volume of tables and methods for the reading and reckoning of ground. I had bought it out of professional curiosity from the estate of a man I never met, a surveyor dead some sixty years, and I had carried it about for most of my working life and consulted it perhaps twice. Surveyors and men of my trade are distant cousins who do not much like to admit the relation; they read the world with rods and chains and I read it with worse-named things. I took the book down that night for its tables on deep-layer behavior, which are as good as any ever made, and I did not find what I was looking for, and I found what I needed instead.

It was in the margin of a page on subsidence, in a third hand, faded to the color of weak tea, cramped into the white space.

*When dealing with a particularly challenging site, or one that a conventional structure has failed upon, it may be necessary to adapt your plans more closely to the natural features of the given site. Do not ask for level. Ask for passage. The ground knows the difference.*

I read it, and I sat back, and the rain that had started again went on ticking at the canvas, and I understood that I had been about to do a stupid thing with a great deal of skill.

The whole of it did not come to me at once. But the shape of it did, there in the lamplight with the old book open on my knee, and the shape of it was the same shape I had felt on the crown that first morning without a name to put to it, and the same shape that had stopped my hand over a working I knew would fail. Cael's father had asked the hill for level. He had told it, rather, in the only grammar he had: four square walls, a floor that did not slope, a footing that would keep the shape he gave it whether the ground consented or not. And the hill had refused him, not out of malice, for there is no malice in it, but the way you would refuse a stranger who came into your house and began moving the furniture. I had been about to do the same thing with a longer reach and a heavier hand, and I would have got the same answer, only later, and at more expense.

There is a great difference between a thing that cannot be built on and a thing that has never once been asked. You go to it not with a shape to impose but with a question, and the question is nearly a courtesy: *here is a weight I mean to set down, and here is roughly where; will you carry it, and if not here, then where?* And you make yourself able to hear the answer, which is the hard part, and the part no chain or rod or gauge will do for you. The ground knows the difference between a command and a request the way a dog knows it, or a child, or a horse who has decided some hours back that you do not know where you are going. I had spent twenty years learning to command ground and had got very good at it. I was about to spend the rest of a much longer life learning to ask. I have never been anything but a beginner at it, and I would not trade the beginning for all the mastery of the other thing.

I did not cast anything that night. You do not walk up to awake ground the day you have decided to be polite to it and expect to be believed. I spent three more days in the doing of a thing that looked, again, like nothing at all: unpicking the working I had drafted, thread by thread, and building in its place something I had no proper name for and still do not, a working that was almost entirely question and hardly at all instruction. Where the reshaping ward had four anchors driven down to bind the deep grain, my new thing had four anchors laid on it, resting, the way you rest a hand on a shoulder rather than take hold of an arm. Where the ward carried a shape to force, mine carried a shape to offer, and a great deal of room around the shape for the ground to answer back with a different one. It was, my old teachers would have said, a working with no spine in it, and they would have been right, and that was the point. I did not want a spine. Cael's father had brought a spine. I wanted a pair of open hands.

I cast it on a still evening at the hour the hill likes to speak, because it seemed only manners to knock when someone is known to be home.

I will not make more of the doing of it than there was. There was no flash, no thunder, none of the furniture a story would give it. I knelt on the wet grass of the crown with my four anchors set out at the compass points and the last of them at the northeast, and I put my palms flat on the ground the way the boy had put his flat on the stone, and I asked. Not aloud. You do not do it aloud; the aloud part is for stories. I put the question down into the ground the way you lower a bucket into a well you cannot see the bottom of, and I let go of wanting any particular answer, which is the whole of the trick and takes a lifetime, and I waited.

For a long moment there was nothing but the cold coming up through my knees and the small sounds of the evening settling and my own heart being louder than I would have liked. And then the hill answered.

I felt it the way I had felt the pulse in the dark, below sound, up through the bone, except that this was not the hill keeping its own hours and taking no notice of me. This was the hill turning its attention on the question I had set down, the way a sleeping man turns his head toward a voice that has finally said something worth waking for. There was a long slow settling in the deep layer beneath me, unhurried, enormous, patient as the tide, and it was not the settling of ground being forced into a shape. It was the settling of ground choosing one. I felt the deep grain, that slow shifting stone that had thrown off two lifetimes of walls, gather itself under the crown and offer me a footing, a true and level and load-bearing place, a good deal wider and a good deal better sited than the one I had drafted, offset some little way toward the northeast of where I had thought to build, because the hill, it turned out, knew where it wanted to carry a weight, and it was not quite where I had meant to set one. The ground took the shape I had asked it for the way dry earth takes rain, not gratefully, for there is no gratitude in it, but completely, and as though it had been waiting a very long time to be asked a question it could say yes to.

Then it was done, and the crown was quiet. I was kneeling on ground that would hold anything I cared to build on it, more tired than I had been in years and could not for a while make my hands stop shaking, and I was not entirely sure, when I put my face down against the cold wet grass, whether what I felt going out of me was the cost of the working, which is real and must always be paid, or something older that had been looking for a way out for three years and had finally found a crack to go through.

I do not know how long I lay like that. Long enough that the wet had gone through my coat at the knees and the elbows and I had stopped noticing it. The cost of a working like that one is not a thing you can put in a ledger, though I have tried; it is paid out of the same account a long day's honest labor is paid from, and I have known men in my trade spend themselves to nothing reaching too far and asking too much. I had reached about as far as I ever had. But it was not only the cost. It was that the ground under my face was different now in a way I had no instrument to measure. It had been ground I stood on. It was ground that had answered me. A man who has spent three years making very sure that nothing in the world required anything of him, or offered him anything either, does not quite know what to do with being answered. I lay there and let it be true a while before I had to get up and be a sensible man about it again.

When I lifted my head the boy was there.

He was at the edge of the crown where the grass goes long, standing very still in the last of the light, and he was looking at me, and then he was looking at the ground between us with the particular attention I had marked in him from the first, the attention of a child at his real work. I do not know how long he had been there. I had not heard him come, and Soren, when he did not want to be heard, was quieter than weather. He did not ask me what I had done. He never asked me anything, that boy, then or for a long time after, and I have told you already how grateful I was for it. But he crossed the crown to where I knelt, and he crouched down beside me, and he laid his own small hand flat on the ground the way he had laid it on the stone the day we walked the bounds, and he held it there a while with his head tipped, listening to something, and then he took it off and looked at me, and he said the only thing either of us said on that hill that whole evening.

"You've started," he said.

It was not a question. I have thought about those two words a great deal in the years since, more than a man should probably admit to thinking about anything a nine-year-old said to him on a hill. At the time I only nodded, because it was true, and because there did not seem to be anything to add to it, and because my voice, if I am honest, was not to be relied upon just then. He nodded back, satisfied, the way you nod when a thing you already knew is finally said out loud, and he got up and went off down the track to his grandfather's fire in the dark, sure-footed as a cat, and left me alone on the ground that had answered me.

I sat up there a long time after he had gone, until the cold drove me in, the way it always did. Below me the valley's windows went dark one by one, in no order a man could ever learn. Out in the north wood the old oak was a darker weight against the dark, keeping its own counsel, as it had kept it for longer than anyone could say. And under me, all the way down through the good black soil to the slow shifting stone, was a hill that had thrown off every wall any soul had ever tried to set on it, and had that evening, for the first time in two lifetimes that Cael could account for and God knows how many that he could not, been asked instead of told, and had said yes.

I did not build anything that night, or the next day. A man does not rush from the asking to the taking; it is a poor sort of guest who is through the door before the welcome is finished. But I lay down that night on ground that felt, for the first time, less like a thing I had bought and more like a thing that had agreed to keep me, and in the morning, grey and ordinary and giving no sign of anything, I got up and I made my tea and I walked down the track into the village to find the one man in three valleys who might raise a wall on that hill and not laugh at me for asking, and might, if I was very fortunate, believe me when I told him the ground was ready.
