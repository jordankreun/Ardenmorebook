# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Four: Ask for Passage

Learning a hill looks, to anybody watching from the lane below, like nothing at all. It cannot be hurried either. For those first weeks before summer I did nothing else. I walked the ground in the early part of the day and the late. I lay on it at the hours it chose to speak and let the two slow pulses come up into me, the one at the failing of the light and the one in the cold before the birds, until I knew them coming the way you know a familiar step on a stair. I set out the instruments of my trade on a folding table under the canvas, the gauges and the glasses and the lengths of drawn wire, and I used them the way I had come round to using them, which is to say to listen with, and not to command.

One of those days will do for all of them, and the shape of it is the shape of the trade.

I woke before the birds. That was not discipline. It was the hill, or my body learning to expect the hill, and after the first week I stopped troubling to decide which. I lay still in the dark under the canvas with my back flat to the groundsheet and waited. In the coldest part of that hour it came up through me, one slow beat, and went, and left the night exactly as it had found it. Then I got up.

The fire wanted rebuilding every morning of that spring. I had the trick of it by the end. Dry grass out of the tin I kept it in. The fine stuff split the evening before and slept on to keep it dry. The kettle set over before the flame had properly caught, so that the water and the fire came up together and neither had to wait on the other. I drank my tea standing, in the dark, with both hands round the cup. Nothing in the practice of my trade ever did me the good that cup did.

Soren came up with the bread while the light was still grey. He did not knock, there being nothing to knock on, and he did not call out. He set the loaf on the flat stone by the fire where I had set it the first morning, and he looked at the kettle, and he looked at the hill, and he went off down the track again about his own morning. Some mornings he said good morning. Most he did not.

Then the instruments.

I set my folding table outside the tent-mouth when the weather allowed it and inside when it did not, and I laid the cases out on it in an order my hands had learned twenty years before. There is a notion abroad that a wizard's tools look like something. Mine looked like a watchmaker's. Callipers, three pairs, the smallest no wider across the jaw than a finger joint. Fine files in a roll of oiled flannel. A jeweller's glass on a lanyard. A brass scale with a pan the size of a thumbnail and a case of drawn-wire weights to go in it. Gauges, glasses, a spirit level in a box with a sliding lid, and drawn wire in half a dozen thicknesses, annealed and hard, each in its own paper. Every piece of it was bought singly and slowly across twenty years, and there was never a year in the twenty when I could have afforded the lot of it at once.

They told me nothing.

I want to be exact about that, because it was the real finding of those weeks and it took me a while to stop resenting it. I hung a plumb of soft wire from a tripod of cut hazel over the middle of the crown and sat by it through a whole dusk hour with the glass to my eye, and it hung as true as a plumb hangs in a shut room. I dusted fine sand across a slate and set the slate flat on the bare turf and watched the sand, and the sand lay where I had put it. I loaded the brass scale to a hair of balance and left it under a box, out of the wind, through the cold hour before the birds, and in the morning it was where I had left it, hair for hair.

Whatever the hill was doing, it was not doing it to iron or glass or a pan of brass. It was doing it to me, and the one instrument on that ground that ever answered was the one I had spent twenty years learning not to trust.

I went on setting the cases out every morning regardless. It gave the morning a shape, and I was not yet ready to say out loud that the answer was coming up my own legs or not at all.

The middle of the day was the hill's dead hour and mine. It kept nothing back then. There was simply nothing to keep. So the middle of the day was when I walked, and I walked the bounds of what I had bought until my boots knew them without me. Down the north side into the wet, where the ground turns from turf to rush in the space of a stride. Along the skirt of the woodland with the larch stand on my left, set out in ranks by a dead man and going up straight and useless and handsome. Over the beck at the flat place and up the far side, and back round the eastern shoulder where the grass runs deeper and greener in a shelf about the size of a barn floor.

Then up again in the afternoon for the small work of keeping myself alive in a field: the wood, the water, the wet things spread along the guy-lines, my notes written up in a hand my father would have accepted. And an hour before the light began to go, I lay down on the crown.

Lying down flat on wet ground on purpose, every evening, in front of a valley that can see the crown from its own doorsteps, is not a dignified way for a grown man to spend a spring. I did it for six weeks. I lay on my back with my arms out and the wet coming through at the shoulder blades first, and I let the light go, and I waited.

It came. Never at the same minute twice, and never with the least warning in the air before it, but it came: a long low turning-over in the ground beneath my back, and the small hairs going up along my forearms half a second before I had any business knowing why. Then nothing. Then the ordinary evening, and the ordinary cold, and a man getting up off wet grass with his knees stiff under him, and going in to see about his supper.

What I was after, under all the walking and the lying still, was a plain enough thing, and it was this: I meant to build, and you cannot build on ground that will not hold. That was the whole of Cael's father's trouble, and the whole of the valley's forty years of certainty. It was not that the hill was steep, for the crown is flat enough and always has been. It was that nothing set on it stayed set. Foundations that went down true came up crooked in a season. Mortar that cured hard everywhere else in the country stayed soft here for a month and then let go. Walls came down in the night with no wind to blame. I had heard it from Cael on the crown in the gold light, and I had felt, in the weeks since, exactly the quality of ground he meant. It was not dead ground. Dead ground is honest. It takes what you give it and keeps it. This ground was awake, and it had been throwing off everything set upon it for longer than the village had a name, shrugging off a blanket it had never asked for.

There was evidence of that on the crown, and I had been careful with it. Cael had pointed it out with his stick on the day we walked the bounds, and I had sat on it for an hour in my first week with my hat off and my hands in my lap and had not shifted a stone of it, because a man's failed work is his own and his son was alive and not a mile off. Two generations of grass had gone over it since that summer. There was no line left to any of it. What had been four walls was a scatter now, a long low swell in the turf with the nettles standing thick along one flank of it, and out in the open at the near end two dressed stones lying with their squared faces up.

I went back to it one morning well on in that spring, and this time I went at it with my hands and a stick. That is as near to digging as I had any business getting on another man's summer.

I did not touch the scatter. What I wanted was under it, and nobody had ever looked at it, and I could get at it without shifting a stone that had a right to lie where it lay. I took the turf off a strip along the flank where the swell ran straightest, cutting it in mats, root and soil together, the way old turf will come up if you are patient with it, and laying the mats out in a row on their faces to go back down after.

The footing was a hand under the grass, and it stopped me where I knelt.

It was a proper trench, and it was far deeper than the job had any call to be. Cut down through the soft to the firm and then further still, no shallower on the downhill side than the up, packed with small stone set on edge and rammed home. Cael had told me his father put the first wall up and had it down in the night and set it again deeper. Here was the deeper. Nobody cuts a trench like that in good spirits. He cut it angry and he cut it well. I went along it on my knees the better part of two hours with the wet coming through, and near the corner I found the place where he had taken his packing out and put it back again, because the first go of it had not satisfied him.

Then I laid my level along it.

I had it out of habit and expected nothing of it. What it told me I sat back on my heels over. That trench had been cut level. It was not level now. It went away from me along its length, not by much, a finger's width in a stride, and not the same finger's width all the way either, so that the fall in it was not the fall a slope makes. It wandered. Ground that settles settles the one way and keeps at it, and you can carry that in your head and allow for it. This had gone one way and then gone another and had finished up somewhere it had never started from.

He had made that trench level. And the ground had waited for his back to be turned and taken it somewhere else.

There was nothing wrong with the work.

That was the whole of what the heap had to say to me. It took a morning and the half of an afternoon on my knees in the wet to hear it, and when I had heard it I sat there with my hands stinging and admitted that for a fortnight I had been telling myself the man was not much of a mason. Cael had said fair. Cael had been modest on his father's behalf, and the trench in the ground said a long way better than fair. I have built things that are still standing, and I would have been glad to put my name under that trench.

I put the mats back over it, every one into its own place, and trod the seams closed with my boot. It was not mine to leave open.

I learned things in those weeks that no book of my trade had ever taught me, and it took me a while to see why. It was not that the books were bad. It was that every one of them, from the first primer to the papers I had put my own name to, was a book about making the world do a thing. That is what a working is. You find the grain of a place, and you take hold of it, and you tell it what to be. Every place has one; that is not a remarkable claim, it is the first page of the first primer. A field has a grain and a quarry has a grain and the floor of the sea has a grain, and in all of them it lies still under the hand and takes the shape it is given and makes nothing of it, there being nothing there to make anything of anything. There is a whole fine literature of that and I had spent my life in it, and there is not a page in the whole of it about ground that answers you back.

I had done the ordinary kind often enough to know what it feels like in the hands.

Years back in Vethmark I was paid to get a footing under the floor of a merchant's warehouse on the made ground by the river. Made ground is not ground at all. It is three generations of rubble and river silt with a street laid over the top of it, and it goes on quietly settling long after everyone who dumped it is dead. This man's floor was going down an inch a year at the east end and taking his wall with it. I went in on the Monday with a boy to carry for me. I found the grain of that made ground the same afternoon, flat on my back under the joists in the dark, and it was where it always is, dull and patient and with nothing in it. On the Tuesday I took hold of it. Before the week was out I had told it what to be, and it became that, and I have no doubt it is being it still, because there was never anything there to change its mind.

The merchant paid me and did not ask how it had been done. I ached for four days after, in the back and the forearms and behind the eyes, and ate like a man who had been digging, and slept as though I had been struck. That is the cost, and the cost is honest. The ground made nothing whatever of me. It did not know I had been in the room.

Every working in the whole literature is that working in different clothes for different trades. Find the grain. Take hold. Tell it. A dozen men I trained with were doing exactly that under a dozen city floors that same week, and doing it well, and going home afterwards to their suppers.

Nobody had troubled to write the other book. Until that spring I had never once stood on ground that would have wanted it.

I learned that the two pulses were not always the same size. On a still fair evening the dusk-fail pulse was a small thing, hardly there, a low word spoken in another room. But on the evening before weather it came up stronger and a little sooner, and stronger again the harder the weather meant to be, so that after a while I could stand on the crown at the failing of the light and tell you, with more confidence than the sky gave me any right to, whether the next day would be wet. I got a name in the village for it before I ever understood it myself. Maret's boy, who watched the hill from the mill-yard the way boys watch anything strange, told his mother the wizard could smell rain three fields off, and it went round from there, and I did not correct it, because it was near enough true and because a man in a new place takes what small coin of goodwill he is given.

It came back to me about ten days later, in Lira's, in a shape I would not have known for my own.

I had come down for salt and a tin of matches and stayed on because it had set in to rain in earnest and because a man can have too much of his own company. The taproom was two thirds full and warm and smelled of wet wool. Lira had a bowl of barley soup in front of me before I had settled which stool I meant to take.

"Room's paid to the end of the month," she said. "You've slept in it three nights of it."

"I know."

"So long as you know." She went off along the bar with the jug.

Down the bench a man I knew by his hands and not by his name leaned over.

"You'll be the one on Cael's hill."

I allowed that I was.

"They say you can tell the weather off it."

"After a fashion."

"How far off?"

I told him the truth. It cost me nothing and did me no good at all. I said I could sometimes say whether the next day would be wet, that I was wrong about one time in four, and that any shepherd of his acquaintance would beat me over a season.

He took that in and set it aside, being in possession of a better version.

"What I heard," he said, "is you smell it three fields off. Rain. Like a dog does."

The man next to him said it was four fields.

"Three," said the first. "I had it off the lad down at the mill."

"You had it off your wife. Who had it off the lad's mother."

"And the lad's mother had it off the lad," said the first man, with the air of a case closed, and drank.

It went round the bench from there and came back improved. By the second telling I could say how hard it would rain. By the third I could say how long. A dry-looking man at the end, who had not spoken until then and did not look up when he did, wanted to know whether the beck would come up over the flat crossing before the week was out, because he had a brother-in-law who meant to move some sheep.

I did not put any of it right. What I gave them instead was the only honest thing in my pocket. I said there was nothing up on that hill but grass and a tent and a man with a kettle, and that anybody who cared to walk up of an evening and see for himself how little there was to see would be welcome, and would get his tea out of it.

There was the kind of pause that follows an invitation nobody means to take up.

"Not on that hill I won't," said the man with the hands, and the bench laughed, and that was the end of it.

Lira came and took the bowl away and set down bread I had not asked for.

"Boy's been fetching your bread up in the mornings," she said.

"He has."

"His grandfather knows."

"I thought he would."

She looked at me a moment longer than the sentence needed. Then she took the bowl off to the back and said nothing else about it, and that was the last I ever heard of it.

I walked up in the rain with the salt in my coat and the matches inside my shirt where they would stay dry. A village will file a strange man under a trade the moment you hand it an excuse, and a man who knows about rain is a trade. It was not the one I had, and I was in no position to be particular.

I learned, too, that the hill would not be hurried, and that it knew the difference between attention and appetite. There was a morning early on when I went up impatient, wanting to have got somewhere, wanting the thing to declare itself so I could be about the business of it, and I lay down on the crown and reached for the pulse the way you reach for a cup in the dark, and there was nothing there at all. Not silence. Absence. The particular flat nothing of a room a person has just left. I lay there a good while with my palms flat on the grass, and nothing came up into them, and after a time I understood that I had come up wanting to take something, and that the taking had been felt, and that the hill had simply declined to be at home to it. I went down and did not go up again until I could go up wanting nothing, which took me the better part of two days, and when I did the pulse was there again at the failing of the light, unbothered, patient, as if it had never gone anywhere, which of course it had not. I had.

Two days is nothing at all to be sent away for. It is a very long stretch to spend learning how to want nothing, and I made a poor job of the first of them.

Snow settled the shape of it for me. She had been three weeks in a let stall at the inn with a full manger and no work in her, and she had her opinion of that ready when I came for her. She stood on the yard cobbles and let the saddle be put on without helping, and when I put my foot in the iron she stepped off two paces, and I got up at the second attempt.

We went out along the low road, past the mill and over the beck and on for the better part of an hour, into country I had bought and never seen. She was easier by the second mile and had forgiven me altogether by the fourth.

Coming back through the village in the middle of the afternoon I stopped at the low stone cottage at the end of the lane where the birches stand, because my hands were in a state after the morning I had spent among the fallen stones and had got no better since. There were herbs drying in the window in bunches, hung head down, and a walled garden behind it whose top I could see over the wall.

The woman who kept the place heard me out, took my right hand and turned it over into the light of the doorway without troubling to ask, and looked at the palm and the knuckles longer than I cared to be looked at.

"Stone," she said, and let go of it. "It's not the work. It's the wet along with the work. Dry hands take stone their whole lives and only go hard. Wet hands split. You'll want this in at night, a little of it, worked in properly, and none at all in the daytime or you'll be leaving it on everything you touch. The stiffness goes first and the cracking after, and it will be a week, so don't be washing it off every few hours to see whether it's working."

She fetched a small jar and named a price. It was a fair price and I paid it without discussion, having never in my life been able to argue over the cost of a thing I came in the door already wanting.

The second day I gave up on idleness and did work instead, and that turned out to be the cure, though I could not have told you so at the time.

I split and stacked a fortnight of wood. I cut a runnel above the tent on the uphill side to carry the water off, a hand's breadth deep and no more, running out and away round the shoulder, and it did its work the first night it rained and every night after. I set the fire-pit stones properly, having set them in a hurry the first week and put up with them since. I ground an edge back onto a hatchet that had none, sitting on an upturned crate with the stone in one hand and a can of water at my foot and the whole of the afternoon in front of me to do it in. I patched a tear in the canvas with waxed thread and a sailmaker's palm, and made a poor job of it, and unpicked the poor job and made a fair one.

And in the evening, setting the hatchet against the crate, I noticed that I had not thought about the hill since breakfast.

That was the whole of the penance. Two days, a jar of salve, and a fortnight of split wood I had no use for until autumn.

So I sat down one grey afternoon with the rain ticking on the canvas and drafted the working that would make it hold.

The board had come up the track on my back under an oilcloth in my first week on that hill, and I set it across two crates in the tent-mouth and pegged the flap wide in spite of the weather, because a man cannot draw in the dark. It was my father's board. He worked on it forty years and it is scored all over with the ghosts of other men's houses, and it was the one thing out of his office I asked for.

Then the ritual of it, and I use the word with no grandness in it, because a ritual is exactly what drawing is. Paper down and squared to the board. Brass weights at the four corners, plain and heavy and older than I am. The pencil sharpened long to a chisel on a scrap of sand-paper, and turned in the fingers as it goes so that it wears even and does not fatten. Rule, set square, and the ruling pen wiped and filled off a quill and tried on the waste corner until the line comes away clean and dead.

I had all of that at my father's elbow before I was twelve, with my chin about level with the edge of the board, and I had the rest of it from him too. You draw a thing as though the man who has to build it is standing behind you with his hands in his pockets and a low opinion of paper. Every line is a decision and every decision costs somebody money. Do not put down a line you cannot answer for.

It is a strange hand to bring to a working, since a working has no client and no builder and nobody at all standing behind you. But the discipline is the same discipline. You cannot hold a thing of that size in your head. You can hold about a third of it, and when you move on to the next third the first quietly falls out of your hands. So it goes on paper, in a hand you can read cold at midnight, and the paper carries the parts you are not thinking about.

I drew for the rest of that afternoon and on into the lamp.

I drafted it the way I had drafted a hundred workings before it, the way any competent man in my trade would have drafted it, which is to say I drafted it to win. A reshaping ward is a large and intricate thing, and it does what its name says: it takes hold of the deep layer of a place, the slow shifting stone under the soil that no spade ever reaches, and it tells that layer what shape to keep. You set your anchors at the corners and you feel down for the deep grain and you bind it, and thereafter the ground holds the footing you have given it because it no longer has any say in the matter. It is a fair-mason's working, if a mason could reach that far down. It is exactly what Cael's father did with his four square walls and his stubbornness, only I could reach the layer his mortar never could, and I did not doubt for a moment that I could make it obey.

I had the whole of it laid out on paper by the time the rain stopped, elegant and sound, and I sat and looked at it in the last of the light and found that I did not want to cast it.

I put it down to tiredness at first, and then to the strangeness of the place, and then I put it down to the truth, which was that I was not sure it would work. Or rather I thought the working would work exactly as well as such things always work, which is completely. I would bind the layer, and the footing would hold through the building of it, and I would raise my walls true, and for a year, or two, or five, they would stand. And then some night with no wind the hill would remember that it had a say after all, and it would shrug, the way it had shrugged off every wall in two lifetimes, and I would come down in the morning to a heap of good stone and the particular silence of a valley that had told me so. I could not get the room's warning out of my mind.

It was that evening, or one very like it, that I went looking through my books for a better idea, and found one.

The most of my books were still crated in the room I was paying for down at the inn, and it was raining, and the mile of track between was wet grass going dark.

I went anyway. I came into the taproom with the water running off the hem of my coat and half the room turning to watch it happen, and went up to a room I had slept in three nights that month, and got the shutters back for what light was left, and started on the crates.

There were nine of them. I had packed them myself in Vethmark in no order whatever, being in a hurry to be gone, and I paid for that hurry for the better part of an hour. The first crate was papers of my own I had thought worth keeping, and I was not glad to see them. The second was primers and the standard works, the whole scaffolding of a training, and I went through the lot of it properly, because a man in difficulty goes back to the primers. There was nothing there. I had known before I opened it that there would be nothing there, and I went through it anyway, the way you check a pocket you have already checked.

The third crate was where I had put the things that fitted nowhere else. A treatise on the deep layer that has been superseded twice and is still the best thing anyone has written on the subject. Two volumes of a set of three, the third having gone missing in a house I no longer own. And at the bottom of it, under a folded coat I had packed in to keep the corners off the boards, a fat foxed quarto with a broken back that I had not put a hand on in eleven years.

I took that and the treatise and one other that I thought might serve, and I did not open any of them there, because the light was going and the room was cold and I wanted my own fire.

Lira was at the foot of the stairs with a lamp when I came down with three books held under my coat and my arms crossed over them. She looked at the shape of me for a moment. Then she went into the back and came out with a flour sack and held it open, and I put the books into it, and she gave me the neck of it and went off along the bar. She did not say one word.

The fat quarto mattered, and I did not know it going up the track with the rain in my collar and the sack held against my chest.

It was a surveyor's compendium a good deal older than I was, tables and methods for the reading and reckoning of ground. I had bought it out of curiosity from the estate of a man I never met, a surveyor dead some sixty years, and I had carried it about for most of my working life and opened it twice. Surveyors and men of my trade are distant cousins who do not much like to admit the relation; they read the world with rods and chains and I read it with worse-named things. I took it down that night for its tables on deep-layer behavior, the best any surveyor ever made, and I did not find what I was looking for, and I found what I needed instead.

It was in the margin of a page on subsidence, in a third hand, faded to the color of weak tea, cramped into the white space.

*When dealing with a particularly challenging site, or one that a conventional structure has failed upon, it may be necessary to adapt your plans more closely to the natural features of the given site. Do not ask for level. Ask for passage. The ground knows the difference.*

I read it. I sat back. The rain had started again and went on ticking at the canvas, and I saw that I had been about to do a stupid thing with a great deal of skill.

The whole of it did not come to me at once. The shape of it did, there in the lamplight with the old book open on my knee, and it was the shape I had felt on the crown that first morning and had no name for, and the shape that had stopped my hand over a working I knew would fail. Cael's father had asked the hill for level. He had told it, rather, in the only grammar he had: four square walls, a floor that did not slope, a footing that would keep the shape he gave it whether the ground consented or not. And the hill had refused him, not out of malice, for there is no malice in it, but the way you would refuse a stranger who came into your house and began moving the furniture. I had been about to do the same thing with a longer reach and a heavier hand, and I would have got the same answer, only later, and at more expense.

There is a great difference between a thing that cannot be built on and a thing that has never once been asked. You go to it not with a shape to impose but with a question, and the question is nearly a courtesy: *here is a weight I mean to set down, and here is roughly where; will you carry it, and if not here, then where?* And you make yourself able to hear the answer. That is the hard part, and the part no chain or rod or gauge will do for you. The ground knows the difference between a command and a request the way a dog knows it, or a child. I had spent twenty years telling ground what to be, and had got very good at it. I was about to spend the rest of a much longer life learning to ask.

I did not cast anything that night. You do not walk up to awake ground the day you have decided to be polite to it and expect to be believed. I spent three more days in the doing of a thing that looked, again, like nothing at all: unpicking the working I had drafted, thread by thread, and building in its place something I had no proper name for and still do not, a working that was almost entirely question and hardly at all instruction. Where the reshaping ward had four anchors driven down to bind the deep grain, my new thing had four anchors laid on it, resting, the way you rest a hand on a shoulder rather than take hold of an arm. Where the ward carried a shape to force, mine carried a shape to offer, and a great deal of room around the shape for the ground to answer back with a different one. It was, my old teachers would have said, a working with no spine in it, and they would have been right, and that was the point. I did not want a spine. Cael's father had brought a spine. I wanted a pair of open hands.

Cael came up the track on the second of those three days.

He came in the late afternoon with the sun out for the first time in a week and the whole valley steaming, and he did not hurry and he had sent nobody ahead. I had a fair idea what had brought him. There is a point at which a village stops finding a man amusing and starts to be uneasy about him, and I had been sitting on a hilltop for weeks doing nothing anybody could see.

He came up onto the flat and stood a while with his hands behind him, looking down at the valley and taking stock of it. Then he sat down on the spare crate. He put himself at my shoulder rather than across from me, without appearing to have chosen it.

"Fine evening at last," he said.

"It is."

"Beck's dropped a foot since morning."

I said I had seen it drop.

We sat a while. Below us somebody was burning something wet, and the smoke went up the far side of the valley in a long flat sheet and did not come apart.

"They're saying down the village you've not turned a sod," he said.

"I haven't."

"No." He nodded slowly at the bottoms. "There's a deal of opinion about it."

"I don't doubt it."

"Some of it's mine." He said it as another man would pass you the salt. Then he let it sit long enough for me to understand that he had no intention of saying what his opinion was, and said something else entirely.

"My father carried the water for that mortar up this track in a yoke. Two pails a trip. He reckoned thirty trips in a day and I've no cause to think he was boasting. I was small enough that the yoke was no use to me. I carried the one pail in both hands and spilled the most of it, and he never said a word to me about the spilling."

"How long did he give it?"

"All the summer." Cael turned his own hand over and looked at his thumbnail without appearing to see it. "He'd go up of a morning and scrape at the joints with that, to find had it gone off in the night. It hadn't. Not in a fortnight. Not in a month. He'd come down and eat his supper and say nothing at all about it, and go up in the morning and scrape at it again."

The smoke went on up the far side of the valley.

"My grandmother came up that summer," he said. "Sat herself on the low course where the wall had got to, and watched him work an afternoon at a time. Brought her knitting up for it. She never told him he shouldn't." He was quiet a moment. "She never told him he should, either. I've thought about that a fair bit since."

I asked the only question I had any business asking.

"Did he ever ask her what it was she heard up here?"

"No." Cael watched the light going along the far ridge. "Nor did I."

We sat a while longer, and neither of us said anything, and the valley went on steaming below us. Then he looked down at the paper weighted on the grass by my boot. It was a working taken half to pieces. There is not a man alive outside my own trade who could have made anything of it, and he did not try to.

"That's a deal of drawing for a fellow who isn't building," he said.

"It's mostly rubbing out."

He considered that for longer than it deserved. "Aye," he said. "That's the most of any job worth doing."

Then he got up and knocked the wet off the back of his coat and went down the track. He had not asked me one question about what I meant to do on his grandmother's hill.

I sat on after he had gone with the light going long and gold across the bottoms, and then I went back to taking my working to pieces, thread by thread, and got another two hours out of the day.

I cast it on a still evening at the hour the hill kept for speaking, because it seemed only manners to knock when someone is home.

I had waited three evenings to get one. The first two came up with weather behind them and the dusk pulse arrived early and hard on both, and it seemed a poor time to put a question to a house with other business on it. The third evening was still and clear and cool, the light going out of the west without any hurry, and not enough wind in the grass to lift a feather.

The anchors themselves are nothing to look at. Four iron pins, a hand long, square in section and drawn to a point, kept in a roll of oiled canvas I have carried since I was twenty-three. The heads are worn bright and the shanks are dark. There is no mark on them and no work in them beyond a smith's, and I could have had four more off any forge in the Commonwealth. What they are is a habit. A working of any size wants somewhere to stand while you put it together, and iron set in the ground will do for that, and after twenty years these four had been in enough ground to be more use to me than better ones.

I had driven them at the corners of every working I ever set. That evening I did not drive them. I laid them out in the grass instead, pointing inward, and left them lying there in the wet.

I will not make more of the doing of it than there was. There was no flash, no thunder, none of the furniture a story would give it. I knelt on the wet grass of the crown with my four anchors set out at the compass points and the last of them at the northeast. I put my palms flat on the ground, the way the boy had put his flat on the stone, and I asked. Not aloud. The aloud part is for stories. I put the question down into the ground the way you lower a bucket into a well you cannot see the bottom of, and I let go of wanting any particular answer, and I waited.

For a long moment there was nothing but the cold coming up through my knees and the small sounds of the evening settling and my own heart being louder than I would have liked. And then the hill answered.

I felt it as I had felt the pulse in the dark, below sound, up through the bone. This was not the hill keeping its own hours and taking no notice of me. This was the hill turning its attention on the question I had set down. There was a long slow settling in the deep layer beneath me, unhurried and enormous, and it was not the settling of ground being forced into a shape. It was the settling of ground choosing one. I felt the deep grain, that slow shifting stone that had thrown off two lifetimes of walls, gather itself under the crown and offer me a footing: a true and level and load-bearing place, wider than the one I had drafted and better sited, offset some little way toward the northeast of where I had thought to build, because the hill knew where it wanted to carry a weight, and it was not quite where I had meant to set one. The ground took the shape I had asked it for, not gratefully, for there is no gratitude in it, but completely, and as though it had been waiting a very long time to be asked a question it could say yes to.

Then it was done, and the crown was quiet. Under my knees was ground that would hold anything I cared to build on it. I was more tired than I had been in years, and it was a while before my hands would stop shaking. I put my face down against the cold wet grass. I was not sure, then or afterward, whether what was going out of me was the cost of the working, which is real and must always be paid, or something older that had been three years looking for a crack to go through.

I do not know how long I lay like that. Long enough that the wet had gone through my coat at the knees and the elbows and I had stopped noticing it. The cost of a working like that one does not go in a ledger. It is paid out of the same account a long day's honest labor is paid from, and I have known men in my trade spend themselves to nothing reaching too far and asking too much. I had reached about as far as I ever had. But it was not only the cost. The ground under my face was different now in a way I had no instrument to measure. It had been ground I stood on. It was ground that had answered me. A man who has spent three years making very sure that nothing in the world required anything of him, or offered him anything either, does not quite know what to do with being answered. I lay there and let it be true a while before I got up and was a sensible man about it again.

When I lifted my head the boy was there.

He was at the edge of the crown where the grass goes long, standing very still in the last of the light, and he was looking at me, and then he was looking at the ground between us with the particular attention I had marked in him from the first, the attention of a child at his real work. I could not have said how long he had been there. I had not heard him come, and Soren, when he did not want to be heard, was quieter than weather. He did not ask me what I had done. He never asked me anything, that boy, then or for a long time after. But he crossed the crown to where I knelt, and he crouched down beside me, and he laid his own small hand flat on the ground as he had laid it on the stone the day we walked the bounds, and he held it there a while with his head tipped, listening to something, and then he took it off and looked at me, and he said the only thing either of us said on that hill that whole evening.

"You've started," he said.

It was not a question. I have thought about those two words often since. At the time I only nodded. It was true, and there was nothing to add to it, and my voice was not to be relied upon just then. He nodded back, and got up, and went down the hill in the dark to his grandfather, and left me alone on the ground that had answered me.

I sat up there a long time after he had gone, until the cold drove me in. Below me the valley's windows went dark one by one, in no order a man could ever learn. Out in the north wood the old oak was a darker weight against the dark, and had been standing there longer than anyone could say. And under me, all the way down through the good black soil to the slow shifting stone, was a hill that had thrown off every wall any soul had ever tried to set on it, and had that evening, for the first time in two lifetimes that Cael could account for and God knows how many that he could not, been asked instead of told, and had said yes.

I did not build anything that night, or the next day. I lay down instead on ground that felt, for the first time, less like a thing I had bought and more like a thing that had agreed to keep me.

In the morning I made my tea and drank it standing, and then I went down the track into the village to find the one man in three valleys who might raise a wall on that hill and not laugh at me for asking. Behind me on the crown the four anchors lay where I had left them, out in the wet grass at the compass points, not one of them driven.
