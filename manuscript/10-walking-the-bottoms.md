# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Ten: Walking the Bottoms

I have got ahead of myself with the house, which is a thing a man does when he is proud of a piece of work. Go back to the front of that spring, to the thaw and the sums and the hill I could not make pay, and there is one more piece of that year still to tell.

I could not farm. That was the whole of the difficulty. I had good ground in the bottoms and no notion what to do with it, and a note coming due that the ground was supposed to answer.

Before I went to anybody, I went down and looked at it alone.

The thaw was three days old and the bottoms lay steaming. I got over the hedge at the beck end and walked out into the thistle, dead and grey and standing to the knee from the autumn before, and I stopped in the middle of eighty acres of it in good boots with the water running somewhere out of sight. A lark was up over the far end, going at it as though the year were already decided. I pulled a handful of the dead stuff to see what held it and came away with a fistful of root, white and thick as a finger, running off sideways further than I cared to follow. I dug at the mat with my heel. The ground under it was black and wetter than I had looked for, and it smelled of a shut room with the door finally opened.

I could read that ground about as well as I read a language I have never been able to speak. There was worth in it. I could read the fall of the ground and where the water would sit in a wet March, and I could have drawn the whole field to a quarter of an inch and had it right. What I could not have told you was when to put a share into it, nor what to sow when I had, nor how many hands and weeks and horses stood between a dead field and a bushel of anything at all. I climbed back over the hedge with my boots weighing twice what they had, and went home, and did no more farming.

So I did the one wise thing left to me. I went to Cael.

I found him in his yard mending a hurdle in the thin spring sun, and I put it to him plainly, that I had good bottom-land going to waste and no notion of how to work it, and asked did he know of any family that wanted ground and had the back and the sense to take it on.

He did not answer me, and he did not stop working either. The hurdle was up on trestles with its butt end propped against the wall, an old one that had been mended twice already, and he had a length of split hazel in his hand and was working it into the weave with his thumbs, patient as a man plaiting hair. A hen went along the foot of the wall behind him on some errand of her own. The sun had no weight in it yet, but it was the first sun of that year worth standing in, and he was standing in it.

"Beck's up," he said.

"It is."

"Took the plank out of the crossing at the wash-house. Third year running." He took up the mallet and knocked the rod down snug against the one below it, two taps, no more. "We'll put it back, and it'll go again."

I said the crossing might be built higher. He said it might. Neither of us was talking about the crossing. You do not walk up to a subject in that valley. You go past it once at a distance on some other business, and let the other man decide whether he cares to be walked toward.

"Study stood the winter, then."

"It stood it."

"Aye." He turned the hurdle end for end on the trestles and started up the other side. "Ground's letting go down there now. Fortnight and the low ground'll bear a horse."

He set down his mallet and looked at me a while with those sharp old eyes, and I understood I was being weighed again, the way I had been weighed the first evening over his cup, and that the weighing was not about me this time but about whoever he was deciding whether to say. A man does not hand his neighbors' futures to a stranger lightly, and I was still, by that valley's slow reckoning, most of a stranger.

He took his time over it. He finished the rod he had in his hand and knocked the ends off flush.

"How long would they have it?"

"As long as they work it."

"And if they've a bad year."

"Then they've had a bad year."

He looked past me down the length of his own yard, at nothing I could see, for long enough that I began to wonder whether the conversation had finished without my being told.

"There's the Wrays," he said at last. "Young Hal Wray and his wife. Good folk, hard folk, more children than land. They're on a place over the far side, working it on shares for a man who takes more than his share." He picked the mallet back up, which was Cael for I have said what I mean to say. "He'd not be sorry to hear of ground of his own to work. Nor would she."

I asked whether I should send word down to him.

"Go yourself." He looked along the next rod, found it wanting, and threw it on the pile. "And go on a working day. Not a Sunday, and not at his lunch. Let him be about his own business when you come at him, so he's a thing to look at while he thinks."

"You have given this some thought."

"I've had the whole winter," said Cael, "and no better use for it."

He bent back over the hurdle. I stood in his yard a moment longer than the business wanted. Then I said the name over to have it right, and he gave it me again without looking up.

"Wray. Hal Wray. Don't call him young Hal to his face; he's had that off this village since he was a lad and he's done with it."

The far side of the valley took an hour and a bit at a walk, down the low road and up the other side past the mill and out along a lane I had never before had cause to ride. The hedges along it had not been laid in years. A woman came the other way with a basket on her arm and wished me a good day, and then looked back at me twice before the bend. I was used to that by then.

That was how I came to be standing, three days later, in the sloped mud of a poor rented field on the far side of the valley, being looked over by Hal Wray, who was younger than I had pictured and built like a man made for exactly this and nothing else, wide and quiet and careful, with big raw hands and a way of not saying a thing until he was sure of it. His wife came out from the low dark house with a child on her hip and two more at her skirts and stood in the doorway not quite in the conversation and not quite out of it. That, I would learn, was Bess Wray's whole manner, and it hid a mind quicker than her husband's, though she would have been the last to say so.

The field ran up away from us at a slope no man would choose, and it had been worked to within an inch of what such ground will bear. The furrows went across the fall as straight as ruled lines, on ground that gives no help whatever in the ruling. At the head of it stood a stone heap the size of a cart, picked off by hand across more years than I liked to count. Whoever held that lease had got everything out of that field that was in it, and a little over, and had not been given one thing back.

I told him who I was, and he knew already. I told him what I had, and half the valley knew that. Then I told him plainly that I had eighty acres of good flat bottom-land lying under thistle for want of anybody to put a share to it, and that I had gone to Cael and asked him where a man might find a family with the back and the sense for such a field, and that Cael had given me his name.

At the name of Cael something crossed his face and was put away again. He did not look at his wife in the doorway. Of everything he did that morning, not looking at her was the loudest.

They heard me out, the both of them, without a word, and when I had done Hal looked at the ground between us for a while, and then he looked up. "And what would you be asking for it," he said, not quite a question. It was the only one that mattered to a man in his place.

I had thought about that on the ride over, and I had thought about it the way Penworth had taught me to, which is to say honestly and a little further ahead than feels comfortable.

"The main bottom-field," I said. "Eighty acres and a bit, along the beck. Not the whole of the bottoms; there's other ground I've not got the measure of yet. That field."

"I know that field." Hal's voice gave nothing away. "It's been under thistle since I was a lad."

"It has."

"There's a year's work in it before it gives you a bushel."

"There's a year's work in it before it gives *you* a bushel," I said, "which is rather my point. Nothing, the first year. Not a copper. You'll spend that year breaking it and cursing me, and it'll give you little enough back for the labour, so the first year is yours entire, rent and all, to get it into heart."

He looked at me for a moment as if checking me for a seam.

"And after?"

"Second year, a tenth of what it yields. Third year and after, a fifth."

"A fifth." That was the wife, from the doorway, and it was the first thing she had said. She was not asking. She was putting a figure down where it could be looked at.

"A fifth," I agreed. "Which is fair, and it is less than you're paying now to a man who gives you nothing for it but the privilege."

Nobody said anything for a while. Somewhere behind her a child was being told to leave something alone.

Hal shifted his weight. He had big raw hands and no notion what to do with them while he was thinking, and what he settled on was taking his cap off, turning it once by the brim, and putting it back on.

"There'd be seed wanted," he said.

"There would." I had not thought about seed once all that winter, and I did not pretend otherwise. "Tell me what it wants."

So he told me. He told me what a field of that size wanted in seed corn to start it, and what it would want in the second year when it had come into some sort of heart, and what he meant to do in a first year with no crop behind him to hold any back from. It was the first time in my life anybody had explained any part of farming to me. He did it standing in the mud of another man's field with his cap on, plainly and in order, and without once making me feel the size of my ignorance.

"I've a little put by," he said, when he had done. "Enough to start. I'm not asking you for seed."

"I know you're not."

"I'd have that said."

"It's said."

"There's a house on it, of a sort," I said. "The old place that came with the bottom-land, empty since well before it was mine. It wants a roof in two rooms and a great deal of honest cursing. But the walls are sound stone and the well is good, and that's yours too, to mend and live in as long as you work the land. If you work it well I'll not find a reason to want it back."

"Men find reasons," said Bess Wray.

"They do," I said. "I'll put the whole of it in writing, and you'll have your own copy of the paper, and if I ever go looking for a reason you may take that paper to Cael and let him read it out to me in front of the village."

Bess Wray looked up from the doorway at that, one quick look, and I saw her and her husband do the thing that married people do without a word passing, the whole of a conversation held in the tilt of a head and the set of a mouth, and then Hal Wray put out one of those big raw hands, and I took it. That was the second bargain I ever shook on in that valley. I have been prouder of very few I ever struck.

Bess Wray came out of her doorway with a cup of milk and gave it to me without a word and stood by while I drank it. It was cold, and it was theirs, and there was not a great deal of it in that house. I drank it off and gave the cup back and thanked her, and she said it was nothing.

I wrote the paper that week.

Kellard sold me the stock for it. I asked across his counter for a quire of decent writing paper and he went and fetched three, and set them along the wood in order of price, and told me what each of them was. Then he told me which of the three a damp cottage would eat in five years and which would not.

"That one," he said, of the middle. "The good one's wasted on a rent paper. The cheap one you'd only be writing twice."

"The middle one, then."

He opened the book, wrote the line, and turned the book round for me to read before he turned it back. "Two copies?"

"Two."

"Then you'll want twice the paper and half the hurry." He squared the quire on the counter with two fingers. "I'll say nothing about your business. But a paper a man can't read is worse to him than no paper, and folk down that end read slow."

"I'll write it plain."

"Write it big," said Kellard.

I wrote it out twice at the Study bench, in the draftsman's lettering my father drilled into me before I was ten, because it is the one hand I have that nobody has ever had to ask me to repeat. Six clauses and no flourishes. The field, by its bounds. The house, to mend and to live in as long as the land was worked. The first year entire. The tenth in the second. The fifth thereafter. And at the foot of it the sentence about Cael and the village, set down in the same plain letters as all the rest, so that it would not look like a kindness.

I took both copies over. Hal read his through twice, slowly, with one finger going under the lines, and never once looked up to see whether I was watching him do it. Then Bess read it over his shoulder, faster, and put her finger on a word near the bottom.

"Worked," she said. "Worked by who?"

I looked at where her finger was. It said *so long as the land is worked*. It did not say who was to be the judge of whether it had been, and it was my paper and my ground, so unless I wrote otherwise the answer was me. I struck the line out there on their table and wrote *so long as they are working it*, and put my initials against the change on both copies, and Hal watched me do it and said nothing whatever.

They came across a fortnight later, the Wrays, all their world on one borrowed cart, and I did little for them beyond the giving of the ground, for a man breaking in a field does not want a wizard underfoot.

I was there when the cart came, because I had wanted to be, and I had told myself it was to hand over the key. There was no key. There had been no door on that house since before anyone had troubled to remember.

It came up the low road at a walk on a grey morning with the mist still lying along the beck, and everything that family owned was on it, and there was room left over. A bed in pieces. A kist. A churn. Two crates of fowl with the slats mended in three colours of wood. A table upside down with its legs in the air and the smallest children sitting in among them like eggs in a nest. Hal walked at the horse's head the entire way and never once got up on the load, and I took that at the time for pride. I have been told since that it is what anybody does on a soft road with a full load. I am no longer certain the two are different things.

They stopped at the gate and looked at the house, all of them at once, and nobody said anything.

Two rooms of good stone with the sky in them. Grass on the top of the wall. A doorway with no door and a window with no glass, and the inside grown up with nettle, and one small ash sapling doing very well for itself in what had been the hearth.

Bess Wray went in first. She stood in the middle of that floor with her arms folded and turned the whole way round once, slowly, and then she said, "Table under the window. Fire wants sweeping before anything."

And that was the house settled.

I carried a chair in off the cart, being unable to stand there doing nothing, and one of the children took the other end of it, and then a moment later took it off me altogether, with the wordless efficiency of somebody who has moved a house before and holds views on who ought to be let help. So I went and looked at the well instead. It was the one thing on that ground I could be useful about. I drew a bucket. The rope ran true and the windlass wanted grease and nothing else. The water came up cold and clean and with no smell in it whatever, and I put my hand down into it and left it there a while, and found nothing in that well that wanted mending.

I said the well was sound. Hal said aye. That was the whole of my contribution to the founding of that household. I got up on Snow and went home.

But I went down now and again through that spring, drawn by something I did not examine too closely, and stood at the edge of the bottoms and watched a dead field come back to life under one family's hands, the thistle going down before Hal's share and the black earth turning up wet and waiting behind him, Bess and the older children following to pick the stones, and I found that watching it moved me in a way I had not predicted. I had spent my whole working life making things happen by will and by working, reaching into the shape of the world and asking it, politely or otherwise, to be other than it was. And here were two people making a far older and better kind of thing happen by nothing but their own bent backs and the turning of the year, asking the ground for nothing it had not always been ready to give, and it humbled me in a way the hill for all its wonders never quite had.

There was one afternoon of it in particular. I had come down the track meaning to go into the village and had somehow not gone, and I stood instead in the gap of the hedge at the beck end with my forearms on the top rail for the better part of an hour.

The share was away at the far end and coming back. Hal had the plough, and one of the older children was at the horse's head, and I never did ask whose horse it was. The thistle went down in front of the pair of them in a long grey wave and did not get up again, and behind it the ground came over black and shining and lay steaming in the cold. There were rooks on it before he was thirty yards past, walking up and down the new furrow with their hands behind their backs like inspectors.

Bess and the two biggest children worked across the turned part with a barrow, picking stones. They did not do it quickly. They did it the way you do a thing whose size you already know: bend, and lift, and carry, and go back. The heap at the hedge end stood to my knee that afternoon. By the autumn I could not see over it.

The smallest of them caught sight of me at the rail and stopped dead and stared, in the frank and appalling manner of a child who has been told something about a person and is checking it against the person. I lifted my hand to her. She did not lift hers. She went and stood against her mother's skirt and looked at me from there, and Bess put a hand on the top of her head without troubling to look down, and went on picking stones.

I walked the bottoms with Hal Wray a fortnight after they came across.

It was his idea, not mine. He sent word up by Soren that if I had a morning he would be obliged to show me something, which is how a careful man says he has found a problem and does not wish to be blamed for it.

The boy brought it up with the bread. He set the loaf on the bench first, as he always did, so that whatever he had come to say could be said with his hands empty, and then he delivered it standing in the middle of the workroom floor with his cap in his fist, the way you deliver a thing that is not yours.

"Hal Wray says, if you've a morning, he'd be obliged to show you something."

"Was there more of it?"

"No." He considered, and corrected himself, because he was exact. "He said it twice. The same both times."

"Why twice?"

"So I'd have it right."

I said I would go down in the morning. Soren nodded and put his cap on, and then stood there a moment longer than he generally stood anywhere.

"He's got the one end of that roof thatched and the other end open," he said. "You can see sky through the back of it from the lane."

"You went and looked, then."

"I went past," said Soren, in the tone of a man defending a distinction he considered important, and took himself off down the hill.

I went down on a bright cold morning at the front of April with my coat buttoned and no notion whatever that I was about to be taught my own property.

We started at the beck end and walked up.

"That's yours," Hal said, of the first stretch, and it was: eighty-odd acres of good flat ground with a hedge one side and water the other, the thistle just beginning to go down before his share at the near end and standing to the knee over all the rest.

He read it to me as we went, without meaning to teach me anything by it. The near corner lay wet and would want a ditch opened before it wanted anything else; there had been one once, and you could still see where by the line the rushes kept. The hedge along the west side was a good hedge that had not been laid in twenty years, and laying it was three weeks with a hook for one man, and worth every hour of it. And under the thistle, if you looked along the ground rather than at it, you could still make out the old ridges from whenever the field had last been in corn, standing up under the mat like the bones of a hand under a cloth.

"How long since it was cropped?"

"Before my time." He put his boot on one of the ridges and rocked his weight on it. "Ground keeps a ridge longer than a man keeps a grudge."

He walked me up the middle of it, and about a third of the way along he stopped and stood and said nothing, and let me work it out, which took me an embarrassing length of time.

There was a change in the ground. Not a fence, not a ditch, nothing a man would notice at speed. A line where the thistle gave way to older, ranker growth, ran along a few dozen paces, and gave back again.

"What's this?"

"That's Hask's."

"Hask's what?"

"Tobin Hask's strip." He said it patiently. "Six acre, near enough. Beck to the hedge. Runs through the middle of your field."

I said something I will not set down. Hal looked off at the hedge and gave no sign of having heard it.

We walked its length, and I looked at it, and I understood what I was looking at, which was a stick dropped across a doorway. To plough my eighty acres a man would have to break his run at that line twice on every single pass, turn on the awkward, and go round. Hal had known that since the day he took the ground. He had not mentioned it, because it was not his business to tell a landlord his own land, and because he had assumed, reasonably, that I knew.

"Who is Tobin Hask?"

Hal gave me a look I have had from a good many people in that valley over the years, and which I have come to think of as the look a man gives you when he is deciding whether you are a fool or merely new.

"The old fellow on the lane," he said. "With the dog. Been there always."

Then he took me round the rest of it, and the rest of it was worse.

We came to a corner of the same field with a cottage in it and a garden that stopped me where I stood, because it was three times the garden anyone else in that valley kept and it was kept by somebody who plainly had nothing else. That was not mine either.

We were the width of the field off it and I did not go nearer. Beds laid out square with the paths between them swept down to the bare earth. Bean-rows already strung on hazel. A frame of old window-glass propped over something at the warm end, and a currant bush cut back hard and cut back right, by a hand that meant to have it another twenty years, and along the south wall a line of pea-sticks going in early and going in straight. There was washing out on a line, and it was the only bright thing in that half of the valley.

I asked whose it was. Hal said it had always been there, and looked at the hedge, and I let it go.

We went up the east side and along the edge of the wood, and Hal put his hand on a gate, and the gate did not open, because it had been wired shut so long that the wire had grown a coat of rust and the hawthorn had come up through it.

It was not a lock and it was not a fence. It was three turns of plain fencing wire round the gate and the post, put on by a man in a temper in about a minute, and left, and gone to rust, and grown into. I have spent my working life being paid to look at a thing and say what it would take to shift it, and I could have had that gate open in ten minutes with a pair of pincers off Tomas's wall. I put my hands in my pockets and left it alone.

"How long?"

"Ten year? Twelve." He shrugged. "There was a falling-out. I couldn't tell you what over. My father might have known."

And beyond that gate, which nobody living could remember the reason for, lay parcel after parcel of good plough-land, sixty acres of it or better, gone entirely back to thistle and scrub and young birch, because the only lane to them ran through the ground of a man who had wired his gate over a quarrel nobody could now recall, and everyone had eventually stopped trying, and the ground had simply been let go.

I got up on the bar to see over, and the hawthorn made that a good deal less dignified than I would have liked. Birch to the height of a man and standing thicker than a man could walk through. A wall down in three places with the stone of it still lying where it fell, dressed stone, somebody's whole autumn. And under the scrub a track that was still a track if you knew to look for the two green lines of it, running away toward ground I owned and had never in my life set foot on.

Sixty acres. That is not a large number on a page.

I stood at that gate a long while.

Because I had heard all of this before, in a manner of speaking, and had not understood one word of it. Cael had told me, on the crown, before I ever put my name to anything: *the good plough-land down in the bottoms is four men's idle acres he's stitched into the one holding that'll bring you a rent, once you've found the right families to put on it.* I had heard "one holding." I had heard "a rent." I had been thinking about a hill.

Penworth had stitched four dead men's ground into one holding, and he had done it exactly as a man of business does such things, which is to say with a pen, in a room, four days' road away. On the paper it was one property. Every line on that plan was true.

And on the ground it was four men's ground still, with other people's lives grown up through the middle of it like the hawthorn through that wire, and not one thing had actually been joined to anything else.

"It's a rare mess," Hal said, from behind me. He was uncomfortable saying it and said it anyway. "Always has been. Nobody's had the whole of it in one hand since before I was born, and every man that's had a piece has fenced what he could reach and let the rest go." A pause. "You've the whole of it in one hand now."

Which was the sentence.

I asked him what he wanted done about the strip, and he said nothing at all; he would plough round it as the last man had and the man before that, and it would cost him something in the working, and he said that with no complaint whatever, as a fact about the world he had accepted long before he came up my track. That was the part that stayed with me longest. He had not walked me over his own ground to ask me to fix it. He had walked me over it so that when the field came in short, I would know why, and could not lay it at his door.

I said I would look into it. I remember saying it. It was the sort of thing I said in those days when I meant that a thing had gone onto a list.

There was an actual list. It lived on the workroom bench under the small brass scale, a single sheet folded once, and I copied it out fresh every few weeks because I liked the look of a clean one, and I ruled it, being my father's son and unable to help myself.

I unfolded it that evening and read it through before I wrote anything on it. Four collars from Tomas, and the pattern to settle with him. Crystal to order, and the carriage of it to arrange with Kellard before the roads went soft. Strap-iron. Glass, and where in the world to get glass on that side of the country. The note had a line to itself with nothing written after it, because there was nothing I wanted to look at yet. Timber, for a thing I had been drawing in my head for a month and had not yet put on paper.

I dipped the pen and wrote at the foot of it, in the same plain letters I had used on the Wrays' paper: *The bottoms. The strip. The gate.*

Then I put the brass scale back on the sheet and went to my supper.

And onto the list it went, under the note and the summer and the lamp trade and the glass, and there it sat for half a year while I did not touch it. I have wondered since why, and I have only the one honest answer, and it does me no credit at all. Hal had said *people*, and I had heard *paperwork*. Paperwork can wait. People, as it turned out, had been waiting eleven years already.
