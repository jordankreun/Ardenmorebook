# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Interlude III

The hill keeps its count.

Not of things that arrive and are gone again. It has never had a count for those. Cold laid over warm and warm laid over cold, wet coming down and going out and coming down again, the grain running under all of it and going where the grain goes. The two risings at either end of the dark, standing where they have always stood. That is the count. The count is only the laying-down, and nothing is ever taken back out of it.

The going-hard has let go. It went out of the top of the good black soil first and then out of the layer beneath that, unhurried, the loosening coming up from below to meet the loosening coming down from above, and where the two met the soil opened and took water. The wet is down in it now as far as the roots go and further. It stands in the cracks it stiffened in and does not stiffen, and runs instead, and finds the old ways down, and finds one way down that was not there before. The roots come out after it and hold and take what they take. The hill has been dry and wet and dry again past any reckoning of the quick weights. It has never done it this fast before.

There is carried stone lying down the long slope, one seam of it, from the crown to the low ground. None of it holds anything up. It is laid flat and laid close, hard weight over soft, following the fall of the land and bending where the land bends. Under the seam the soil no longer opens and shuts with the wet; it stays as it was pressed. Cold goes into it later than into the ground on either side and comes out of it later, and the grain beneath it runs on unbroken, as it runs under any outcrop.

Weights cross on it and do not sink. That is new. Everything that went up or down this slope before pressed itself into the soft ground, and the ground took its own long time closing over the press of it, and closed it. Now the quick weights come up dry-footed and go down dry-footed and leave nothing behind them but a little warmth where they stopped, and laid stone lets that warmth go faster than grass ever did.

The hoofed weight comes up the seam, and comes up it oftener than it used to. Hard points set close under one long body, striking laid stone instead of soil, and the striking goes down into the seam and sideways along it and is spent before it is well begun. Behind it sometimes a dragging weight with load on it, pressing everywhere along the line at once and nowhere in particular. None of it leans.

Below the crown, on the side the light lies longest on, there is a made place that keeps the light. Quick weights came and went about it all through the dark end of the round. They brought hard bright weight up with them, colder at night than anything else on the hill and slow to warm at all, and stood it up in lines, and went away, and came again. Then something was put over the lines that is neither stone nor water nor wood, and after that the place stopped going cold.

It takes the light in and holds it past the going of it. Everything else on the crown lets go what it has held when the light goes. That one place does not. It lets its warmth down into the soil beneath it instead, low and slow and all night long, and the soil there did not go hard when the rest of the hill went hard, and there are roots reaching down in it out of their turn. The hill has no count for out of their turn. It has only the warmth, arriving where warmth was not, and staying.

Two small weights walk it now, along with the great one. The hill knows them apart the way it knows rain from meltwater. One hears. That one has heard since it was hardly weight at all, and hearing is the whole of what it does: it takes the sound in and holds it, still, entire, the way the old one in the wood holds it. The other does not hear. The other stands in the sound the way a root stands in water and takes it down through itself into the ground again, and the hill lets it go through and gets it back changed, hard bright weight in it, and cut wood, and a straightness.

They come at spacings the hill does not reckon. The one that hears comes oftenest, up the seam and down it again inside the one light, and its weight is the lesser, and is a little more weight each round than it was the round before. It stands still on the crown more than it walks on it. The other comes seldom and comes heavy, and heavier again when it comes carrying, and sets the whole of it down at once.

The great weight goes down to the cold room now on more nights than before. The hill feels a small blunt tooth touched to its floor-stone, and the note asked, and answers, and feels the answer taken up twice over: once by the hand it has learned line by line since the first asking, once through the standing weight of the other. It gives the same answer to both. It has only the one.

In the floor of the cold room a stranger stone lies flush, cold and clear and doing nothing, and takes each rising and keeps it. The one lying there now has lain there only since the letting-go. The one before it lay through the whole of the going-hard, and was taken up out of the floor and carried off and has not come back, and it had not stopped taking when it went. What was set down in its place began empty and is not empty now, and the staying has run on in this one as it ran on in that one. There is nothing in the hill that weighs such a thing. There is only the thing itself, lying in the floor, cold, not filling.

At the fall of the light, and again in the black before the birds, the hill rises and says it. Toward the dry corner of the cut room, where the small closed weight lies in the dark that does not trouble it. The saying is better now. The hill has the great weight's shape to build with, all those learned lines. It lays them together and turns them and lays them again, practicing the shape of a word it has no mouth for. Each night the shape is nearer. The small closed weight lies as it has lain, through bones and roots and the slow settling of stone.

The rising goes out everywhere at once. It always has. Up through the good black soil and out along the grain and off the edges of the hill into the low ground, into the meadow, into the roots of the thorn standing along the seam. None of that takes it. None of that keeps any of it. Ground out there is only ground, and lets the rising through and lets it go, as all this ground did before there was anything in it that shut.

Far out past the low ground, in the wood, the old one keeps its long attention pointed this way and does not take it off. It was pointed this way before there was carried stone anywhere on the slope. It does not rise. It has never once answered. It only holds, and the hill goes on rising toward the dry corner, and the holding and the rising have never come near one another.

The hill is not troubled. The hill has never once been troubled. It settles its long weight over the cut room and the dry corner. Below it in the valley the two small weights sleep. The hill keeps its count.
