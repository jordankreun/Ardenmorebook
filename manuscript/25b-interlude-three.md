# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Interlude III

The hill keeps its count.

Not of light-turnings. It has never had those. Cold laid over warm and warm laid over cold, wet coming down and going out and coming down again, the grain running under all of it and going where the grain goes. The two risings at either end of the dark, standing where they have always stood. That is the count, and the count is only the laying-down, and nothing is ever taken back out of it.

The going-hard has let go. It went out of the top of the good black soil first and then out of the layer beneath that, unhurried, the loosening coming up from below to meet the loosening coming down from above, and where the two met the soil opened and took water. The wet is down in it now as far as the roots go and further. It stands in the cracks it stiffened in and does not stiffen, and runs instead, and finds the old ways down, and finds a way or so that was not there before. The roots come out after it and hold and take what they take. The hill has been dry and wet and dry again past any reckoning of the quick weights. It has never once done it faster.

There is carried stone lying down the long slope, one seam of it, from the crown to the low ground. None of it holds anything up. It is laid flat and laid close, hard weight over soft, following the fall of the land and bending where the land bends. The hill had those bends long before anything came and found them. Under the seam the soil no longer opens and shuts with the wet; it stays as it was pressed. Cold goes into it later than into the ground on either side and comes out of it later, and the grain beneath it runs on unbroken, as it runs under any outcrop.

Weights cross on it and do not sink. That is the new thing. Everything that went up or down this slope before wrote itself into the soft ground, and the ground took its own long time closing the writing over, and closed it. Now the quick weights come up dry-footed and go down dry-footed and leave nothing behind them but a little warmth where they stopped, and laid stone lets that warmth go faster than grass ever did.

The hoofed weight comes up the seam, and comes up it oftener than it used to. Hard points set close under one long body, striking laid stone instead of soil, and the striking goes down into the seam and sideways along it and is spent before it is well begun. Behind it sometimes a dragging weight with load on it, pressing everywhere along the line at once and nowhere in particular. None of it leans.

Below the crown, on the side the light lies longest on, there is a made place that keeps the light. Quick weights came and went about it all through the dark end of the round, and brought hard bright weight up with them, colder at night than anything else on the hill and slow to warm at all, and stood it up in lines, and went away, and came again. Then something was put over the lines that is neither stone nor water nor wood, and after that the place stopped going cold.

It takes the light in and holds it past the going of it. Everything else on the crown lets go what it has held when the light goes. That one place does not. It lets its warmth down into the soil beneath it instead, low and slow and all night long, and the soil there did not go hard when the rest of the hill went hard, and there are roots reaching down in it out of their turn. The hill has no count for out of their turn. It has only the warmth, arriving where warmth was not, and staying.

Two small weights walk it now, along with the great one. The hill knows them apart the way it knows rain from meltwater. One hears. That one has always heard, since he was light enough for a bird to have carried, and hearing is all he does: he takes the sound in and holds it, still, entire, the way the old one in the wood holds it. The other does not hear. The other stands in the sound the way a root stands in water, and takes it downward through himself into the ground again, and does not know he is doing it, and the hill lets the sound go through him and receives it back changed, a taste of iron and cut timber in it, a straightness.

They come at spacings the hill does not reckon. The one that hears comes oftenest, up the seam and down it again inside the one light, and its weight is the lesser and is always the same weight, and has been the same a long time, where quick weights that go on being quick grow heavier every round. It stands still on the crown more than it walks on it. The other comes seldom and comes heavy, and its weight has not stopped changing since it first came up the slope, and it sets that weight down the way a thing does that has carried loads while its own frame was still settling.

The great weight goes down to the cold room now on more nights than before. The hill feels the small blunt tooth of steel touched to its floor-stone, and the note asked, and answers, and feels its answer listened to twice over, once by the trained hand it has learned line by line these two years, once through the root-boy's boots. It gives the same answer to both. It has only ever had the one thing to say.

In the floor of the cold room a stranger stone lies flush, cold and clear and doing nothing, and takes each rising and keeps it. There has been one lying there since before the going-hard, and one lying there before that. The one before that was taken up out of the floor and carried off and has not come back. It was full when it went and it had not stopped taking. What was set down in its place began empty and is not empty now, and the staying has run on in this one as it ran on in that one, dark laid over dark and neither of them going faint. There is nothing in the hill that weighs such a thing. There is only the fact of it lying in the floor, cold, not filling.

At the fall of the light, and again in the black before the birds, the hill rises and says it. Toward the dry corner of the cut room, where the small closed weight sits in the dark that does not trouble it. The saying is better now. The hill has the great weight's shape to build with, all those learned lines, and it lays them together and turns them and lays them again, patient as frost, practicing the shape of a word it has no mouth for, and each night the shape is nearer, and each night the small closed weight keeps its own counsel, as it has kept it through bones and roots and the slow settling of stone.

The rising goes out everywhere at once. It always has. Up through the good black soil and out along the grain and off the edges of the hill into the low ground, into the meadow, into the roots of the thorn standing along the seam. None of that takes it. None of that keeps any of it. Ground out there is only ground, and lets the rising through and lets it go, as all this ground did before there was anything in it that shut.

Far out past the low ground, in the wood, the old one keeps its long attention pointed this way and does not take it off. It was pointed this way before there was carried stone anywhere on the slope. It does not rise. It has never once answered. It only holds, and the hill goes on rising toward the dry corner, and the holding and the rising have never come near one another.

The hill is not troubled. The hill has never once been troubled. It settles its long weight over the cut room and the dry corner and the two small weights asleep in the valley below, and keeps its count.
