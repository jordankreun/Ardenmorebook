# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Five: Good

The whole village knew I meant to build before I had told a soul but the boy, which is the way of villages, and the whole village knew it was a foolishness, which was the way of this one in particular. So I did not go down expecting to be believed. I went down to hire Jorin, and I went down knowing that the sensible half of him would refuse me and the other half was the reason I wanted him.

I found him at his yard at the lower end of the village, a tidy square of hard ground stacked with dressed stone and seasoned timber, everything in it squared and sorted and put where a man could lay his hand on it in the dark, which told me as much about him as an hour of talk would have. He was setting a lintel over a doorway for somebody's byre with the boy I had marked at Lira's, the dark one built long, and he did not stop when I came up, and I had the sense to wait until the stone was seated and pinned before I said anything. A man who interrupts another man's lintel deserves what he gets.

"You'll be the wizard," Jorin said, when it was done, in the tone Lira had used, which I was coming to understand was not rudeness but the plain naming of a trade.

"I mean to raise a room on the hill," I said. "Stone. Small to start. A study, and quarters after, if the first one stands." I did not dress it up. You do not dress things up for a man like that; he will only have to undress them again, and resent the labor.

He looked at me a while out of a face that gave nothing away, and behind him the boy had stopped his work and was watching me too, openly, the way he had at Lira's, filing me somewhere. The yard was quiet. Somewhere down the lane a dog was arguing with a cart. I have been in a good many negotiations in my time, and the ones that matter are mostly silence, and the man who cannot bear the silence is the man who loses.

"That hill's thrown off every wall in two lifetimes," Jorin said at last. It was not a refusal. It was a fact set on the table, the way Cael had set his father's story down, to see what I would do with it.

"It has," I said. "It threw off Cael's father, and it will throw off me too, if I build the way his father built. I don't mean to."

"Everyone means not to."

"I've readied the ground," I said.

That was the sentence the whole errand turned on, and I felt him weigh it. A lesser builder would have laughed, or argued, or asked me what in God's name I meant by readied, which is a question with no honest short answer. Jorin did none of those things. He looked at me for a long moment with the flat assessing attention I had seen him turn on me across Lira's room, the way you look at a stone you are deciding whether to set load on. Then he wiped his hands on his apron and said, "I'll come look at the ground." And that was the whole of it. He would not build on my word, and he would not refuse on the village's; he would come and put his own hands on the thing and let it tell him. It is the only honest way to hire a man or be hired, and I have kept to it since.

He came up the next morning with the boy and a bar and a mattock and a spirit level and a length of knotted cord, and he did to my hill exactly what he would have done to any ground a man asked him to build on, which is to say he distrusted it thoroughly and at length. He drove the bar down at the crown and at the corners and felt what came back up the iron. He dug a test hole shoulder-deep and got down into it and looked at the sides of it and crumbled the deep soil between his fingers and, once, held a handful of it to his face and smelled it, which I have never seen another builder do and have done myself ever since. He set his level on the footing the hill had given me and moved it and set it again in a dozen places, and it read true in every one, true to a finer tolerance than a man with a level has any right to expect from open ground, and I watched him find that out and watched him decline to be impressed by it, which I respected. He laid his knotted cord along the ground and paced it and knelt and pressed the heel of his hand against the packed base of the test hole and held it there a long moment the way his son held his hand against things, and I understood, watching the two of them, exactly where the boy had got it.

When he climbed out of the hole he did not say the ground was sound, or ask me how I had done it, or any of the things a lesser man would have said to fill the space. He knocked the dirt off his knees and looked at the crown a while, and then he looked at me, and he said, "Whose corner's that." He was pointing at the northeast, where the footing sat a little wide of where a man would have set it by eye, offset the way the hill had offset it when it answered me.

"The hill's," I said, because it was the truth and because I did not think he would take a lie, and because a man who smells soil to know it will know a lie about ground when he hears one.

Jorin looked at the northeast corner a while longer. Then he said, "Build there, then," which was as near as he ever came, in the whole of the work, to saying that he believed me, and it was enough, and we shook on it, and I had my builder.

The stone came from Edran's quarry, a day's round trip by cart over the low road, and it came up the last of the track the way my life had come up it, by hand, because the giving-up road gives up in earnest before the foot of the hill and no loaded wagon will make the climb. That was a labor and no mistake, and I paid for the hands to do it, and I put my own back into it besides, which surprised the hands and did me more good than a month of lying still had done. There is a particular honesty in carrying dressed stone up a hill you mean to answer for. It gets into your shoulders and it stays there. It is the nearest thing I know to prayer that a man can do with his body and no words. The timber came from Maret's mill, good seasoned oak, and I paid Maret his price without haggling and warded the framing timber against rot and the small cold rots that live in green wood, a small working and an honest one, and the first bit of my trade the village ever saw me do for money. Maret watched me do it to a stack of his own joists with an expression I could not read and then named a lower price for the next lot, which is a miller's way of saying he was impressed, and I did not insult him by refusing it.

The ironwork I had of Tomas, at the smithy at the top of the street, and I met him properly over that commission. He was a big still man with the quietest forge I have ever stood in, and he made me hinges and brackets and a set of long floor-cramps to the drawings I brought him, and he made them better than the drawings, which is the mark of a real smith, and he said perhaps forty words the whole of the business. I was telling him what the ironwork was for, the door and the frame of a study I was raising, and I said, without thinking anything of it, that it was to go up on the hill. And the hammer, which had been going the whole while we talked, the way a smith will talk over his own steady work, paused. Half a beat, no more. Then it came down again exactly as before, and Tomas said nothing, and neither did I. A man's hammer is his own affair.

We raised the Study over the back half of that summer, and I will not give you the whole of it stone by stone, though I could, for I remember every course of it the way you remember the face of a first child. Jorin ran the work and I labored on it and learned from him, and his crew came up the track each morning and the walls went up, dressed stone laid true on the footing the hill had given me, and the mortar, the mortar that had stayed soft on this ground for a month for Cael's father and let his walls go, took its cure in three days like mortar anywhere honest, because the ground was not fighting it now. The village noticed that. Word of it went down the hill faster than we did of an evening, and for a while I would look up from the work and find one or two of them at the edge of the crown where the grass goes long, come up on some invented errand to see the fool's wall before it fell, and stay to watch it not fall, and go down quieter than they came.

Cael came up once, in the middle of it, on a fair afternoon when the walls were about half their height. He did not make an occasion of it. He came up the track on his stick by the slow treaty he had made with his knees, and he stood at the edge of the crown a while and looked at the stone going up on his grandmother's hill, and he said nothing for a long time, and I had the sense to go on with my work and let him have the looking. When he did speak it was not to me. "Well," he said, to the wall, or to the hill, or to someone who was not there to hear it, and there was forty years folded up in the one word. Then he turned and went back down the track without another, and I did not follow him or call after him, because some things a man does are not done for an audience, and that was one.

The boy Ren I came to know over that summer's stone, and his name deserves saying properly, because it was on the Study that I first used it, and first had cause to. Jorin worked him hard and said little to him, and the boy took the hard work and the little saying the way good ground takes rain, and between them there was a whole language of glances and grunts and the passing of tools that I came, slowly, to be let a little way into. He was perhaps sixteen that summer, built long where his father was built square, and he had his father's hands and something his father did not have, which I did not have a name for yet.

It was a plain enough thing on the face of it. We were setting the northeast corner, the hill's corner, and the course had to come true off a footing that ran a hair proud of level there, the hill's own doing, and it is a finicking business, coaxing a wall up plumb off a base that is a hair out, and it usually wants a deal of measuring and shimming and swearing. Ren did not measure it. He took up the corner stone and turned it once in his hands, feeling the faces of it the way I had seen his father smell the soil, and he set it down, and it was true. Not near true. True, to the string and to the level both, as though the stone had told him where it wanted to sit and he had merely not argued. Jorin saw it. I saw Jorin see it. Neither of them said a word, and the work went on, and I laid the next stone myself off the true one the boy had set, and I did not say anything either, because I had already learned that saying a thing to that family was the surest way to make them careful of it, and because I did not yet understand what I had seen, and a man should be slow to name a thing he does not understand, especially a gift, and most especially a gift in someone else's son.

But I marked it. I have marked a great many things over the years, and forgotten most of them, and that one I never have.

The wall that had to stand was the north wall, the tall one, the one that would have to hold a roof and a season of weather and the memory of every wall that hill had ever thrown down. We finished the height of it on a still grey evening with rain coming, and Jorin looked at it a while in the failing light, plumb and true and going nowhere, and then he did a thing I have thought about since. He did not tell me it would hold. He gathered his crew and his tools and he went down the hill and left it standing there alone, unpropped, in the dark and the coming rain, on the one night that mattered, the way you leave a child to sleep the first night in its own room to find out whether it can. It was the test his own grandfather's generation had failed and Cael's father had failed, the test the whole valley knew by heart. A wall on that hill stood up fine in the daylight with men around it. The question was always the night.

I did not sleep much. I will admit that. I lay in the tent with the chest a black weight at my back and the rain coming on in earnest and I listened, past the rain, for the sound I had been told about my whole first month in that valley, the sound of good stone letting go with no wind to blame. It did not come. The hill kept its own two hours in the dark, the dusk-fail and the cold before the birds, and between them it lay quiet under the rain, and my wall lay quiet on it, and when the grey light came up at last I got out into the wet with my heart going like a boy's and walked round the north wall three times and could not find that it had moved so much as the width of a hair.

Jorin came up not long after. He had not been able to sleep either, I think, though he would have died before he said so. He came up through the wet grass and he stood in front of the north wall a long time with the rain running off the brim of his hat, and he put his hand flat against the stone of it the way his son put his hands against things, and he held it there, feeling for something, some tremor or settling or the small betrayal of a footing about to give, and there was none, because the hill was carrying the wall the way it had said it would carry it, because I had asked and it had answered and neither of us had gone back on the bargain. And Jorin took his hand off the stone, and he looked at the wall, and he looked at me, and out of the whole broad country of things a man might have said standing there in the rain in front of the impossible, he chose the one word he always chose when a thing was right and there was nothing to be done to improve it.

"Good," he said.

It was one word, from a hard old builder in the rain. But I had been three years in a country where no one required anything of me and nothing I did came to much, and that evening a man whose good opinion could not be bought or hurried had put his hand on my work and called it good. I stood a while after he had gone. The wall did not fall down then either.

We roofed it before the first hard weather, oak rafters off Maret's mill and good slate that Edran's quarry turned out as a sideline, and Tomas's hinges went on the door and swung silent the first time and every time after, for he was that kind of smith. And on a cold bright afternoon at the very end of that summer, with the light going long and gold the way it had gone the day I shook Cael's hand, I moved out of the tent I had kept on the crown since spring and into the first true room of my life's second half. It was not much to look at. Four stone walls and a slate roof and a floor of good boards over the footing the hill had given me, a hearth, a window on the south to take the light, a door that swung silent on Tomas's hinges. I had built grander things and lost them. I have never in my life been prouder of anything.

I brought my books up and stacked them along the walls in the beginning of an order. I set my trade instruments out on a proper bench at last, out of the damp. And when it was near dark and the room was mine, I brought in the chest, the last thing, the way it had been the last thing off the wagons, and I set it against the wall in the corner, the northeast corner, the hill's corner, because it was the driest and because a thing you handle with more care than yourself wants the soundest wall in the house at its back, and I did not think anything more of the choosing of the corner than that. I set it down, and I did not open it, and I laid my hand flat on the top of it a moment the way Cael had laid his hand on the deeds, and then I took my hand away, and that was the whole of my ceremony.

The boy Soren had brought my bread up that morning as he did every morning, and he came back at dusk on some errand of his own that we both knew was no errand, and he stood in the doorway of the finished room and looked at it a while, the walls and the hearth and the ordered books, and he did not say it was fine, or ask to come in, or any of the things a child says. He looked at the northeast corner where the chest stood, only for a moment, and then he looked away from it as though he had been somewhere he ought not, and he said, "The hill likes the room." And he went off down the track in the dark before I could answer, as sure of the dark track as if he had laid it himself, the way he always went.

I stood a while in the door of my own first room with the dusk coming up the valley like slow water, and I thought about a hill that had thrown off every wall in two lifetimes and had held mine because I asked, and about a boy who heard it, and an old man who had smelled the soil and called the work good, and a chest in the corner I would not open, and I found, to my very great surprise, that I was glad to be alive, and that I meant to see what the morning would bring.
