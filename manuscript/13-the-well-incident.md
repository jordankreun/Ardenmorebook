# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Thirteen: The Well Incident

It happened on an ordinary market morning, which is the only kind of morning such things ever happen on. The extraordinary ones are all braced for. It is the day you have gone down to the village for nothing more urgent than bread and a spool of waxed thread that the ground chooses to remind you the world is not as settled as you had let yourself believe.

I had come down for exactly that, bread and thread, and had got the bread, a warm loaf out of the communal oven with the crust still ticking as it cooled, and was crossing the square toward the inn with it under my arm when I saw the boy at the well.

The well stood in the middle of the square, an old one, older than anyone living, a ring of mortared fieldstone waist-high with a good oak windlass and a rope gone soft and grey with a hundred years of hands. Soren was drawing water at it, which was a thing he did most mornings, a bucket for his grandfather's house and a bucket for the inn, a chore he had been trusted with since he was small enough to need both hands on the crank. There were a dozen people about, the ordinary traffic of a market morning, a woman haggling eggs, two men leaning on the oven wall, Kellard counting something into a cart. Nobody was watching the boy. There is no reason on earth to watch a boy draw water. It is the most nothing thing in the world.

I was watching him, though, because I had fallen into the habit of watching him, the way you watch a thing you have begun to be afraid for without letting yourself say why.

He had the bucket most of the way up, cranking easy, and I saw him falter, a small catch in the smooth turning of the crank, the way a man's step catches when a thought takes him unawares. And then the rope went slack.

It should not have gone slack. There was a full bucket on the end of it, ten pounds of water and wood, and the crank was three turns from the top, and the rope between the boy's hands and the windlass simply lost its tension, went loose and easy as a washing-line, because the weight was no longer pulling down. The bucket was rising on its own. I saw it come up over the lip of the well, clear of the stone, and for the length of one heartbeat it hung there in the air a hand's breadth above the rim with the water standing up inside it in a way water does not stand, humped and trembling and reaching, as if the whole of it had turned its face up toward the boy and was climbing to be nearer him.

It was the smallest thing. It lasted no longer than it takes to read this sentence. But a market square is a body, and a body feels a wrong note in it before the mind can name the note, and I felt the whole square go still around me in that heartbeat, the haggling woman's voice stopping mid-word, the two men at the oven going quiet, a dozen animal parts of a dozen people all turning at once toward the wrongness at the well without yet knowing what they were turning toward.

I did the only thing there was to do. I made it mine.

"There it is," I said, loudly, the way a man says a thing he has been waiting to say, and I strode to the well as if I had been walking to it all along, and I put my hand flat over the mouth of the bucket and pressed it down onto the stone with a good solid knock of wood on rock. "I told Cael that windlass was singing. Feel that." I gripped the old oak of the frame and gave it a shake, and I looked round at the nearest of them with the particular cheerful exasperation of a craftsman who has found a fault nobody believed in. "The whole thing's gone resonant. Dry rot in the post, most like, or the iron's working loose in the stone, and it throws the load back up the rope when you least want it. Nearly took the bucket out of the boy's hands. You want to mind that before someone cranks it into their own teeth."

It was nonsense. A windlass cannot do any such thing, and any man who had ever drawn water knew in his bones it could not, but I am a wizard, and I said it in the wizard's voice, the flat certain one that invites no argument because it comes from the one man in the valley whose business is precisely the things that ought not to happen, and I said it while laying confident hands on the frame and frowning at the joint of the post like a man diagnosing a sick horse. And the square, which had wanted very badly not to have felt what it had just felt, took the excuse I handed it with both hands. A wrong thing had happened at the well, and here was the wizard, who dealt in wrong things, telling them it was dry rot. Dry rot they could live with. The haggling woman went back to her eggs. The men at the oven wall laughed at some small thing, glad to be laughing. Kellard called across that he'd send a man to look at the post, and I said I'd look at it myself while I was up, and the morning closed back over the moment like water over a dropped stone.

Only two people in that square knew a windlass had not done it.

One was the boy, who stood with his hands still on the crank and his face gone carefully, terribly blank, the blank of a child who has felt the ground shift and is waiting to be told how frightened to be. I did not look at him longer than I looked at anyone. That was the whole art of it. I clapped the frame once more, and said, "Leave the water, lad, I'll want to pull the post," and gave him the smallest thing to do that would let him take his hands off the crank and walk away without it looking like fleeing.

The other was Lira.

She was in the doorway of the inn with a cloth in her hand and a table half-wiped behind her, and she was looking at the well, and she was not looking at the well the way the others had looked, in the quick animal startle that wants an explanation and is grateful for any. She was looking at it the way I look at a working I am trying to feel the shape of. She had seen the bucket rise. She had seen where the boy's hands were, and where mine were, a full stride away and too late, and she had done the sum. I met her eye for exactly as long as a man meets the eye of the woman whose inn he lodges at, no longer, and I smiled the ordinary smile, and she gave me the ordinary nod back, measured and neutral as a line in a ledger, and turned and finished wiping her table.

She said nothing. Anyone else who had done that sum would have said something, if only to the next person in for ale, a *did you see, the wizard's boy* that is half wonder and half a story worth the telling. Lira only finished her table and carried her cloth back inside. I marked it the way you mark a quiet where you had braced for a noise, and then the market morning went back to its business, and I had a windlass post to pretend to mend, and I let it go.

I found the boy where I knew he would be, up the path a way, sitting on the low wall at the turn of the track with his knees drawn up, in the exact attitude I had first seen him in a year and more before, the attitude of a boy doing the particular nothing that was in truth a kind of listening. He did not look at me as I came up. He looked at the valley.

I sat down on the wall beside him, and set the bread between us, and for a while I said nothing, because there are things you cannot walk a frightened person toward at speed.

"I didn't mean to," he said at last. Not defiant. Just setting the fact down where I could see it, the way his grandfather set a fact down. "I wasn't doing anything. I was drawing water. And then it went strange." He stopped there, because that was the whole of the word he had for it, and it was not near enough, and no one had ever given him a better one, because the thing had never before done itself where anyone could see.

"I know you weren't doing anything," I said. "That's rather the trouble."

He turned that over. He was a boy who turned everything over. "It's happened before," he said. "Little. The water in the trough goes toward my hand sometimes when I'm not minding it. I thought everyone's did."

"Everyone's does not," I said.

"I know that now." He said it flatly, and I heard under the flatness the size of the thing he had just learned, that he was not like the others, that a piece of the world answered him that did not answer them, and that he had been carrying it in the open all his life the way you carry a lamp in daylight, not knowing it was lit.

I have thought a great deal, since, about what I said to him on that wall, because it was the first true teaching I ever gave anyone, and I gave it badly, the way you give the important things, before you have had time to find the good words. But I will set down what I said, because he kept it, and it mattered.

"There are two kinds of unusual thing a person can be," I told him. "The world has a use for one kind and a fear of the other, and the trouble is they are the very same kind, and which one you get depends entirely on who is watching when it shows. You have a thing in you that is going to be worth more than this whole valley one day, to the right people, in the right room. And there are wrong people, and wrong rooms, and to them a boy who can call the water up a well is not a wonder. He is a resource. He is a thing to be got hold of early and used." I picked up a stone off the wall and turned it in my fingers, because I could not look at him and say the next part. "I am not going to let that happen to you. But I need your help to not let it happen, and your help is this: for now, between us and no one else, you hold it in. The way you hold the sound of the hill in. You've done that your whole life without being told. This is the same. You keep it close, and you keep it quiet, and you let it show only where I can see you, until you are old enough and strong enough that no room in the world can hold you against your will. Can you do that?"

He was quiet a long time. Below us the valley did its ordinary midday things, the beck loud, a cart on the low road, smoke standing up from the Wray place in the bottoms.

"Is that what you did?" he asked.

It was not the question I had braced for, and it went into me further than he could have known, because it was the exact right question, the one a duller child would never have found. He had heard the whole shape of it in what I had not said: that I spoke of wrong rooms like a man who had stood in one.

"Yes," I said, because he had earned the truth and I was done that morning handing out anything else. "That is what I did. And I did it too late, and not well, and it cost me some things I'd have liked to keep. So I know the road, which is why I'd rather you didn't have to walk the whole of it to learn it."

He nodded, slow, the grave structural nod he brought to anything he meant to build his life on. He picked up the loaf and tore the heel off it, because he was ten years old and had been up since before dawn and a frightened boy is still a hungry one, and he handed me the larger part, which was his grandfather in him, and we sat and ate my bread on the wall and did not talk any more about it.

Then, with his mouth full and his eyes on the hill, in the plain voice of a boy stating the last item on a list he has finally got straight, he said the thing I have kept longer than almost anything anyone ever said to me on that hill.

"I'll be quiet," he said. "Like the hill. It's loud all the time and nobody hears it. I can do that."
