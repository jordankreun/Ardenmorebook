# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Four: The Reading

Jorin went down the way, and I went in and set the lumen at my shoulder and sat down to the notebooks, as I had sat down to them a hundred evenings before. I gave all four of those evenings to them, and went down the cellar stairs at the end of the four already carrying somebody else's forty years.

I had been reading them in order, as Brenna required, one volume at a time, each handed over only when she judged the last one had been treated with the seriousness it was owed. I had stopped arguing with the system. Her grandmother had earned her records the slow way, a season at a time on a cold hilltop, and Brenna portioned them out the same way. She was right to. Read all at once, the notebooks would have been a curiosity. Read across two years, at the pace of their making, they had become something else: a correspondence, almost, with a woman I would never meet, who had stood where I stood and heard what I heard and had nothing to work with but patience and a pencil, and who had, with those two tools, got further than I liked to admit.

I had a method for the reading by then, and I kept to it. I sat at the workroom bench with her volume on my left and my own book on my right and a loose sheet between them for the concordance, and I did not read her front to back at the speed of the sentences, as you read a story. I read a season of her at a time and then went and found my own season to lay against it. She gave her dates by quarter-days and by the state of the stream. It took me a fortnight to learn to read them and nothing at all after that. When she wrote that the hill came harder on the nights a weather stood over the valley, I turned back in my own numbers and found three storms out of my own thirty days saying the identical thing in a different hand, and I ruled the two entries together and sat back, and left the rule lying across them. When she wrote of a quiet spell in her fourth autumn I had nothing whatever to set beside it, and I wrote *no comparison* in the middle column. It is the entry I made most often across two years of her, and I never learned to like making it.

The bench wanted setting up before any of it could begin, and the setting up was a small ceremony I did not skip. Her volume flat on the left with a strip of lead across the gutter to hold it open, the stitching having gone soft in three places, and I would not be the man who broke her spine. My own book squared to the bench edge on the right. The loose sheet between, ruled down its length into three with the rule and a pencil: her side, my side, and the middle column where the two of them were made to answer each other or made to admit they could not. A second pencil laid across the top of the sheet to stop it curling. The lumen behind my left shoulder, so the light came onto the page and not into my eyes.

Then there was her hand to get used to, and that took a season of evenings on its own. She wrote small and level and without ornament, a working hand and not a taught one, and she abbreviated in ways she never once glossed, because she was not writing for anybody. A stroke under a word meant she was sure of it. Two strokes meant she had been sure, and had gone back afterward and checked. A word ringed round meant she doubted it and had let it stand anyway, and I thought more of that ring than of anything else in the books. She never crossed anything out. She would write a thing, and then years later write underneath it that she had been wrong about it, and leave the two of them standing there together.

Her seventh winter I laid against my own second, and got, for once, something better than nothing. She had four entries running across a hard frost and all four are short.

*Michaelmas and nine weeks. Stream locked in the shallows, black down the middle. Hard frost four nights together. The song comes thinner in a frost. Not later, only thinner. I have said for years that it was my own ears going stiff with the cold, and I have stopped saying it.*

*Fourth night, sat out with a hot brick in my lap to be certain of the ears. Thinner still.*

*Fifth night, thaw in the afternoon. Song back to its ordinary weight before the ice was off the stones. So it is not the water.*

I had the frost of my own second winter in my own book, three nights of it, taken above ground with nothing but my hands and a lamp, and my figures said thinner and said it in her shape, down and not late. I brought hers and mine up alongside each other and wrote *agrees* in the middle column. Then, because *agrees* is a comfortable word, I set down beneath it the two ways they might agree and mean nothing: cold hands hear less, and cold stone carries differently. Neither of them touches her fifth night. I set both objections down under the agreement in smaller letters.

The middle column mostly did not get *agrees*. She had kept her books through forty turns of the year and I had kept mine through two, and two years of my numbers laid against forty of her ears is not a comparison, it is an apology. Whole runs of her went past with nothing of mine to set beside them. A summer she spent almost entirely on the hour after the evening pulse, when I had never once been out on that hilltop at that hour with a pencil in my hand. Three years in her twenties when she was recording the state of the stream to a nicety I have never troubled with, low in June and lower in July and the exact stone the water came off. I wrote *no comparison* down the middle of those pages and turned them.

There was one rule of the reading that was mine and not Brenna's. I never turned past the last page I had earned that evening. The volume lay open under my hand every night of two years, and the woman had been dead a long time and would not have minded in the least, and I did not do it, because the evening I went ahead would be the evening I stopped keeping a concordance and started looking for the end.

Four evenings of that week. The rain came on the second and stayed. The lumen burned amber at my elbow and the fire went down behind me and I let it go down, because a cold room kept me honest past midnight, and I would get up at some hour with my ink gone thick and my feet gone dead and the valley black in the window, and go to my bed carrying a dead woman's fourth autumn.

The rain suited it. A wet night puts your own weather down and takes the valley out of the window, and by the second evening I had stopped hearing it except when it changed, and it changed twice: once toward midnight into the small hard sort that comes off the north wood, and once, an hour after, back again. My ink went thick by ten and wanted the pot moved nearer the lamp. My feet went at about the half hour after that. I read her best cold, sitting up straight, with the fire out behind me and nothing burning in that room but the lumen.

By then I could place her within a few years of her own life by the look of a book, before I read a word in it. The early volumes are generously spaced, a wide margin left standing on both sides, a woman with paper enough and the evening to fill it. Somewhere through the middle of the run the margins go. She writes out to both edges, and when the ruled lines are used up she goes on along the foot below the last of them, and once or twice up the side of a page turned sideways. Nothing in the entries accounts for it. She never says she is short of paper, and the quarter-days and the state of the stream go on being given as carefully as they ever were.

The volume I had that week was from her late middle years, the pencil pressed harder, the entries shorter. On the third evening of the four I came to the entry her forty years had been going toward.

She wrote: *I have been wrong all this time about one thing. I thought the song was for the tree, and the tree's answering was for the hill, two old things keeping company. It is not so. I have listened long enough now to say it is not so. The song goes to the tree and the tree holds it and passes it on, the way a man at a door takes a coat, and what the song is for has not come yet. That is the whole of it. The song is for something that is not here. It is the patientest thing I ever heard of, and I will not live to see what it is waiting for, and I find I mind that less than I expected. Somebody will.*

I read it through twice. Then a third time with the loose sheet laid over the rest of the page, to keep the sentence from being helped along by its neighbours. It stood in the middle of an ordinary week. There is a line above it about the stream being up and a line below it about a gate that wanted rehanging. She had not underscored it. She had not ringed it. She had written it at the size she wrote everything, in the same level hand, and gone on to the gate.

I copied the entry out fair into my own working notes, dated it in her year and mine, and sat for a while. Then I banked the fire and went to bed. The moon was dark the next night and I meant to be sharp for it.

Being sharp for it has a procedure, and the procedure is dull. I ate at four in the afternoon and ate plainly. I took no drink. I did the day's ordinary work with my hands and gave it none of my attention, and went to my bed at seven in the evening, and slept badly, and got up at two. A reading hand wants to come to the stone rested, fed, and bored. Every hour of anticipation you carry down the stair is an hour of yourself you will have to subtract out of the record afterward, and there is no honest way to do that subtraction.

The day's ordinary work went into the glasshouse, and it was the right sort: hours of it, all of it in the hands, none of it anywhere else. I went down at eight and worked the propagation benches at the cold end, pricking seedlings out into boxes, and there is no better occupation for somebody who must not think, because the job punishes thinking exactly as hard as it punishes daydreaming and lets you off neither. I watered along the centre walk with the can held low. I opened two of the hooded vents at eleven and shut one of them again at two, the wind having come round. I carried the winter's dead stuff out and burned it wet at the bottom of the slope, and stood in the smoke of that longer than the job wanted.

At four I ate, and plainly meant bread, a piece off the cheese under its cloth, two apples out of the rack, and water. Nothing that would sit on me. Nothing that would want anything after it. Then I put an hour into the mare, brushing her down for no reason except that brushing a horse is work the hands can do with nobody at home behind them, and turned her out, and shut the barn, and went in.

I laid the gear out on the bench the night before, in the order I would want it, because a man fumbling in the cold at four in the morning invents whatever he cannot find. The lumen. The folded blanket. Two notebooks, the working one and the fair one. Three pencils sharpened, because a point in that cold goes the way it goes and two of them may go in a night. A rule and a length of waxed string for laying out a bearing. The case for the spent stone, lined and waxed, with its label written but for the hour. A flask of water I did not mean to drink and drank.

I laid them out in a line and then handled every one of them in the order I would want it, and the handling is the point. Looking at a list is not checking it. So: the lumen turned up and down twice and left low. The blanket unfolded, shaken out, and folded again along its old creases, because a blanket folded fresh in the dark opens the wrong way and you find that out kneeling. The two notebooks opened at the page I meant to start on, a pencil laid in each as a marker, working book on top of the fair one. The three pencils sharpened at the bench with the good light on them, and each point run across the ball of my thumb after; one of them I sharpened a second time, not because it was blunt but because I had not liked the sound it made. The rule and the waxed string coiled together so they would come up out of the pile as one thing and not as two. The case for the spent stone opened, the wool taken out and shaken and put back, the case shut. The label written out but for the hour, and the pen set down beside it, so that I would not be hunting a pen at five in the morning with my hands gone stupid.

Then I left the lot lying and went to my bed, and did not go back to look at it, though I thought about going back to look at it.

Bed at seven in the evening in the spring is no sort of bed. The light was still in the room and still on the ceiling, and I lay under it with my boots off and my mind going round and round a fine mortar joint, the sort of thing it does the moment it has been told to stop. I slept from somewhere after eight and woke at eleven certain it was three, and worked out from the window that it was not, and lay a while with my eyes open, and slept again in the shallow way sleep goes when it knows it is going to be interrupted, and dreamed about counting, and woke twice more.

At two I got up without needing to be got up. That happens perhaps once in five times and is the only part of the procedure I have never made reliable. I did not light the fire. I washed in cold water, put on the shirt I had left over the chair, ate nothing, and drank half a cup standing at the table.

I stopped in my own door a minute before I went down. There was no moon at all. The valley had put itself out entirely, not one light in the whole length of it, and the air had that scoured smell the spring keeps for the last of its cold nights.

I went down at the third hour after midnight, into the cold that lives in cut stone, with the lumen in one hand.

That stair is not a thing to hurry in the dark, and I do not hurry it. The cold does not meet you at the top of it. It gathers on you as you go down, so that you arrive already inside it, and the door of the world has shut behind you without anybody touching it. The smell down there is roots and apples and cold dressed stone, and under all three the flat mineral smell of mortar that has not entirely given up its water. The sacks stood along the west wall where they always stood. The flour bin had its lid on and the weight on the lid. Somebody with no business in the room would have taken it for an ordinary root store and been perfectly right.

Two blanks lay in the cellar floor, set flush in the stone a pace apart. The first had listened for thirty days, the summer before, and I had read it then and got from it the beginnings of everything: the hum's architecture, the two pulses, the first thin evidence that the sound was not weather. The second had lain listening ever since, through the harvest and the rent book and the steel and the glass, nine months of nights taken down entire while I was busy above it with money. A recording blank does not tire and does not select. It keeps everything, at depth, in the fine lattice of its making, and it gives everything back to a reading hand in the order it was received.

The cellar took its time about accepting me, as it always did. A room cut into a hill has no weather; it has a temperature, and the temperature that night was the temperature it keeps in February and in August. My breath stood in the light. I set the lumen on the shelf and turned it down to the low amber, because a bright lamp in a small room is a noise of its own kind. I unrolled the blanket. I put my coat on over my working shirt and then took it off again, a coat between a man and the cold being one more thing between a man and the count, and settled the matter with the sleeves down and the coat folded under the notebook.

Then I sat on that floor for a quarter of an hour and did nothing whatever.

That quarter hour is the part of my trade that looks least like work and is the least skippable. You are letting your own weather die down. Heart, breath, the low hum you carry about with you after a day of being alive; all of it has to go quiet enough that it will not print itself over the top of the record you have come down to read.

I sat with my back against the west wall between two sacks of last year's roots, legs out in front of me, hands palm down on my thighs, and I did not shut my eyes. Shut your eyes and your body is told it is going to sleep, and your body believes it. So I looked at the floor, at the near edge of the light, and let the room have me.

The first three or four minutes of it are wasted and I have never found a way to shorten them. You are still arriving. The day comes up in you in the order it happened, wanting to be finished: a seedling box carried too far and set down in the wrong place, the second vent, a smell of wet smoke, a mortar joint. All of that has to go past like carts on a road, and the trick is to let it go past and not to get up on any of it.

Then the body, which takes longer and is more interesting. The heart is first and loudest, and it goes out of the ears within a minute and down into the chest, and from the chest into nowhere in particular, and that is the one you want. Then the pulse in the hands, and the pulse in the hands is the whole business, and it does not so much stop as stop being an event. Then the breath. Then the fine ring the ears keep after a day of iron and weather and people talking, always there, never noticed until somebody goes looking for silence.

And the room does its part, because a cut room in a hill has a sound of its own when you are still enough for it, and none of it is mystery. The vent breathing very slightly against the night. Water somewhere behind stone, further off than it sounds. A sack settling a quarter inch. Twice in that quarter hour something ticked in the wall, and both times my hands wanted to be doing something about it, and both times I left them where they were.

I keep no clock down there and I do not want one. You know the quarter hour is done because your own noise has stopped being a list and become a floor. It is the only measure I have for it.

The reading of such a stone is done palm to crystal, and it is not mystical, whatever it looks like. It is close work, like assaying, like taking a long column of figures that someone else has kept. You still your own noise as far as your training goes, you set your hand flat, and you let the record come up through the skin in its own order, and you count. I knelt on the folded blanket I keep down there for the purpose, and set my palm to the second stone, and for the better part of three hours, in the dark before the birds of the spring's darkest night, I did the dullest and most demanding thing in my trade. I audited a hill.

It goes like this. The record does not come up as sound, whatever people want of it. It comes up as pressure and grain under the skin of the palm, the way a south wall gives the day's heat back into your hand at night, except that the pressure has a shape and the shape keeps changing at a rate you cannot hurry. Nine months of nights do not arrive in nine months; they arrive compressed, at whatever pace the crystal consents to give them, and the pace is not yours to set. My whole part of the arrangement was to hold still and count.

The first pass is only for the shape. You are not hunting findings on the first pass; you are learning the hand of the record, the way an auditor learns a new clerk's writing before he goes near the columns. I took the first pass at a walk, start to finish, and wrote nothing at all while I did it, and it took the better part of an hour and cost me more discipline than the rest of the night together.

Then the counting. Palm flat, two fingers of the free hand on the page to keep my place, the pencil working in the dark by feel, tallies in fives, because you cannot look away to the paper and hold the count at the same time, and a tally you can barely read afterward is still worth more than a tidy number you have improved out of memory.

The page is the difficulty. You cannot look at it, so you learn to work a page you cannot see. Two fingers of the free hand walk down the margin a line at a time. The pencil comes down to meet them and puts in its four strokes and closes the gate with the fifth across. The fingers move. The pencil follows. When a line is full the fingers know it before the pencil does, and when a page is full there is nothing for it but to turn the page one-handed with the other palm still flat on the stone, and that is a piece of clumsiness I gave up trying to make elegant. I turn it, and I lose a beat, and I write *turn* in the margin, so the beat is lost on the paper as well as in the room and I can put it back where it belongs afterward.

The first tallies on that sheet are a clerk's work. The last are the work of a hand at the wrong end of an argument with the cold: the fives leaning over, the gates not shutting, two rows run together where the fingers lied about the margin. It is a good page. The date at the top is the only thing on it a stranger could read, and I wrote the date upstairs the evening before, in a warm room, with a lamp on it.

I lost the count twice inside the first hour. Once because my own pulse came up into my palm and got itself mixed into the record. That is the commonest fault in the work and the whole reason for that quarter hour of sitting still; I took the hand off, shook it out, and began the segment again from its start. Once because my knees had gone past aching into the hot numbness that comes after it, and I was attending to my knees and to nothing else. I got up and walked the length of the room four times in the dark, and put my hands under my arms until the feeling came back into them badly, worse than the cold, and knelt again and began again.

Twice more across the three hours I stopped, warmed my hands, and re-took a stretch I had already taken, on no better ground than that I had wanted the first result too much. That is not scrupulousness. That is self-interest. A finding I had wanted was a finding I would be arguing with for the rest of my life, and I would sooner spend the twenty minutes in the cellar and be done.

The cold does its work from the floor upward and it is not the sharp cold of a night outdoors. It comes into you through the shins and the heels of the hands and it is patient about it. Twice I felt the first flicker of a shiver starting across the shoulders, and a shiver in the shoulders goes down the arm and into the palm and prints itself on the record like a thumb in wet plaster, and both times I stopped before it arrived, and stood, and walked, and got warm enough to be no use at the stone, and knelt down again. There is a temperature at which this work goes best, and it is colder than comfort and warmer than that floor, and the whole art of the middle hours is walking back and forth across it and never settling anywhere.

I will give the findings as I entered them in my notes that morning, because the order of them is the honest record of that night.

The first finding was this: the hum carries my signature.

Not generally. Specifically. Every worked thing takes an impression of its maker; that is elementary, that is why sensitized paper is any use at all. But the hill is not my work, and the impression was not residue. Nine months of nights showed my own arcane signature worked into the fine structure of the hum itself, repeated, elaborated, corrected over time, the later renderings truer than the early ones, the way a copyist improves across a long manuscript. The hill has been learning my hand. I wrote that sentence in my notes, looked at it, struck out *learning my hand*, and wrote instead: *incorporating my signature with increasing accuracy*. Both were true. Only the second was a finding.

Then I did what wants doing after a finding of that size. I tried to kill it.

Residue was the first candidate and the easiest to be rid of. Every man leaves his impression on what he handles, and I had knelt on that floor a dozen times in nine months, keyed the stone with my own palm, put my hands on those walls. But residue is a smear. It lies on the top of a thing. It does not run through the fine structure of a signal at depth, and above all it does not improve. What I had was worked in, and it was truer in the ninth month than in the second, and residue does not get better with practice. I wrote *not residue* and set the three reasons under it in a column.

The column read, in the order I put it down. That residue lies on the surface of a thing, and this was at depth, in the lattice and not on it. That residue does not repeat, and this recurred in every one of the nine months. That residue does not improve, and this improved. Then I ruled a line under the three and wrote the one honest weakness of the argument beneath the rule: that I had no piece of nine-month residue to hold it up against, nobody having ever had a reason to make one. I wrote that down too, and left it there under the line where I could not miss it.

The second candidate was myself, kneeling in the dark wanting a result. Against that I had the first stone: thirty days of the summer before, read and written up in a season when I had no notion to look for any such thing. I got my old figures for the grain of the hum out of the working book and laid them against the new, and what could be matched, matched, and what was new to the second stone was new in one direction only, toward my own hand and not away from it.

Laying two sets of counts against each other in a cold room by a low lamp is awkward work. The old book open on the corner of the blanket. The new sheet held flat under one knee. The pencil in my teeth while I found the place, and the place found by the smell of the paper as much as by anything. Thirty days against nine months is not a fair fight and I did not pretend it was one. What I could do was ask the narrow question. Not *is it the same* but *does the old set hold anything the new set contradicts*, and a narrow question can be answered. It did not. And where the thirty days had given me something I could not account for at the time and had written down anyway, because what I could not account for was exactly what wanted writing down, the nine months gave the same thing back, oftener and truer.

The third candidate was the crystal itself, and I dealt with it by re-taking three segments out of their order, one from the second month, one from the sixth, one from the ninth, chosen by a student's old trick: I let the pencil fall on the page and took the segment nearest where the point landed. All three gave back what they had given the first time. I have no proof against a stone that would give me the same wrong answer three times running. I have only the habit of asking it from three sides and writing down that it did not change its story.

I let a pencil choose because I cannot be trusted to. Left to myself I would take the segment I remembered, and what I remembered was what had struck me, and what had struck me is exactly the evidence I ought not to be weighing twice. Let the point fall and you get the nineteenth night of the second month, which nobody remembers and nothing hangs on, and that is the entire value of it. I took the three where the point landed and I did not take a fourth, because a fourth would have been me choosing again.

The second finding was this: the predawn pulse has structure now.

It did not use to. Two years ago the pulse was a pulse: one long slow event, regular as breathing, the same each night within the tolerances of any instrument I own. The record showed the change beginning in a small way about the time the Study was finished and accelerating from the winter of the glasshouse: variation inside the pulse, ordered variation, patterned recurrence with systematic differences, elements that repeat and elements that answer. I have spent my working life around signal and structure, and I know what ordered variation against a stable base is generally for, and I sat in that cellar at four in the morning declining, as a professional matter, to use the word for it. I will use it now. The word is grammar.

I got it the dullest way it can be got, which is by tallying. An element that recurs is a fact you can count. An element that answers another element is a fact you can count twice, once for each end of it. So I counted. I took the pulses in blocks of ten nights across the nine months and set down, for every block, how many distinct elements I could tell apart with any confidence, how many of those came again inside the same block, and how many stood in the same relation to one another every time they came.

That is three figures to a block and twenty-seven blocks across the nine months. The first block gave four, one, and none: four things I could tell apart at all, one of them coming again inside its ten nights, and nothing yet standing in a fixed relation to anything else. The fourth block gave six, three, and one. By the twelfth I was writing smaller to keep the row on its line, and the row said nine, six, three.

I set no store by any single row. A row is one hand's opinion of ten nights, taken in the dark, at whatever hour that stretch happened to arrive at, and I could argue any one of them down. What I could not argue down was the column read from the top. Errors do not queue up in order and go the same way for nine months. They scatter. That is how you know them.

The numbers went up. Not smoothly. There is a flat stretch through the deep of the winter that I have no account for, and a step up on either side of it. But they went up, and they went up faster in the second column than the first. The hill was not only making more elements. It was making the same ones oftener, and putting them in the same places.

I ran the block-counts twice, forward from the second month and then backward from the ninth, because I was counting toward a rising figure and would have helped it rise. Backward, they fell exactly as they had climbed. The two columns agreed inside the tolerance of a cold hand and a poor pencil.

Somewhere in the middle of that the lumen sank as it does at that hour, and I woke it up with my thumb, and noticed that my breath had stopped standing in the light, and understood that my face had come to the same temperature as the room. I put my hands under my arms and looked at two columns of figures on a page. The figures said what they said. There was nobody down there to say it to.

The third finding took the longest, because I did not believe my own count and ran it four times.

The hum radiates. It always has; that is what a hum is. But inside the general radiation, the record showed a directed component. Oriented. Consistent across nine months to a fraction of a degree. I worked the geometry twice on paper, there in the cold with my breath standing in the lumen light, because a bearing means nothing until you lay it against the world. The directed component went upward through the body of the hill, at a rising angle, on a line that passed through the cellar itself. Through the cellar's northeast corner.

A line. Not a spreading, not a swelling, not a general warmth in the stone. A line, held to a fraction of a degree across nine months of nights, and I thought while I sat there of a dead woman with no instrument but her own ears writing that the sound lay along something, and coming truer one way than the other, and calling it the grain because she had needed a word and that was the word to hand. She had heard the direction of it forty years before I measured the angle of it. I set that down beside my figures without comment, which is where it belongs and where I have left it.

I will set down how that was got, because a bearing is the one finding in my trade I could talk myself into, and I do not care to have it taken on my word.

The first run gave a direction and I mistrusted it at once, on the sole ground that it was interesting. So I put it away from me. I wrote the figure on a loose leaf, folded the leaf, sat on it, and took the whole count again from the second month without looking at what I had written. The second run agreed with the first to the width of the pencil, which is not comfort; two counts by one hand in one hour are a single count with a witness. I stood and walked the room and came back and took it a third time, from the ninth month backward. Then a fourth with my left palm, coarser and slower than my right, and the left gave a cruder figure and the same bearing.

The left hand is a poor instrument and I keep it for exactly this. It comes to the record late, it loses more than it takes, and it drops half of what it holds before the pencil arrives. What it has is none of my right hand's habits. It gave me a coarse figure, arrived at slowly, sitting square on top of the other three. Then I got the folded leaf out from under me and opened it and laid all four figures down the margin, one under the next, and looked at them a while with my hands in my armpits. The fourth of them wanted writing twice. My hand had reached the stage where the pencil goes where it is put and not where it is aimed.

Then the paper. Angle off the floor and angle off north, both taken from the room itself, which I had at least the luck to know exactly, Jorin having shot that floor twice and his lines being on my drawings. I struck the bearing out with the rule and the waxed string, carried the elevation up through the plane of the floor, and it met the wall high in the northeast; and I did not care for the answer, so I laid the whole construction out a second time from the other end, from the wall back down to the stone, to see whether the geometry would consent to put it anywhere else.

It would not. Four counts, two constructions, one corner.

I sat back on my heels and looked at the corner. The chest stood there where I had set it the summer before, against the wall the hill had kept driest, black wood and old metal, holding what it held.

There was a quality to the directed component that my notation has no mark for. I have a mark for hostile. It was not that. I have a mark for inert, and it was the opposite of that. What it most resembled was a name, said over and over, by something content to wait between the sayings. In my notes I wrote *salutation, sustained*.

Three findings, then, in a cold room in the dark: the hill knows my hand; the hill is building a grammar; and the hill is saying something, patiently, over and over, toward the corner where her chest stands.

And under my knees, holding all of it, the stone's long record and the grandmother's short sentence lay one against the other. Forty years of a woman's ears: *the song is for something that is not here.* Thirty days and then fourteen months of my instruments: *salutation, sustained, on a bearing.* Two ways of counting. One fact, come at from two sides of a century.

I set the two lines one above the other in my notes and drew no conclusion under them. I had none I could defend. I had a direction, and a direction is not a conclusion.

Two other things I noted that morning, for the honesty of the record. The old oak's long line of attention I checked twice, and it ran nowhere near my bearing. Whatever the tree held itself toward, it was not my cellar. There were two open questions on that hill and not one. And whether the woman whose notebooks lay under my hand and the woman who had sworn the listening to Cael were one grandmother or two, I still had not asked, and did not ask now. Some questions keep better than they answer.

What I did next is what my trade does when it does not know: I extended the record. I lifted the second stone, cased it, and set a third blank flush in its place, and keyed it, and gave it no term at all. Let it listen as long as listening takes.

It is ten minutes of work with good hands and it took me the better part of half an hour. Lifting a spent blank is done with a chisel of soft brass and a great deal of patience, the stone being flush and the mortar around it fine, and a chipped edge is a chipped record. My fingers had stopped being much use to me. I got the second stone into its case on the wool and wrote the hour on the label, and the writing was the worst part of it; my hand made the figures of the date like a man practising his own name. Then the third blank out of its wrap, bedded down in the seat the second had left, the mortar mixed thin and worked in with my thumb, and the keying, which is a small quiet business done with the palm and takes exactly as long as it takes.

Brass, because brass gives before crystal does and steel does not, and a steel chisel in a tired hand finds that out for you once and once only. And the chip is never where you can see it. The stone takes the damage inward along its own lines, and what you lose is not the corner, it is whatever the corner was holding, and you do not discover which nights those were until you go looking for them and they are not there to be looked at. So you cut the mortar and never the stone. Score the joint the whole way round first. Take the bed out in three passes and not in one. Leave the corners until last, because a corner is where a hurrying man puts his weight. And drive it with the heel of the hand and not a mallet. A hand can be stopped in the middle of what it is doing.

I had gone round it four times before it shifted at all. On the fourth pass one long side let go with a small dry sound like a nail drawn out of dry deal, and the stone sat loose in its seat, a finger's breadth proud of the floor, and I waited a full minute for my hands before I would touch it, because a stone dropped on a stone floor is nine months of nights and there is no second copy anywhere in the world. That is the reason for the half hour.

I gave it no term. Every blank I had ever set had a term written into it at the keying, thirty days, sixty, nine months; a term is how you keep the stone honest and how you keep yourself from going down every week to fret at it. I left the term off, and wrote *open* in the register with the date beside it, and put the register back on the shelf, and my hands ached in the way they ache when they have been cold far longer than they should have been.

Then I gathered my notes and my lamp and climbed the stairs, up out of the stone cold into the ordinary cold of a spring morning an hour before dawn, and stood in my own door. The valley was dark below. One light burned early down at the forge, where a man who had kept a four-day count for five months was already awake. The birds had not started. The east had begun to grey. Under my boots, far down, at the hour it had kept since before the village had a name, the hill rose to its pulse and said what it had been saying all along, to whatever was not here yet. I stood there with my notes under my arm and my hands still no use to me. I live on top of a word.
