# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Four: The Reading

I spent the evenings of those four days with the notebooks, as I had spent a hundred evenings before them, and I went down the cellar stairs at the end of them already carrying somebody else's forty years.

I had been reading them in order, as Brenna required, one volume at a time, each handed over only when she judged the last one had been treated with the seriousness it was owed. There was no arguing with the system and I had stopped trying; her grandmother had earned her records the slow way, a season at a time on a cold hilltop, and Brenna portioned them out the same way, and I have come to believe she was right to. Read all at once, the notebooks would have been a curiosity. Read across two years, at the pace of their making, they had become something else: a correspondence, almost, with a woman I would never meet, who had stood where I stood and heard what I heard and had nothing to work with but patience and a pencil, and who had, with those two tools, got further than I liked to admit.

I had a method for the reading by then, and the method was most of the pleasure of it. I sat at the workroom bench with her volume on my left and my own book on my right and a loose sheet between them for the concordance, and I did not read her the way a man reads a story, front to back at the speed of the sentences. I read a season of her at a time and then went and found my own season to lay against it. She gave her dates by quarter-days and by the state of the beck, which cost me a fortnight once to work out and has cost me nothing since. When she wrote that the hill came harder on the nights a weather stood over the valley, I turned back in my own numbers and found three storms out of my own thirty days saying the identical thing in a different hand, and I ruled the two entries together and felt, for about a minute, less alone in the trade than I had felt in years. When she wrote of a quiet spell in her fourth autumn I had nothing whatever to set beside it, and I wrote *no comparison* in the middle column. It is the entry I made most often across two years of her, and I never learned to like making it.

Four evenings of that week. The rain came on the second and stayed. The lumen burned amber at my elbow and the fire went down behind me and I let it go down, because a cold room keeps a man honest past midnight, and I would get up at some hour with my ink gone thick and my feet gone dead and the valley entirely black in the window, and go to my bed carrying a dead woman's fourth autumn, and be perfectly content.

The volume I had that week was from her late middle years, the pencil pressed harder, the entries shorter. And on the third evening of the four, by the lumen's amber, I came to the place where the whole of her forty years finally stood up and said what it had gathered itself to say.

She wrote: *I have been wrong all this time about one thing. I thought the song was for the tree, and the tree's answering was for the hill, two old things keeping company. It is not so. I have listened long enough now to say it is not so. The song goes to the tree and the tree holds it and passes it on, the way a man at a door takes a coat, and what the song is for has not come yet. That is the whole of it. The song is for something that is not here. It is the patientest thing I ever heard of, and I will not live to see what it is waiting for, and I find I mind that less than I expected. Somebody will.*

I copied the entry out fair into my own working notes, dated it in her year and mine, and sat for a while, and then I banked the fire and went to bed, because the moon was dark the next night, and I meant to be sharp for it.

Being sharp for it has a procedure, and the procedure is unglamorous. I ate at four in the afternoon and ate plainly. I took no drink. I did the day's ordinary work with my hands and gave it none of my attention, and I went to my bed at seven in the evening like a man of eighty, and slept badly, and got up at two. A reading hand wants to come to the stone rested, fed, and bored. Every hour of anticipation you carry down the stair is an hour of yourself you will have to subtract out of the record afterward, and subtraction of that kind is guesswork wearing arithmetic's coat.

I laid the gear out on the bench the night before, in the order I would want it, because a man fumbling in the cold at four in the morning invents whatever he cannot find. The lumen. The folded blanket. Two notebooks, the working one and the fair one. Three pencils sharpened, because a point in that cold goes the way it goes and I have snapped two in one night before now. A rule and a length of waxed string for laying out a bearing. The case for the spent stone, lined and waxed, with its label written but for the hour. A flask of water I did not mean to drink and drank.

I stood in my own doorway a minute before I went down. There was no moon at all. The valley had put itself out entirely, not one light in the whole length of it, and the air had that scoured smell the spring keeps for the last of its cold nights.

I went down at the third hour after midnight, into the cold that lives in cut stone, with the lumen in one hand.

Two blanks lay in the cellar floor, set flush in the stone a pace apart. The first had listened for thirty days, the summer before, and I had read it then and got from it the beginnings of everything: the hum's architecture, the two pulses, the first thin evidence that the sound was not weather. The second had lain listening ever since, through the harvest and the rent book and the steel and the glass, nine months of nights taken down entire while I was busy above it with money. A recording blank does not tire and does not select. It keeps everything, at depth, in the fine lattice of its making, and it gives everything back to a reading hand in the order it was received. There is no droller commentary on a man's year than the patience of his instruments.

The cellar took its time about accepting me, as it always did. A room cut into a hill has no weather; it has a temperature, and the temperature that night was the temperature it keeps in February and in August, and my breath stood in the light in a way that felt like an opinion. I set the lumen on the shelf and turned it down to the low amber, because a bright lamp in a small room is a noise of its own kind. I unrolled the blanket. I put my coat on over my working shirt and then took it off again, a coat between a man and the cold being one more thing between a man and the count, and settled the matter with the sleeves down and the coat folded under the notebook.

Then I sat on that floor for a quarter of an hour and did nothing whatever.

That quarter hour is the part of my trade that looks least like work and is the least skippable. You are letting your own weather die down. Heart, breath, the low hum a man carries about with him after a day of being alive; all of it has to go quiet enough that it will not print itself over the top of the record you have come down to read.

The reading of such a stone is done palm to crystal, and it is not mystical, whatever it looks like. It is close work, like assaying, like taking a long column of figures that someone else has kept. You still your own noise as far as your training goes, you set your hand flat, and you let the record come up through the skin in its own order, and you count. I knelt on the folded blanket I keep down there for the purpose, and set my palm to the second stone, and for the better part of three hours, in the dark before the birds of the spring's darkest night, I did the dullest and most demanding thing in my trade. I audited a hill.

It goes like this. The record does not come up as sound, whatever people want of it. It comes up as pressure and grain under the skin of the palm, the way a south wall gives the day's heat back into your hand at night, except that the pressure has a shape and the shape keeps changing at a rate you cannot hurry. Nine months of nights do not arrive in nine months; they arrive compressed, at whatever pace the crystal consents to give them, and the pace is not yours to set. My whole part of the arrangement was to hold still and count.

The first pass is only for the shape. You are not hunting findings on the first pass; you are learning the hand of the record, the way an auditor learns a new clerk's writing before he goes near the columns. I took the first pass at a walk, start to finish, and wrote nothing at all while I did it, and it took the better part of an hour and cost me more discipline than the rest of the night together.

Then the counting. Palm flat, two fingers of the free hand on the page to keep my place, the pencil working in the dark by feel, tallies in fives, because you cannot look away to the paper and hold the count at the same time, and a tally you can barely read afterward is still worth more than a tidy number you have improved out of memory.

I lost the count twice inside the first hour. Once because my own pulse came up into my palm and got itself mixed into the record. That is the commonest fault in the work and the whole reason for that quarter hour of sitting still; I took the hand off, shook it out, and began the segment again from its start. Once because my knees had gone past aching into that hot stupid numbness that has no opinions, and a man attending to his knees is attending to nothing else. I got up and walked the length of the room four times in the dark, and put my hands under my arms until the feeling came back into them badly, worse than the cold, and knelt again and began again.

Twice more across the three hours I stopped, warmed my hands, and re-took a stretch I had already taken, on no better ground than that I had wanted the first result too much. That is not scrupulousness. That is plain self-interest. A finding you have wanted is a finding you will be arguing with at four in the morning for the rest of your life, and I would sooner spend the twenty minutes in the cellar and be done.

I will give the findings as I entered them in my notes that morning, because I have found no better way to give them, and because the plain order of them is the honest record of that night, each one arriving on the heels of the last like a man bringing worse news politely.

The first finding was this: the hum carries my signature.

Not generally. Specifically. Every worked thing takes an impression of its maker; that is elementary, that is why sensitized paper is any use at all. But the hill is not my work, and the impression was not residue. Nine months of nights showed my own arcane signature worked into the fine structure of the hum itself, repeated, elaborated, corrected over time, the later renderings truer than the early ones, the way a copyist improves across a long manuscript. The hill has been learning my hand. I wrote that sentence in my notes, looked at it, struck out *learning my hand*, and wrote instead, because precision is a discipline exactly when you least want it: *incorporating my signature with increasing accuracy*. Both sentences were true. One of them I could defend to a committee.

Then I did what wants doing after a finding of that size, which is to try to kill it.

Residue was the first candidate and the easiest to be rid of. Every man leaves his impression on what he handles, and I had knelt on that floor a dozen times in nine months, keyed the stone with my own palm, put my hands on those walls. But residue is a smear. It lies on the top of a thing. It does not run through the fine structure of a signal at depth, and above all it does not improve. What I had was worked in, and it was truer in the ninth month than in the second, and residue never once in the history of the trade got better with practice. I wrote *not residue* and set the three reasons under it in a column.

The second candidate was myself, kneeling in the dark wanting a result. Against that I had the first stone: thirty days of the summer before, read and written up in a season when I had no notion to look for any such thing. I got my old figures for the grain of the hum out of the working book and laid them against the new, and what could be matched, matched, and what was new to the second stone was new in one direction only, toward my own hand and not away from it.

The third candidate was the crystal itself, and I dealt with it by re-taking three segments out of their order, one from the second month, one from the sixth, one from the ninth, chosen by a student's old trick: I let the pencil fall on the page and took the segment nearest where the point landed. All three gave back what they had given the first time. I have no proof against a stone determined to lie to me. I have only the habit of asking it the same question from three sides and writing down, plainly, that it did not change its story.

The second finding was this: the predawn pulse has structure now.

It did not use to. Two years ago the pulse was a pulse: one long slow event, regular as breathing, the same each night within the tolerances of any instrument I own. The record showed the change beginning in a small way about the time the Study was finished and accelerating from the winter of the glasshouse: variation inside the pulse, ordered variation, patterned recurrence with systematic differences, elements that repeat and elements that answer. I have spent my working life around signal and structure, and I know what ordered variation against a stable base is generally for, and I sat in that cellar at four in the morning declining, as a professional matter, to use the word for it. I will use it now. The word is grammar. Sound organizing itself the way sound becomes language when a mind wants to be understood.

I got it the dullest way it can be got, which is by tallying. An element that recurs is a fact you can count. An element that answers another element is a fact you can count twice, once for each end of it. So I counted. I took the pulses in blocks of ten nights across the nine months and set down, for every block, how many distinct elements I could tell apart with any confidence, how many of those came again inside the same block, and how many stood in the same relation to one another every time they came.

The numbers went up. Not smoothly. There is a flat stretch through the deep of the winter that I have no account for, and a step up on either side of it. But they went up, and they went up faster in the second column than the first, which is to say the hill was not merely making more elements, it was making the same ones oftener and putting them in the same places.

I ran the block-counts twice, forward from the second month and then backward from the ninth, because a man counting toward a rising figure will help it rise. Backward, they fell exactly as they had climbed. The two columns agreed inside the tolerance of a cold hand and a poor pencil.

Somewhere in the middle of that the lumen sank the way it does at that hour, and I woke it up with my thumb, and noticed that my breath had stopped standing in the light, and understood that my face had come to the same temperature as the room. I put my hands under my arms and looked at two columns of figures on a page. The figures said what they said. There was nobody down there to say it to.

The third finding took the longest, because I did not believe my own count and ran it four times.

The hum radiates. It always has; that is what a hum is. But inside the general radiation, the record showed a directed component. Oriented. Consistent across nine months to a fraction of a degree. I worked the geometry twice on paper, there in the cold with my breath standing in the lumen light, because a bearing means nothing until you lay it against the world. The directed component went upward through the body of the hill, at a rising angle, on a line that passed through the cellar itself. Through the cellar's northeast corner.

I will set down how that was got, because a bearing is the one finding in my trade a man can talk himself into, and I do not care to have it taken on my word.

The first run gave a direction and I mistrusted it at once, on the sole ground that it was interesting. So I put it away from me. I wrote the figure on a loose leaf, folded the leaf, sat on it, and took the whole count again from the second month without looking at what I had written. The second run agreed with the first to the width of the pencil, which is not comfort; two counts by one hand in one hour are a single count with a witness. I stood and walked the room and came back and took it a third time, from the ninth month backward. Then a fourth with my left palm, which is coarser and slower than my right and has none of its habits, and the left gave a cruder figure and the same bearing.

Then the paper. Angle off the floor and angle off north, both taken from the room itself, which I had at least the luck to know exactly, Jorin having shot that floor twice and his lines being on my drawings. I struck the bearing out with the rule and the waxed string, carried the elevation up through the plane of the floor, and it met the wall high in the northeast; and I did not care for the answer, so I laid the whole construction out a second time from the other end, from the wall back down to the stone, to see whether the geometry would consent to put it anywhere else.

It would not. Four counts, two constructions, one corner.

I sat back on my heels and looked at the corner. The chest stood there where I had set it the summer before, against the wall the hill had kept driest, black wood and old metal, holding what it held.

There was a quality to the directed component that my notation has no proper mark for. I have a mark for hostile, and it was not that, not any part of it. I have a mark for inert, and it was the opposite of that. What it most resembled, in the plain mechanics of it, was a name, said softly, over and over, by something content to wait between the sayings: variation, address, and a patience built into the shape. In my notes I wrote *salutation, sustained*. I have not improved on it since.

Three findings, then, in a cold room in the dark: the hill knows my hand; the hill is building a grammar; and the hill is saying something, patiently, over and over, toward the corner where her chest stands.

And under my knees, holding all of it, the stone's long record and the grandmother's short sentence lay against each other like two halves of a broken seal. Forty years of a woman's ears: *the song is for something that is not here.* Thirty days and then fourteen months of my instruments: *salutation, sustained, on a bearing.* Two ways of counting, arriving at the one fact from two sides of a century. I set the two lines one above the other in my notes and drew no conclusion under them, because I had none that I could defend, only a direction, and a direction is not a conclusion. It is an invitation to be wrong at greater length. Two other things I noted that morning, for the honesty of the record. The old oak's long line of attention, which I checked twice, ran nowhere near my bearing; whatever the tree held itself toward, it was not my cellar, and so there were two open questions on that hill and not one. And whether the woman whose notebooks lay under my hand and the woman who had sworn the listening to Cael were one grandmother or two, I still had not asked, and did not ask now. Some questions keep better than they answer.

What I did next is what my trade does when it does not know: I extended the record. I lifted the second stone, cased it, and set a third blank flush in its place, and keyed it, and gave it no term at all. Let it listen as long as listening takes.

It is ten minutes of work with good hands and it took me the better part of half an hour. Lifting a spent blank is done with a chisel of soft brass and a great deal of patience, the stone being flush and the mortar around it fine, and a chipped edge is a chipped record. My fingers had stopped being much use to me. I got the second stone into its case on the wool and wrote the hour on the label, and the writing was the worst part of it; my hand made the figures of the date like a man practising his own name. Then the third blank out of its wrap, bedded down in the seat the second had left, the mortar mixed thin and worked in with my thumb, and the keying, which is a small quiet business done with the palm and takes exactly as long as it takes.

I gave it no term. Every blank I had ever set had a term written into it at the keying, thirty days, sixty, nine months; a term is how you keep the stone honest and how you keep yourself from going down every week to fret at it. I left the term off, and wrote *open* in the register with the date beside it, and put the register back on the shelf, and my hands ached in the particular way that means they have been cold a good deal longer than a sensible man permits.

Then I gathered my notes and my lamp and I climbed the stairs, up out of the stone cold into the ordinary cold of a spring morning an hour before dawn, and stood in my own doorway. The valley was dark below, one light burning early down at the forge, where a man who had kept a four-day count for five months was awake with his own patience. The birds had not started. The east had just begun to think about grey. Under my boots, far down, at the hour it had kept since before the village had a name, the hill rose to its pulse, and said what it had been saying all along, to whatever was not here yet, and I stood in the door with two years of mornings behind me and could no longer even pretend that I did not live on top of a word.
