# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Six: Keeping the Promise

I let a week go by after the reading, and I will tell you why.

The promise I had given Tomas was a year old by then, and it had exact wording, because between men like Tomas and me the wording is the thing: *when I know what it is, you will know.* Not when I learn something. Not when there is news. When I know what it is. And the plain fact I carried up out of that cellar on the morning of the reading was that I still did not know what it was. I knew more than any man alive about what it did. What it was stood exactly as far off as it had stood the night Tomas first said the words *two year, near enough* to his own fire.

A week is a long while to spend on a point of grammar. I spent it. I have never in my life found a form of words I could not sit down and argue with, and I sat down with that one every evening of that week and put it in the dock and asked it what it thought it had promised, and it answered me the same each time, because it was my own sentence and I had built it to be answerable. *When I know what it is.* There is no arguing with a clause you made yourself out of the flattest words there are for the express purpose of not being able to slide out of it later.

There was work enough that week and I did all of it. The shelf below the crown had men on it most days, and a line of pegs down the grass, and the first courses coming up out of the ground so square that I could put my eye along them from the top of the way and find nothing to say. I stayed off it. A builder does not want the owner of the thing standing over his first courses, and I had learned that much by then, and I took my pleasure in it from a distance and at the hours when nobody was looking. I watered flats in the glasshouse. I wrote up two runs of housings in the book and got the second column wrong twice, which for me is a symptom rather than an accident. And at the end of every one of those days the same sentence came up the slope after me and stood at my elbow while I worked, patient as a dog at a table.

So by the letter of it I owed him nothing yet, and I spent a week learning that the letter of it was no longer the truth of it. He had kept a four-day count for five months off one remark of mine. His forge light had been burning before dawn on the reading night; I had seen it from my own doorway and known exactly what it was doing lit. A man who has held a thing alone for two years, and then not alone for one more on the strength of your handshake, has standing that no wording covers. The promise had been made about knowledge. It had turned out, the way promises do if they live long enough, to be about company.

The evening it turned over in me was a Tuesday, and nothing whatever happened on it.

I had come up off the shelf at six with grit in my boots and my hands smelling of wet stone, and I had made tea and then forgotten to be a man who drinks tea, and I stood on my own doorstone with the mug going cold in my fist and watched the valley shut itself up for the night. The thorn along the top of the way was out, and going over already at the edges, and the smell of it came up in gusts when the air moved and was gone when it did not. Somebody down there was burning green stuff in a garden and making a poor job of it. The glasshouse held the west a long time after the grass had given it up.

Then the light failed off the rim, and the ground under my boots took its breath and let it out again, and I stood in it as I had stood in it on more evenings than I had troubled to add up, and did not write anything down, because that night it was not mine to measure. I had the notebooks, and what was in them I could have given you to the entry. What I did not have was one single word for what was doing it, and standing there with a cold mug in my hand I understood that I was not going to have one by waiting, and that I had somehow got into the habit of behaving as though I would.

And below me, at the top of the street, in the black shape of the forge, there was a square of light with a man in it.

He was not waiting for me. It would be a warmer story if he had been, and it would not be true. He was at his bench at seven in the evening finishing something, and the light was on because the light is always on when there is something to finish, and there was nothing in that window to reproach me with at all. It reproached me anyway. I thought about the count he had kept all winter under his hammer without one word to me about it, and about a man who lets you get all the way to his door before he asks the only question he has, and about what it costs to ask a question that small.

I went in and set the tea down untouched and got out the drawing of the collars, because I had decided a thing at last and wanted something in my hands to take down the hill with me.

I went down on a Thursday and asked him to come up the hill with me at the fall of the light.

He straightened from the bench and looked at me one long moment, and I watched him take in what the asking meant, all of it, at his usual speed, which was immediate. In two years, through every commission and delivery and quiet hour at that forge, I had never once asked Tomas up my hill. Nobody asked Tomas anywhere; he was the fixed point the village went to.

I had brought an errand with me to hold, the way a man brings a hat in to turn in his hands. The summer's collars wanted going wider by a hair, and I had the drawing folded in my coat, and I had meant to do the iron first and the asking after, and I got about as far as his doorway with that plan intact.

It was a real errand. The summer glass was coming through fatter than the winter's had, no more than a shade of it, and a shade is nothing until you go to seat it. I had drawn the collar out twice on my own bench with the disc under my thumb, and gone wider by a hair, and drawn it again to be sure the hair had not grown while I was not looking at it. Any other week I would have been pleased with that sheet of paper. That week it was a hat.

The forge was at the cool end of its day. He kept it so when the work allowed, and the work had allowed; the fire was low, the coals ticking themselves down, and the long light came in level off the street and lay along the far bench where the first of the twenty sets stood ranged in their rows waiting on their glass. A workshop in its last hour has a particular quiet in it. Nothing in the room was urgent any more. Everything in it was going to be there in the morning.

I knocked my boots off on his stone out of pure habit, there being nothing on them worth knocking off, and went in. He looked up once and went back to the file, which in that room is a welcome and is meant as one. I stood by the bench with my hands behind me and waited on the stroke to finish.

The file stopped. He set the piece down on the leather and turned it a half turn with one finger so that it lay along the edge of the bench and not across it, and looked at me.

I got the drawing out and put it down where his hand was.

"Wider," he said.

"By a hair."

He did not pick the sheet up. He put his thumb flat on it beside the line and looked at the line and then at his thumb, and there is no gauge on earth that has ever been checked against anything better.

"Whose hair," he said.

"Yours. Mine's a good deal finer and it has never once been right."

Something went across his face and did not become anything. He took the sheet up then, and held it out at the length of his arm where his eyes wanted it, and read it the way he reads a drawing, from the outside in, the seams last.

"Summer glass," he said.

"Coming through fat. Not enough to see. Enough to bind."

"Mm." He laid the sheet flat on the bench and set a hammer head on the corner of it. The sheet was his now. "In time."

"In time."

And that was the errand, all of it, done and gone in the time it takes to bank a fire, and I stood in his forge with nothing whatever left in my hands.

He waited. That is the thing about him. He did not go back to the file and he did not fill the room, he only stood there with his weight even and let the silence sit where I had put it, and the silence got large, and I let it get larger, and then I opened my mouth and made it larger still.

And I said it badly. I have gone over what I said a fair number of times since and it does not improve with going over.

"The hum comes at the last of the light," I said. "You'll know that better than I do. And the evening looks like being clear, out there." He said nothing at all. "What I came to say is this. If you've no call on the rest of your day, and if it's no trouble to you, and if the climb is more than you want after standing at a fire since noon, then you're to say so straight out and I'll think nothing whatever of it, and we'll say no more. Only I wondered whether you might care to come up and stand on the top of it while it goes."

Set down cold, it comes to this. I said that the hum came at the last of the light, which he knew better than I did. I said the evening looked like being clear, which he could see out of his own door. I said that if he had no call on the rest of his day, and if it was no trouble to him, and if the climb was more than a man wanted after standing at a fire since noon then he was to say so plainly and I would think nothing of it. Three separate ways out I built him into the one sentence, and every one of them was for my comfort and not for his, and he stood there at the bench with the file in his hand and let me get to the end of it. He wiped his hands on the leather, and banked his fire with the unhurried thoroughness of a man who does not intend to be back soon, and took his coat off the peg, and the whole of his answer was that he was beside me on the way's first flags while I was still working out what I would say if he wanted persuading.

I have skipped the street, and the street will not have it.

He went out through his own gate ahead of me and stopped at his house door and said three words in at it, and I did not hear them and had no business hearing them. The lamp I had given him at the turn of that spring was burning on the table inside, amber and steady, and it put a square of its light out onto the stones, and we walked out through the edge of it and away up the street.

I will not pretend that nobody looked. Ardenmoor had been holding opinions about me for two years and more and had never once felt the want of holding them quietly. The version in circulation that spring ran that I paid hard coin for good stone and then lay down flat in the grass beside it; that I had bought the whole of the bottoms and grew nothing whatever on the half a plough could reach; and that the trade I was in, whatever it was, was slow and expensive and had not yet set fire to anything, which the more charitable held to be a point in my favour. Half that street watched Tomas walk up out of it beside me with his coat on and his fire banked at six in the evening. By morning it would be the whole of it, and I knew that going, and so did he, and neither of us mentioned it.

We went up past the oven with its mouth open and cooling and the last of the day's heat coming off the brick of it, and past a woman taking in washing off a line, who said good evening to Tomas and then, a beat afterward, to me. That beat was the valley's entire opinion of my standing in it, delivered without a grain of malice and without the smallest idea that it had been delivered. Two children stopped what they were doing to watch us go by, not because of me, because Tomas was out of his forge with his coat on and the light still up, and children know an irregularity when they see one. Somewhere behind us at the low end Maret's wheel went on turning over, which it does whether there is anything to grind or not, and the sound of it came with us as far as the last houses and then let us go.

We went up without talking. The way took the climb the way I had built it to, and I watched him notice the work with his feet, the set of the flags, the drained elbow, and file it under things done properly, which was the filing I had built it for. The evening was clear and going gold at the rim.

The first reach out of the lane is the one I am fondest of, because it is the one I laid last and by then I knew what I was doing. It runs out along the foot of the slope with the beck on the left of it and does not begin to climb at all for forty paces, so that a man comes off the lane and finds himself already on stone and going up without ever having been asked to. Tomas came off the lane onto it and I felt him register the change under his boots at the second stride, and he did not look down, and he did not say anything, and his stride altered by about a hand's breadth and stayed altered.

The way does not go at the hill straight. It takes it in long reaches with the turns set where a man carrying something would want them, on the principle that a path you can walk with a load on is worth two you can only walk empty, which is a thing I had been told by three separate people and did not believe until I had laid it myself with my own back. Tomas went up it at one speed. He did not slow for the steep reach and he did not hurry the level, and I found myself matching him, and found also that his one speed was a little faster than my own, which I minded rather more than I have any right to admit.

There is a companionship in going up a hill beside somebody that has nothing to do with talking and is spoiled by it about half the time. What I had was his breathing, which came deeper as the ground stood up and never once came faster; and my own, which did both; and four boots on laid stone, and the grit at the turns where the wind puts it, going scritch under mine and not, so far as I could hear, under his. Behind us and below, the valley was letting off the day. A gate. A dog that had one bark in it and then thought better. The beck, which you hear on the second reach and lose on the third and get back at the crossing, and which I have never got tired of losing and getting back.

Twice I had a sentence made up and ready in my mouth, and twice I looked at the back of his coat going up in front of me and put it away again, and by the third reach I had stopped making them.

He stopped once. It was at the elbow, where the seep runs under now in a seam of its own choosing, and he had not stopped for the valley or the light. He had stopped because the flag there answers differently underfoot. He stood on it square and put his weight over twice, listening down through his boots the way he listened to everything, and waited to be told.

"There's water under that," I said. "It came across the track before there was a track. It runs its own line now and it minds its business."

"Not hollow, then," he said, and went on up.

That was the whole conversation of the climb, and I have sat through worse ones that ran an hour. The cold came up as we got height, out of the grass at the knees first, a long while before it found either of our collars. Below us the valley was pulling its ordinary sounds in for the night, a dog, a gate, somebody's last bucket, and the glasshouse down the slope caught the west and burned quietly with it for a few minutes, the way it did on clear evenings, one long pane of held fire, and Tomas looked at that a while as we climbed but said nothing, and we came up onto the crown with the light almost done.

Tomas went to the Study first, without being asked and without asking, the way a man goes straight to the one thing in a strange field that has his own work in it. He put his hand to the door and swung it out and swung it back, and then stood a moment with his head a little down, listening to a hinge he had made two years before make no sound at all on the top of a quiet hill. Then he closed it, and squared it in its frame with one finger, and came back out to me on the grass.

"Still true," he said.

"They've never once complained."

He laid his hand on the doorpost on his way past it, the once, and took it off again, and I do not believe he knew he had done it. There is no explaining that to anybody who has not made a thing with his own hands and then come upon it long afterward, doing its work, in a place he has never been. I have it myself with a lamp. You do not go up to it and admire it. You touch it in passing, once, on your way to something else, and then you leave it alone for the rest of your life.

Then he stood on my grass and looked at where he was.

It is not a large hill. I want to be honest about that, having gone on about it at length for a good part of my life. It is a green shouldered rise at the head of a valley, and there are hills within a day's ride that stand higher, and nothing about the look of it would stop a traveller on the low road. But it is the only ground in that country you can stand on and see the whole of the valley's floor at once, from the bottoms to the wood, and at that hour of a clear evening the floor lies down there in one long held piece with the beck drawn through it in silver, and the fields go the colours fields go at the end of a day, and Tomas of Ardenmoor, who had lived at the top of that street the whole of his life and made half the iron in every house of it, had never once seen his own valley from above.

He stood and looked at it a good while. He turned round once, slowly, and finished where he started.

Below us on the east the shelf lay opened, pegs and lines and the first courses going in where the hill had already dug half a storehouse for whoever wanted it. He read that over too, the whole of it, in about the time it takes to read a short letter, and put it away wherever he kept such things. Then the swifts came over, four of them, cutting the top of the hill at the height they cut it at every evening of that season, and we both watched them go and neither of us said anything about them, and the last of the gold went off the rim and left the sky that clean green it goes before it gives the day up altogether.

I took him to the middle of the grass, a little east of the Study, over the spot I knew to a stride from two years of instruments. The dusk was settling. The first stars were making up their minds.

"It comes at the last of the light," I said. "You know that better than I do. You've heard it three years through a forge floor and a village between. I thought you might care to hear it once from the top."

He did not answer that, there being nothing in it that wanted answering, and we stood.

We stood longer than I had looked to stand, because the light that evening went out of the sky with no hurry in it at all. The grass had wet in it already. I felt it come through at the ankle first and then get in under the top of my boot and settle there. Away over the north wood a single star came out and stopped pretending to flicker. Down the slope the glasshouse turned from gold to grey to a long black shine and began, very faintly, to tick, the way that house does as the heat goes out of the frame, and I found I was counting the ticks and made myself stop.

It is a curious business, waiting on a thing you have never once known to fail. I had a hundred and some nights of it written down by then, in a hand that got steadier as the years went, and on not one of those nights had the hill missed its hour. And I stood on my own grass beside a man I had brought the whole way up there on the strength of that record, and I went methodically through every reason it might not come, and invented two more that had never occurred to anybody, and got up a fine cold sweat inside a good coat about a thing that had never happened.

I will give you three of them, since I paid for them. That the sky, being unusually clear, would somehow let the thing off. That the presence of a second man on the crown, being a weight and a warmth and a stranger both, might damp it, hold it, put it off its hour. And that it had been three days since I had last stood out for it, and that in those three days it might quietly have finished whatever it had been doing all this while and gone to sleep, and I would be the man who had brought a smith up a hill at the end of his working day to feel a hill not do anything.

So I talked. That is what I do, and I did it. I told him what the instruments said about the interval, which he had not asked. I told him about the third stone under the Study keeping its own count of the nights, which was rather more than I had meant to give away and considerably less than it sounds. I told him that some evenings it came a minute behind the last of the light and some evenings three, and that I had never yet got a rule out of it that was worth the paper I ruled it on.

"There's a second one," I said, "in the dark before dawn. Same size, or as near as makes no difference. I've stood out for that one in every month there is, and I can tell you it is harder to love at four in the morning in February." He did not laugh, and it was not a joke, and I went on. "The interval between the two is not the interval between the light going and the light coming, which you would think it must be, and it is not. It runs its own count. I have had the almanac and my own book side by side on that bench for two winters trying to make one of them tell me something about the other."

Away below us somebody's door went.

"The grass up here is greener than it has any business being," I said, "and it goes over first in a hard spring and it stays over longer in a dry one. That may be the ground and nothing else. Most things are the ground and nothing else. I have written down a good many mornings on my knees in that grass to be able to tell you that I do not know, and I want you to have the size of it, because it is the size of what I have and it is smaller than the size of what you have carried."

Tomas stood with his hands at his sides and let me run down the way a clock runs down, which took some while, and then a while longer, and then I heard myself and stopped.

He stood on the crown of the hill with his hat in his two hands, which he had taken off without my noticing, the way men of his generation take a hat off in a church door out of a habit deeper than doctrine. He did not kneel or put a palm to the ground, none of the things the rest of us did, the listeners and the measurers. He only stood, square, the way he stood at his anvil, and waited without one particle of impatience, and the light went, and the hill rose.

I have felt the dusk pulse more times than I could count by then, and I can tell you that I have never felt it the way I felt it standing next to Tomas. Through the boots, first, the long slow lift, like the deck of the largest ship there ever was taking one breath; and then the underhum swelling up around it, below sound, the thing you hear with your ribs. The swifts cut past. The glasshouse let go of the last of the west. And the pulse crested, and held, and laid itself back down the way it does, gradual as a tide deciding, and I stood in the middle of it watching a smith's face and I will keep what was in his face to myself, except to say that no man should have to carry for two years, alone, at a distance, through rumor of his own madness, a thing that when you finally stand inside it is so plainly, patiently calm.

When it had gone all the way down he put his hat back on.

"Louder up here," he said.

"Yes."

"Cleaner."

"Yes."

He nodded, and was quiet a while, and the stars came on.

They came on quickly after that, three and four at a time, the top of the sky first and then the west filling in behind them. The air went from cold to properly cold in about the time it takes to notice. Neither of us moved to go, and neither of us said anything about not moving to go, and I stood there beside him with my hands getting stiff and understood that the evening had further to run yet and that it was not mine to run it.

Then he sat down.

I want that in, because in all the time I had known that man I had never seen him sit anywhere but a bench with work in front of him. He went down onto the grass the way a big man goes down, in two stages and without hurry, and put his forearms across his knees and looked out over the dark valley with his hat back on his head. I sat down beside him, there being nothing else to do that was not standing over him.

I do not think I can make that sound like what it was. He was a man who stood. He stood at his fire, and he stood in his own doorway of an evening with the bar under his arm, and in the whole of my acquaintance with him I never saw him take a chair at anybody's table, not once, though he was asked at half the doors in that valley. He came in, and he stood, and he did what he had come for, and he went. And on the top of my hill in the dark with the cold coming up out of the ground he put himself down on the wet grass like a man who means to be there a while, and settled his back, and there was no more to it than that.

The cold came up out of the ground and into us both. Somewhere below a door went, and a bucket, and after that the valley had nothing further to say for the night.

"Bigger," he said.

"Than you had it?"

"Than the sound."

Three years of hearing an edge of a thing through a stone floor with a village in between, and he had built himself a picture off that edge the way you build a horse off a hoofprint, and it had been honest and careful and far too small, and he set it down in three words and did not complain once that it had been wrong. Then he asked his question, the only one, the one he had been walking beside for three years, and he asked it the way he did everything, without preamble and straight through the middle.

"What is it?"

"I don't know," I said. "That is still the true answer, and I want you to have the true answer before I give you the rest, because the rest is going to sound like knowing, and it isn't. It's only closer."

"Closer, then."

So I told him. Standing on the crown in the dark with the first stars out, I gave the smith of Ardenmoor the true shape of it, as far as the shape went: that the hill is aware; that it always has been, since long before the village had a name to give it; that what he had been hearing through his floor for three years was not a leak or a working or any doing of mine, but the sound of something old and patient being what it is. That it had been listening back. That it had learned my hand, the way he might learn a customer's step, and that its sound was changing now the slow way a thing changes when it is trying to be understood. That it was not angry, and never had been, and that every instrument I owned and every hour I had spent flat on my face in that grass said the same one thing about its temper, which is that it has the patience of stone because it is stone, and that it wanted something, and had wanted it since before either of us was born, and that I did not yet know what.

That is a fair account of what he had off me, and it is in the order I would give it in now. It did not come out in that order or anything near it. It came out in pieces, because he took it in pieces. He stopped me where he wanted stopping and let me run on where he did not, and by the finish of it he had done a good half of the work of the telling himself, and I have thought since that this is what it is to be told a thing by somebody who is any good at hearing.

I began with the word, because the word was the load and there is no sense carrying a load round the back of the house.

"It's aware," I said.

He was quiet long enough that I heard the beck.

"Aware," he said.

"Yes."

"Of what."

"Of us. Of what stands on it and what gets done on it. Not aware as you or I are; there's no eye in it and no face, and it does not want your name and would not know what to do with it. But there is something under that grass that can tell the difference between weather and a man, and it has been telling the difference a long time."

He turned that. I could hear him doing it.

"How long," he said.

"Longer than the village. Longer than the name of the village, and the name is older than the village. I have gone at that question from four directions and every one of them comes back at me saying older, and none of them has yet come back with a number. Put it this way. There is nothing alive in that valley, and nothing standing in it either, that was there when this started."

"Not your doing, then," he said.

I had known that one was coming and I had still not made anything ready for it.

"No," I said. "Not mine. Not the lamps, not the cellar, not anything I have built or dug or set down on this hill. And I want you to know I went at that with a good deal more suspicion of myself than you would ever have brought to it, because it would have been a comfortable answer and I distrust a comfortable answer on principle. It was doing this before I came up the road. It will be doing it after I have gone off it."

"Mm."

"You had it in your floor before you ever heard my name, Tomas."

"Aye," he said, and that was all. I have never been thanked harder for anything in my life.

I gave him the listening next, and it was the piece I had least idea how to hand across, so I gave it to him in his own trade. That is what you do when you have nothing better.

"There's a step comes into your yard," I said. "You're at the fire with your back to the door. You know who it is."

"Aye."

"You couldn't tell me how. You've never sat down and worked it out. You just know before he's through the door, because you have heard that step a hundred times and it has gone in somewhere and stayed."

Nothing from him.

"It has learned mine," I said. "Not my face and not my name and not one thing about me that I would recognize as myself. My hand. The particular way I go at it. It knows my step in its yard, and it has known it for some time now, and when I go down and put my palm on the floor of that cellar the thing that comes back at me is not the same as what came back at me in my first year, and the difference is not in my palm."

He took a while over that.

"It answers you," he said.

"It says the same thing to everybody. It has only ever had the one thing to say, as far as I have ever been able to find. What has altered is the saying of it. It is going at it differently, and it has been going at it differently for a while, and each time I go down it is a shade nearer to something I could nearly put a word to." I stopped, because I was about to make it prettier than I could stand behind. "You've had a customer come in three times with a broken thing and no words for what is wrong with it. And the third time he stops talking and draws it in the dust with his boot."

Tomas thought about that a good long while. Down in the valley a light went out and another one, further along, did not.

"Angry?" he said.

That is the question. Out of everything he might have asked, standing on top of the thing that had been under his own floor for three years, he asked the one question that mattered to a man who has to go home and sleep over it.

"No," I said. "Never once. Not on the worst night I ever had out here and not on the night I did something to it I should not have done. I have got a great deal wrong about that hill and I am as certain of this as I am of my own hands: there is no anger in it anywhere. It has the patience of stone because it is stone. You could hammer on it for a hundred years and it would take the hundred years and think nothing had happened."

"And it wants something," he said.

"It wants something."

"What."

"That is the part I do not have."

He let that stand.

"It has wanted it a long time," I said. "Longer than anybody in that valley has been alive, and I would not put much money on longer than anybody in that valley has had a grandmother's grandmother. It is not waiting on an answer from me in particular. I want that plain, because it would be very easy and very warm to think otherwise and I have caught myself at it twice. Anybody who stood here long enough and asked would have got the same. I am only the first who thought to ask."

"Is it the same all over the hill," he said.

There it was, laid down flat with nothing on it, the way he laid down everything.

"It's under all of it," I said. "You get it in the beck field and you get it under your own floor and you get it here, stronger, because here is the top of it. It does not stop at my fence."

That was a true answer. It was not the answer to what he had asked. I stood on my own grass in the dark and let him have it.

He nodded slowly and looked out at the dark.

"Say the last of that again," he said. "The changing."

So I said it again, shorter, and he took it the second time with his head a little down, and that was the finish.

I kept the corner to myself, and the chest, and the bearing. That was not a withholding I was proud of, and I have turned it over many times since, and I would do it again. Those belonged to a grief before they belonged to a wonder, and a man may tell his friend the country's whole shape without handing him the deed to the one field that is not his to give.

Tomas heard the whole of it the way he read Penworth's contract, straight through, twice, nothing moving in his face. When I had finished he stood a while with his hands in his coat, looking out at the dark valley, at the little lights of it, his own forge's banked glow among them.

"Two year I thought it was the iron," he said at last. "Some fault in my own floor. Then a year I thought it was me." He said that levelly, the way a man reports weather, and it landed on me the way weather does when it has been building a long time. "Never thought to be stood on top of it with the man who'd measured it."

"You were never mad, Tomas. And you were never wrong, and as of tonight you have never once been alone with it either. Whatever it turns out to be, that part is finished."

He nodded, slow.

"Waiting for what?" he said.

"I don't know yet." The stars were all the way out now, and under the two of us, deep down, the ground kept its long silence between pulses, which I had learned was not silence at all, only patience at a scale I was still learning to count. "But I'll keep the promise as long as it keeps being true. When I know what it is, you will know. You'll have it the same hour I do."

He turned that over a good while. The cold had got properly into my hands by then and I put them in my pockets, and he did not put his anywhere.

"Do I tell?" he said.

I had not looked for that question and I ought to have. He had carried the thing alone for years on nobody's instruction, and now that there was a second man in it with him he wanted to know whose it was to give away, and he was not going to assume.

"It's yours to tell or keep," I said. "I'll not set a rule on you about your own valley. My advice, and it is only advice, is keep it a while yet. It will come out kinder later than it comes out now, and the day it goes down that street it stops being a thing and starts being a story, and a story gets improved. But that is not a condition and there is no condition."

"Aye," he said.

I have wondered since whether he had already decided before he asked, and asked anyway because asking was the courtesy. I never put it to him. There are questions you do not need the answer to, and a man who has kept one thing quiet for years without being asked to is not a man you cross-examine about his intentions.

So I sat on the top of my own hill in the dark, having given a good man the whole shape of the country and kept back the one field in it, and I did not say one word about the corner, and I knew precisely what I was doing while I did not say it.

"Aye," said Tomas, and put out his hand, and we shook on the crown of the hill in the dark, the second time the two of us had ever shaken, and then we went down the way together by lamplight, neither of us saying anything more, because everything after that would have been smaller than what we had, and he knew it, and I knew it, and the hill under us knew more than the both of us, and said, for the moment, nothing.

I lit the lamp at the top and carried it low, down at the knee, which is how you carry a light on a way you know, so that the flags come up out of the dark a stride at a time and you keep your eyes.

Going down took longer than going up. I have never been able to decide how much of that was the dark and how much was neither of us being in any hurry to arrive. He went first, since I had the light behind him and it threw his shadow out ahead of him down the flags, and twice on the reaches he put a hand out flat at his side, palm down, in the small unconscious way of a man walking a floor he does not yet know, and both times took it back. At the ford he waited on the near stone until I was over, which nobody has ever needed to do for me on those stones, and which I said nothing about.

A lamp makes a small room out of a whole hillside and puts everything outside that room a very long way off. Inside ours there was the stone going by underfoot, going darker as the dew came on; my boots; his, which fell heavier than mine and quieter; a moth that came and stayed with us the length of one reach and then had business elsewhere; and at the elbow, faint, the water talking to itself under the flag he had stood on going up. We came down over the beck on Ren's stones with the beck loud in the dark the way water is at night, and out onto the last flag, the one I had set first of all of them so that it would be the first thing anybody coming up would stand on.

The valley came up to meet us in lights. Lira's, which was the largest and the loudest even from that distance. Brenna's, small and steady and lit far later than a herbalist's window has any business being. And at the top of the street, behind the black shape of the forge, one square of amber that had come off my own bench and would not want oil again in either of our lifetimes.

We came in off the way and into the street in the dark, and there was nobody out in it to see us come back, which I was glad of and have never worked out why.

At his gate he stopped, and turned, and lifted his chin at the hill standing over the both of us. He had been putting that question to me that way since the night at his forge, without troubling to open his mouth for it, across every collar and bracket and slow morning, a small tilt of the head at the dark above the roofs. Every time before, I had shaken my head at it, small, and given him the two words.

I did not shake my head. I stood in his gateway with the lamp down at my knee and looked back up at the hill alongside him, and there was nothing left in the gesture for me to answer, because we both had it now, whatever it was.

He put his hand on the gate and went in. I walked back up alone, and the light came up in his house behind me, and the ground under my feet kept its own counsel the entire way, which by then I had very nearly stopped taking personally.
