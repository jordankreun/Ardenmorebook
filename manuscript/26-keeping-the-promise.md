# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Six: Keeping the Promise

I let a week go by after the reading, and I will tell you why.

The promise I had given Tomas was a year old by then, and it had exact wording, because between men like Tomas and me the wording is the thing: *when I know what it is, you will know.* Not when I learn something. Not when there is news. When I know what it is. And the plain fact I carried up out of that cellar on the morning of the reading was that I still did not know what it was. I knew more than any man alive about what it did. What it was stood exactly as far off as it had stood the night Tomas first said the words *two year, near enough* to his own fire.

So by the letter of it I owed him nothing yet, and I spent a week learning that the letter of it was no longer the truth of it. He had kept a four-day count for five months off one remark of mine. His forge light had been burning before dawn on the reading night; I had seen it from my own doorway and known exactly what it was doing lit. A man who has held a thing alone for two years, and then not alone for one more on the strength of your handshake, has standing that no wording covers. The promise had been made about knowledge. It had turned out, the way promises do if they live long enough, to be about company.

I went down on a Thursday and asked him to come up the hill with me at the fall of the light.

He straightened from the bench and looked at me one long moment, and I watched him take in what the asking meant, all of it, at his usual speed, which was immediate. In two years, through every commission and delivery and quiet hour at that forge, I had never once asked Tomas up my hill. Nobody asked Tomas anywhere; he was the fixed point the village went to. He wiped his hands on the leather, and banked his fire with the unhurried thoroughness of a man who does not intend to be back soon, and took his coat off the peg, and the whole of his answer was that he was beside me on the way's first flags while I was still working out what I would say if he wanted persuading.

We went up without talking. The way took the climb the way I had built it to, and I watched him notice the work with his feet, the set of the flags, the drained elbow, and file it under things done properly, which was the filing I had built it for. The evening was clear and going gold at the rim. Below us the valley was pulling its ordinary sounds in for the night, a dog, a gate, somebody's last bucket, and the glasshouse down the slope caught the west and burned quietly with it for a few minutes, the way it did on clear evenings, one long pane of held fire, and Tomas looked at that a while as we climbed but said nothing, and we came up onto the crown with the light almost done.

I took him to the middle of the grass, a little east of the Study, over the spot I knew to a stride from two years of instruments. The dusk was settling. The first stars were making up their minds.

"It comes at the last of the light," I said. "You know that better than I do. You've heard it three years through a forge floor and a village between. I thought you might care to hear it once from the top."

He stood on the crown of the hill with his hat in his two hands, which he had taken off without my noticing, the way men of his generation take a hat off in a church door out of a habit deeper than doctrine. He did not kneel or put a palm to the ground, none of the things the rest of us did, the listeners and the measurers. He only stood, square, the way he stood at his anvil, and waited without one particle of impatience, and the light went, and the hill rose.

I have felt the dusk pulse more times than I could count by then, and I can tell you that I have never felt it the way I felt it standing next to Tomas. Through the boots, first, the long slow lift, like the deck of the largest ship there ever was taking one breath; and then the underhum swelling up around it, below sound, the thing you hear with your ribs. The swifts cut past. The glasshouse let go of the last of the west. And the pulse crested, and held, and laid itself back down the way it does, gradual as a tide deciding, and I stood in the middle of it watching a smith's face and I will keep what was in his face to myself, except to say that no man should have to carry for two years, alone, at a distance, through rumor of his own madness, a thing that when you finally stand inside it is so plainly, patiently calm.

When it had gone all the way down he put his hat back on.

"Louder up here," he said.

"Yes."

"Cleaner."

"Yes."

He nodded, and was quiet a while, and the stars came on. Then he asked his question, the only one, the one he had been walking beside for three years, and he asked it the way he did everything, without preamble and straight through the middle.

"What is it?"

"I don't know," I said. "That is still the true answer, and I want you to have the true answer before I give you the rest, because the rest is going to sound like knowing, and it isn't. It's only closer."

"Closer, then."

So I told him. Standing on the crown in the dark with the first stars out, I gave the smith of Ardenmoor the true shape of it, as far as the shape went: that the hill is aware; that it always has been, since long before the village had a name to give it; that what he had been hearing through his floor for three years was not a leak or a working or any doing of mine, but the sound of something old and patient being what it is. That it had been listening back. That it had learned my hand, the way he might learn a customer's step, and that its sound was changing now the slow way a thing changes when it is trying to be understood. That it was not angry, and never had been, and that every instrument I owned and every hour I had spent flat on my face in that grass said the same one thing about its temper, which is that it has the patience of stone because it is stone, and that it wanted something, and had wanted it since before either of us was born, and that I did not yet know what.

I kept the corner to myself, and the chest, and the bearing. That was not a withholding I was proud of, and I have turned it over many times since, and I would do it again. Those belonged to a grief before they belonged to a wonder, and a man may tell his friend the country's whole shape without handing him the deed to the one field that is not his to give.

Tomas heard the whole of it the way he read Penworth's contract, straight through, twice, nothing moving in his face. When I had finished he stood a while with his hands in his coat, looking out at the dark valley, at the little lights of it, his own forge's banked glow among them.

"Two year I thought it was the iron," he said at last. "Some fault in my own floor. Then a year I thought it was me." He said that levelly, the way a man reports weather, and it landed on me the way weather does when it has been building a long time. "Never thought to be stood on top of it with the man who'd measured it."

"You were never mad, Tomas. And you were never wrong, and as of tonight you have never once been alone with it either. Whatever it turns out to be, that part is finished."

He nodded, slow.

"Waiting for what?" he said.

"I don't know yet." The stars were all the way out now, and under the two of us, deep down, the ground kept its long silence between pulses, which I had learned was not silence at all, only patience at a scale I was still learning to count. "But I'll keep the promise as long as it keeps being true. When I know what it is, you will know. You'll have it the same hour I do."

"Aye," said Tomas, and put out his hand, and we shook on the crown of the hill in the dark, the second time the two of us had ever shaken, and then we went down the way together by lamplight, neither of us saying anything more, because everything after that would have been smaller than what we had, and he knew it, and I knew it, and the hill under us knew more than the both of us, and said, for the moment, nothing.
