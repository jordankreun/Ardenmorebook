# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter One — The Hill That Waited

*(Voice test — opening ~1,600 words. First person, retrospective past, close on Opus. Kingkiller register: a storyteller aware he is telling. Reconstructed from the Notion story bible; canon-safe.)*

---

I have built a great many things in my life, and warded most of them, and set my seal on doors that will not open for anyone who does not carry my name in their blood. There is only one thing I have never once been able to lock, though I have wanted to more than I have wanted almost anything, and that is the memory of the first evening I saw the hill.

So I will begin there. A man should begin with the thing he cannot put away.

It was the third week of spring, and I had been four days on a road that did not so much end as give up — softening from honest packed dirt into a green lane that a cart used perhaps twice in a month, so that in the end I was riding through grass with the ghost of a track under it. I followed it anyway. I was going that direction, and I was young then — younger than I have been in a great many years — and even so I had already learned that roads which stop trying are usually the ones worth taking. The ones that keep insisting on themselves generally lead to somewhere that wants your custom. The ones that give up lead to places that have forgotten to expect anyone at all.

I had come to look at a hill. That was the whole of it, said plainly, and I have always preferred a thing said plainly. A hill above a village I had never seen, sold to me by a man I had never met, in a valley I could not find on any of the four maps I owned by any name any of them agreed on. I had a folded letter in my coat that answered none of the sensible questions and one I had not thought to ask, and I had ridden four days on the strength of it. That told me something about the man I had become, and I was not certain, then, that I liked it. I am more forgiving of him now. He was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road.

The hedges had come into that first reckless leaf, the kind with no caution in it, and the light lay long and yellow over a country of low green hills and older stone. Somewhere off to my left a stream went about its business without any interest in mine. I rode with the reins loose and let Snow choose the pace, which was slow, and I did not correct her. She had carried me a great many miles by then and had come to have opinions about the last of them, and I was inclined to let her keep them. It is a small courtesy, letting a good animal be right about the road. I have found it repays you.

The village arrived all at once, the way small places do. The lane turned around the shoulder of an old oak and there was Ardenmoor spread out below me in the last of the afternoon — a scatter of thatch and grey stone strung along a beck, a mill wheel turning without hurry, smoke standing straight up from a dozen chimneys into windless air. A place that had been where it was for a long time and fully intended to go on being there. I reined in at the bend and simply sat and looked, because looking was the thing I did in those days instead of most of the things a man is supposed to do. And because I was who I was, I let my attention go quiet and wide — the way you do when you stop asking a place your questions and let it answer its own.

And the hill answered me.

I want to be careful here, because this is the part people always want me to make more than it was, and it was already enough. I did not see a light. Nothing spoke. There was only, underneath the ordinary evening — under the beck and the mill and the far complaint of somebody's dog — a thread of something so low it was less a sound than a pressure. I felt it in the back teeth and in the long bones of the arm, the way you feel a cart go by two streets over. I had crossed whole countries that hummed like that and thought nothing of them. The world is full of quiet resonances, if you have the ears; ley and lode and the long patience of stone all have their notes. This was not that. This one had a shape. It was the difference between a room that is empty and a room in which someone is holding their breath, hoping you will not notice, hoping just as hard that you will.

I turned my head, and there it was: north and a little east of the village, rounded and grassed and crowned with nothing but weather. Nothing about it asked to be looked at. Everything about it, once I had looked, was very difficult to stop looking at.

*Well,* I thought, and found I had said it half aloud, so that Snow flicked one ear back at me. *There you are, then.*

I did not yet know how right the words were. I have had a long time to think about them since.

---

The man was waiting at the foot of the hill as though someone had told him the hour.

No one had told him the hour. I had sent no word ahead — had, if I am honest, half hoped to arrive, look, find the whole thing a fancy of a grieving mind, and be gone again down the giving-up road before anyone in the valley learned my name. Yet there he sat: an old man in a good plain coat on the low stone wall where the lane met the hill track, a walking stick across his knees, his face turned up the slope as if he were keeping it company and had been for some while. He rose when I came near, unhurried, and there was none of the wariness in him that a stranger on horseback usually earns in a small place.

"You'll be him, then," he said. It was not a question, and I have never learned to be easy with a man who can say a thing like that and be right.

"That depends," I said, "on who you were expecting."

"Nobody." His mouth moved toward a smile and thought the better of it. "For forty years, nobody in particular. Then a season back the hill changed its mind about that, and I thought — well." He put out a hand. "Cael. I sold you this. Some while before you agreed to buy it, if we're going to be careful about our dates."

I came down off the mare and unfolded slowly, travel-stiff, taller than he had expected and used to being so. I took the hand. The grip in it was dry and steady and entirely unafraid. "Emlyn," I said, and left it at that. My family name I had stopped giving to strangers some years before — I kept most things to myself in those years — and Cael took the half I offered him as though it were plenty, which almost no one did.

"I know what you are," he said, mild as milk. "Not the particulars. My grandmother had the particulars, and she's forty years in the churchyard, and if she were here she'd talk your ear down to a stump. I know the shape of it. A man who listens for his living." He tipped his head up the slope. "There's precious little in this valley worth listening to. And then there's that."

"You hear it," I said.

"I don't." He said it without a scrap of regret. "Never have, not once. But I've spent my whole life stood next to someone who did, and you learn the weather of a thing even when you can't feel the rain on you. She waited on you, at the last. Not on you by your name — she never had that. On somebody with the right ears, coming up that road, some year she knew she wouldn't live to see." He looked at me with a frankness that ought to have been rude and somehow managed not to be. "You came up the road. You've got the ears. You stopped at the bend and went still as a pointing dog. I've sat on this wall since noon on a hunch, and I'm far too old for hunches, and here you are."

I said nothing. Below us the beck talked to itself and the mill wheel turned, and under all of it the note went on — patient, folded, waiting — and now that I was near it I could feel a thing I would spend years learning not to doubt: that it had begun to lean. Toward me. Not toward the valley, not toward the wide evening. Toward the particular shape of the man standing at the foot of it. And that leaning sat very strangely in a chest that had spent three careful years learning to want nothing it could lose.

---

I went up the hill alone. Cael did not offer to come, which I have since understood was a kindness, and one of the first of many he did me without troubling to have them noticed.

The track was old — older than the wall, worn into the turf by feet that had climbed it for reasons no one living remembered plainly. Near the crown the wind fell off to nothing and the grass stood straight, and the note that had been in my teeth the whole while came up through the soles of my boots and settled into me like something recognized. I stood on the top of the hill in the last of the spring light with the whole valley opening beneath me — Ardenmoor small and lamplit and kind, the beck a bright thread through it, the oak woods going dark to the north — and for the first time in a great many miles I was entirely still inside.

I had not come there to build anything. I want to be clear about that, even now, telling it — because of everything that came after, everything I raised on that hill and everyone I raised it with, it would be easy to let you think I climbed up already dreaming of towers. I did not. I had come to look at a hill and then to keep moving, because moving was the arrangement I had made with my grief, and it had held for three years, and I saw no reason it should not hold for thirty more.

In my coat, over my heart, folded small, was a letter I had carried a long way and never once had to unfold, because I already knew every word of it. It had not come from anyone in that valley. It had come three weeks late, in another spring, and told me a thing I had been too far away to prevent.

I stood on the hill a long while, and did not take the letter out, and watched the lamps of Ardenmoor come on one by one below me.

Then I went down to tell Cael I would stay the night, and see the place properly by daylight, and decide.

I had, of course, already decided. I simply did not know it yet — or I knew it the way the hill knew me, which is to say, from underneath.
