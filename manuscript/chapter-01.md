# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter One — The Hill That Waited

*(Voice test — first-person retrospective, Kingkiller register. The narrator, Emlyn, tells this from the far end of an unnaturally long life; in the scene itself he is a man in his mid-thirties. His family name is withheld by design. Reconstructed from the story bible; canon-safe.)*

---

In a long life I have built a great many things, and warded most of them, and set my seal on doors that will not open for any hand not carrying my name in its blood. Of all of it, there is only one thing I have never once managed to lock away — though I have tried, and wanted to, more than I have wanted almost anything else — and that is the memory of the first evening I saw the hill.

So I will begin there. A man should begin with the thing he cannot put down.

It was the third week of spring, and I had been four days on a road that did not so much end as give up on itself — the honest packed dirt of it softening into a green lane a cart used perhaps twice in a month, until at the last I was riding through grass with only the ghost of a track beneath it. I followed it anyway. I was going that direction; and I was young then, younger than I have been in a great many years, and even so I had already learned that a road which stops trying is usually the one worth taking. The roads that keep insisting on themselves tend to lead somewhere that wants your custom. The ones that give up lead to places that have forgotten to expect anyone at all.

I had come to look at a hill. That is the whole of it, said plainly, and I have always preferred a thing said plainly. A hill above a village I had never seen, sold to me by a man I had never met, in a valley I could not find on any of the four maps I owned under any name the four of them agreed on. I carried a letter in my coat that answered none of the sensible questions and one I had not thought to ask, and I had ridden four days on the strength of it. That told me something about the man I had become, and I was not certain, in those days, that I liked him. I am gentler with him now. He was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road, and he had been tired that way for three years.

The hedges had come into that first reckless leaf, the kind with no caution left in it, and the light lay long and yellow over a country of low green hills and older stone. Somewhere off to my left a stream went about its business without the least interest in mine. I rode with the reins loose and let Snow choose the pace, which was slow, and did not correct her. She had carried me a great many miles by then and had come to hold opinions about the last few of them, and I was inclined to let her keep them. It is a small courtesy, letting a good animal be right about the road, and I have found it repays you more honestly than most.

The village arrived all at once, the way small places do. The lane bent round the shoulder of an old oak and there was Ardenmoor laid out below me in the last of the afternoon — a scatter of thatch and grey stone strung along a beck, a mill wheel turning without hurry, smoke standing straight up from a dozen chimneys into windless air. A place that had been where it was for a long time and fully meant to go on being there. I reined in at the bend and only sat, and looked, because looking was the thing I did in those years in the place of most of the things a man is meant to do. And because I was what I was, I let my attention go quiet and wide — the way you learn to, when you stop putting your own questions to a place and let it answer with its own.

And the hill answered me.

I will be careful here, because this is the part every listener wants me to make more than it was, and it was already enough. I saw no light. Nothing spoke. There was only, beneath the ordinary evening — under the beck and the mill and the far, aggrieved barking of somebody's dog — a thread of something so low it was less a sound than a pressure. I felt it in the back teeth and along the long bones of the arm, the way you feel a loaded cart pass two streets over. I had crossed whole countries that hummed like that and thought nothing of them; the world is full of quiet resonances, if you have the ears for them, and ley and lode and the deep patience of stone all keep their own low notes. This was not that. This one had a shape. It was the difference between a room that is empty and a room in which someone is holding their breath — hoping you will not notice them, and hoping, just as hard and at the very same time, that you will.

I turned my head, and there it was: north and a little east of the village, rounded and grassed and crowned with nothing but weather. Nothing about it asked to be looked at. Everything about it, once I had looked, was very difficult to leave off looking at.

*Well,* I thought, and found I had said the half of it aloud, so that Snow flicked one ear back at me. *There you are, then.*

I did not yet know how nearly right the words were. I have had a long time since to sit with them.

---

The man was waiting at the foot of the hill as though someone had told him the hour.

No one had told him the hour. I had sent no word ahead — had, if I am honest, half hoped to arrive, to look, to find the whole errand a fancy of a grieving mind, and be gone again down the giving-up road before anyone in the valley learned my name. Yet there he sat: an old man in a good plain coat on the low stone wall where the lane met the hill track, a walking stick laid across his knees, his face turned up the slope as though he were keeping it company and had been at it some while. He rose as I came near, unhurried, and there was none in him of the wariness a stranger on horseback usually earns in a small place.

"You'll be him, then," he said. It was not a question, and I have never learned to be easy around a man who can say a thing like that and turn out right.

"That depends," I said, "on who you were expecting."

"Nobody." His mouth moved toward a smile and thought the better of it. "For forty years, nobody in particular. Then a season back the hill changed its mind about that, and I thought — well." He put out his hand. "Cael. I sold you this. Some while before you ever agreed to buy it, if we mean to be careful about our dates."

I came down off the mare and unfolded slowly, travel-stiff, taller than he had braced for and long since used to being so. I took the hand. The grip in it was dry and steady and wholly unafraid.

"Emlyn," I said, and left it at that. My family name I had stopped handing to strangers some years before — I kept most things to myself in those years — and Cael took the half I offered him as though it were plenty, which almost no one ever did.

"I know what you are," he said, mild as milk. "Not the particulars. My grandmother had the particulars, and she's been forty years in the churchyard, and were she here she'd talk your ear down to a stump inside the hour. I know the shape of it. A man who listens for his living." He tipped his head up the slope. "There's precious little in this valley worth the listening. And then there's that."

"You hear it," I said.

"I don't." He said it without a grain of regret. "Never have, not once in my life. But I've spent that life stood next to someone who did, and you come to learn the weather of a thing even when the rain of it never falls on you. She waited on you, at the end. Not on you by your name — she never had that to wait on. On somebody with the right ears, coming up that road, in some year she knew well she would not live to see." He looked at me with a frankness that ought to have been rude and somehow declined to be. "You came up the road. You've got the ears. You stopped at the bend and went still as a pointing dog. I've sat on this wall since noon on the strength of a hunch, and I am far too old for hunches, and here you are."

I said nothing. Below us the beck talked to itself and the mill wheel turned, and under all of it the note went on — patient, folded, waiting — and now that I stood near it I could feel a thing I would spend years teaching myself not to doubt: that it had begun to lean. Toward me. Not toward the valley, not toward the wide grey evening. Toward the particular shape of the man standing at the foot of it. And that leaning sat very strangely in a chest that had spent three careful years learning to want nothing it could be made to lose.

"How much," I said, "for the hill."

"Paid for," said Cael. "By me, two summers gone, out of my own pocket, off a fellow who was glad to be shut of it. I'll sell it you at what it cost me and not a copper over, and throw in the wall to lean your saddle against. I'm not selling you a hill, son." His voice stayed light. His eyes were not light at all. "I'm handing on a post I've stood too long. Whether you want it is your own affair. But go up before the dark takes it, and see the thing you'd be standing at."

---

I went up the hill alone. Cael did not offer to come, which I understood only later to have been a kindness — the first of a great many he did me without ever troubling to have them noticed.

The track was old, older than the wall, worn into the turf by feet that had climbed it for reasons no one living remembered plainly. Near the crown the wind fell off to nothing and the grass stood straight, and the note that had been in my teeth the whole while came up now through the soles of my boots and settled into me like a thing recognized. I stood at the top of the hill in the last of the spring light with the valley opening beneath me — Ardenmoor small and lamplit and kind, the beck a bright thread run through it, the oak woods going dark and close to the north — and for the first time in a great many miles I was entirely still on the inside.

I had not come there to build anything. I want that understood, even now, telling it — because of everything that came after, everything I raised on that hill and every soul I raised it alongside, it would be an easy thing to let you believe I climbed up already dreaming of towers. I did not. I had come to look at a hill and then to go on moving, because moving was the arrangement I had made with my grief, and it had held for three years, and I saw no cause on earth why it should not hold for thirty more.

In my coat, folded small over my heart, was a letter I had carried a long way and never once had to open, because I knew every word already. It had not come from anyone in that valley. It had come three weeks late, in another spring than this one, and had told me a thing I had been too far away to prevent.

I stood on the hill a long while, and did not take the letter out, and watched the lamps of Ardenmoor come on one at a time below me.

Then I went down to tell Cael I would stay the night, and see the place honestly by daylight, and decide.

I had, of course, already decided. I only did not yet know it — or I knew it the way the hill knew me, which is to say, from underneath, and long before I was ready to be known.
