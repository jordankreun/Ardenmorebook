# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter One — The Hill That Waited

*(Voice test — opening ~1,500 words. Third limited, past tense, close on Opus. Reconstructed from the Notion story bible; canon-safe.)*

---

The road into Ardenmoor did not so much end as give up, softening from packed dirt to a green lane that a cart used perhaps twice a month, and Opus followed it because it was going his direction and because he had learned, over a longer life than his face admitted to, that roads which stopped trying were usually the ones worth taking.

It was the third week of spring. The hedges had come into that first reckless leaf, the kind that has no caution in it yet, and the light fell long and yellow across a country of low hills and older stone. Somewhere off to his left a stream was busy about its own affairs. Opus rode with the reins loose and let the mare choose her pace, which was slow, and did not correct her. Snow had carried him a great many miles and had opinions now about the last of them. He was inclined to let her have them.

He had come to look at a hill.

That was the whole of it, said plainly, and Opus was a man who preferred things said plainly. A hill above a village he had never seen, sold by a man he had never met, in a valley that appeared on no map he owned by any name he could find. He had a folded letter in his coat that answered none of the sensible questions and one he had not thought to ask, and he had ridden four days on the strength of it, which told him something about himself he was not certain he liked.

The village, when it arrived, arrived all at once. The lane turned around the shoulder of an oak and there was Ardenmoor laid out below him in the last of the afternoon: a scatter of thatch and grey stone along a beck, a mill wheel turning without hurry, smoke standing up straight from a dozen chimneys into windless air. A place that had been where it was for a long time and expected to go on being there. Opus reined in at the bend and sat, and looked, and — because looking was the thing he did instead of most other things — he let his attention go quiet and wide the way a practitioner's does when he stops asking a place questions and simply lets it answer.

And the hill answered.

He noticed it before he found it with his eyes. A thread of something underneath the ordinary evening — under the beck and the mill and the far-off complaint of somebody's dog — so low it was less a sound than a pressure, a held note felt in the back teeth and the long bones of the arm. He had crossed continents that hummed like this and thought nothing of it; the world was full of quiet resonances, ley and lode and the deep patience of stone. This was not that. This had a shape. It was the difference between a room that is empty and a room in which someone is holding their breath.

Opus turned his head, and there it was: the hill, north and a little east of the village, rounded and grassed and unremarkable, crowned with nothing but weather. Nothing about it asked to be looked at. Everything about it, once looked at, was difficult to look away from.

*Well,* he thought, and found he had said it half aloud, and Snow flicked one ear back at him. *There you are.*

---

The man was waiting at the foot of the hill as though he had been told the hour.

He had not been told the hour. Opus had sent no word ahead — had, if he was honest, half hoped to arrive, look, find the whole thing a fancy, and be gone again before anyone learned his name. Yet here was an old man in a good plain coat sitting on the low stone wall where the green lane met the hill track, a walking stick across his knees, his face turned up the slope as if he were keeping it company. He rose when Opus came near, unhurried, and there was in the set of his shoulders none of the wariness a stranger on horseback usually earned in a small place.

"You'll be him, then," the old man said. Not a question.

"That depends," Opus said, "on who you were expecting."

"Nobody." The old man's mouth moved toward a smile and thought better of it. "For forty years, nobody in particular. Then a season back the hill changed its mind about that, and I thought — well. Cael," he added, and put out a hand. "I sold you this. Some while before you agreed to buy it, if we're being careful about our dates."

Opus swung down. He was a tall man and unfolded slowly, travel-stiff, and he took the offered hand and found the grip in it dry and steady and unafraid. "Opus," he said, which was as much of a name as he gave anyone these days, and Cael received it as if it were sufficient, which few people did.

"I know what you are," Cael said, mild as milk. "Not the particulars. My grandmother had the particulars, and she's forty years in the churchyard, and she'd have talked your ear to a stump. I know the shape of it. A man who listens for a living." He tipped his head up the hill. "There's precious little in this valley worth listening to. And then there's that."

"You hear it."

"I don't." Cael said it without regret. "Never have. But I've spent my whole life next to someone who did, and you learn the weather of a thing even when you can't feel the rain. She waited on you, at the end. Not you by name. Somebody with the right ears, coming up that road, some year she wouldn't live to see." He looked at Opus with a frankness that ought to have been rude and somehow was not. "You came up the road. You've got the ears. You stopped at the bend and went still as a pointing dog. I've been sitting on this wall since noon on a hunch, and I'm too old for hunches, and here you are."

Opus said nothing. Down the hill the beck talked to itself and the mill wheel turned, and under it all the note went on, patient, folded, waiting — and now that he was near it he could feel that it was not, quite, only a sound. It had begun to lean. Toward him, specifically; he was almost sure of it, and the being-almost-sure sat oddly in a chest that had spent a long time now careful of what it let itself want.

"How much," he said, "for the hill."

"Paid for," Cael said. "By me, two summers back, out of my own pocket, from a fellow who was glad to be shut of it. I'll sell it you at what it cost me and not a copper over, and I'll throw in the wall to lean your saddle against. I'm not selling you a hill, son. I'm handing on a post I've stood too long." He said it lightly. His eyes were not light at all. "Whether you want the post is your own business. But you'll want to go up before dark and see what you'd be standing at."

---

Opus went up alone.

The track was old, older than the wall, worn into the turf by feet that had climbed it for reasons no one living remembered clearly. Near the top the wind fell away to nothing, and the grass stood up straight, and the note that had been in his teeth all this while came up through the soles of his boots and settled in him like something recognized. He stood on the crown of the hill in the last of the spring light with the whole valley opening below him — Ardenmoor small and lamplit and kind, the beck a bright thread, the oak woods dark to the north — and he was, for the first time in a great many miles, entirely still inside.

He had not come here to build anything. He wanted to be clear with himself about that, standing where he stood. He had come to look at a hill, and to keep moving, because moving was the arrangement he had made with his grief and it had held for three years and he saw no reason it should not hold for thirty more.

The hill hummed against the soles of his feet, unhurried, certain, glad — there was no other honest word for it — of his weight upon it at last.

In his coat, over his heart, folded small, was a letter he had carried a long way and never once had to read, because he already knew every word. It was not from anyone in this valley. It had come three weeks late, in another spring, and told him a thing he had been too far away to prevent.

Opus stood on the hill a long while, and did not take the letter out, and watched the lamps of Ardenmoor come on one by one below him.

Then he went down to tell Cael he would stay the night, and see the place properly by daylight, and decide.

He had, of course, already decided. He simply did not know it yet — or knew it the way the hill knew him, which was to say, from underneath.
