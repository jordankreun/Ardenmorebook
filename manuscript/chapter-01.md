# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter One — Ardenmoor

*(Voice test — first-person retrospective, Rothfuss register: grounded, unhurried, wry. The narrator is Emlyn, telling this from the far end of a very long life; in the scene he is a man in his mid-thirties. His family name is withheld by design, and his craft is kept almost wholly offstage. The arrival is a plain arranged errand; the hill's strangeness is deferred. Canon-safe.)*

---

I came to Ardenmoor at the far end of a long road, in the third week of spring, on a good horse who had decided some hours back that I did not know where I was going.

She was not altogether wrong. I had a letter that said where — folded twice and gone soft at the creases from a season in my coat — and a name signed at the bottom of it, and beyond those two things not a great deal else but the particular stubbornness of a man who had come to find moving easier than sitting still. I will not pretend otherwise now. People who have heard the rest of it like to go back and lend that arrival some weight it did not have at the time, some tug of fate or foreknowing, and I understand the wish. But the truth is plainer, and I have come to think it the better story: I went because a stranger had written me a kind and unhurried letter, and because it was spring, and because there was nowhere in the wide world I would rather have been — which in those days was not the boast it sounds. It was only, and rather sadly, true.

The letter had found me in a town three weeks and a good many miles behind, written in a square and careful hand, the kind a man uses when he does not write often and means every word he sets down. It said there was a hill for sale above a village called Ardenmoor; that the price was fair and the seller in no particular hurry; and that if I cared to see the place I might come on the first clear week of spring and ask at the mill for a man named Cael. That was the whole of it. I read it more times than a plain notice of land has any right to be read, and I could not have told you why, then or for a long while after. I signed my reply with the one name, as I signed everything in those years, and saddled Snow the next morning, and went.

The last of the road did not so much end as give up on itself — good packed dirt softening to a green lane a cart might trouble twice in a month, until near the end I was riding through grass with only the ghost of a track beneath it. The hedges had come into that first reckless leaf that has no caution left in it. The light lay long and yellow over a country of low hills and older stone, and a stream went about its business off to my left without the least interest in mine. I let Snow set the pace, which was slow, and did not correct her. She had carried me a great many miles and had earned her opinions of the last few, and it is a small courtesy, letting a good animal be right about the road. I have found it repays a man more honestly than most of the larger ones.

The village came into view all at once, the way small places do, as the lane bent round the shoulder of an old oak. Ardenmoor lay below me in the last of the afternoon, and I want to say plainly that it was an ordinary place — a scatter of thatch and grey stone strung along a beck, a mill wheel turning without hurry, smoke standing straight up from a dozen chimneys into windless air. Somewhere a dog was making its position known. Somewhere else a woman called two children in to supper by name, and the second name twice, with the particular patience that means it will shortly run out. I sat at the bend a while and looked at it, and felt the road go out of my shoulders, and was glad in a low and uncomplicated way simply to have arrived somewhere.

There was a hill to the north of the village and a little east of it, rounded and green and crowned with nothing but weather. I marked it the way you mark any rise of ground you have ridden a long way to look at — *that will be the one* — and thought no more of it than that. It was a hill. I had seen a thousand hills. Whatever else it might have had to say for itself, it kept the saying for later, and I have been grateful since that it did. I do not think I could have borne, that first tired evening, to be met by all of it at once.

By the time I came down among the houses the lamps were being lit. I found the mill by its wheel and its sound, and a miller with the day's flour worked into the creases of him, who looked me over once and was not surprised — a stranger on a good horse was plainly not the first thing the letter had readied the village for. He sent a boy running up the lane before I had properly finished asking, and I got down and stood and worked the ache from my legs, and had not got far with the work before the old man came down to meet me himself.

He was not the man I had built out of the letter. I had made him older and grander in my head; he was only old, in a good plain coat, with a walking stick he seemed to keep more for company than for need and a face that had done a great deal of weather in its time. He came down the lane at the same unhurried pace as his writing, and stopped a little short of me, and looked me over the way you look at a thing you have been expecting and are pleased, on the whole, to find is real.

"You'll be Emlyn," he said.

"I will," I said. "And you'll be Cael."

"Since a good while before you got here." His mouth moved toward a smile and did not quite spend it. "You made better time than I'd looked for. I half thought to see you next week, if I saw you at all. Folk say they'll come, and then the road talks them out of it somewhere past the second day." He put out his hand and I took it. The grip was dry and steady and in no more hurry than the rest of him. "Well. You've ridden a long way to look at a field with a slope to it. Come and get something hot in you first, and we'll talk the price of it by a fire, and you can walk the hill in the morning when there's light to show you the truth of the place. Nothing worth buying ever looks like much at dusk."

I told him that suited me, and was mildly surprised to find how much it did. I had spent three years arranging my life so that no one would offer me a fire and a plate and expect nothing back for it, and I had got good at it, and here was an old man doing exactly that as though it were the plainest thing in the world. I followed him up the lane and did not examine the feeling too closely, for fear of what it might turn out to be.

We ate at his table — a good plain supper, bread and a barley soup and small beer, the food of a man who cooks for himself and has stopped apologizing for it — and he talked, easily and without pressing, in the way of someone long used to his own company and glad of a change from it. He told me the price, which was fair, and told me he had bought the hill himself some years back off a fellow who had been glad to be rid of it, and did not say why he had wanted it, and I did not ask. He told me his grandmother had loved that rise of ground past all sense, had sworn to her last day that on the right sort of quiet evening you could hear it — though what you were meant to hear she would never quite say, and he said this last lightly, the way a man passes on a family foolishness he is fond of and does not believe. I said hills were strange sometimes. He said they were, and that this one was welcome to be as strange as it liked so long as it went to someone who would mind it properly, and refilled my cup, and let the matter be.

He gave me the small room under the eaves, which had a bed and a stool and a window, and wished me good night, and went heavily up to his own.

I did not sleep at once. I sat on the edge of the strange bed in the dark with my coat still on and, after a while, took from the inside of it the other letter — not the square careful one that had brought me here, but the one I had carried a great deal longer and never once had to open, because I knew every word already. It had come three weeks late, in another spring than this one, and told me a thing I had been too far from home to prevent. I did not open it now. I only held it, which is a thing I had gotten into the habit of and am not proud of, and I looked out the small window to where the hill stood as a darker shape against a dark sky, keeping whatever it kept, and I thought — with no more weight on the thought than that — *I will look at you in the morning, and then I will go.*

I was half right. I did look at it in the morning.

But I will save the hill for when the light is better, as Cael told me to, because he was right about that as he was right about a great many things, and because some places should not be walked into in the dark — not on the page and not in life. It is enough, for now, to say that I lay down at last in a stranger's house in a valley I had never meant to stay in, and slept better than I had in a long while, and did not yet know that I had already, in every way that would come to matter, arrived.
