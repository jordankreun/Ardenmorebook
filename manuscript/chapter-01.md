# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter One — Ardenmoor

*(Voice test — first-person retrospective, Rothfuss register: grounded, unhurried, wry. The narrator is Emlyn, telling this from the far end of a very long life; in the scene he is a man in his mid-thirties. The arrival is a plain arranged transaction: his man of affairs, Penworth, found the parcel and provisionally bought the hill and the land around it. His craft stays offstage and the hill's strangeness is deferred. Canon-safe.)*

---

I came to Ardenmoor at the far end of a long road, in the third week of spring, on a good horse who had decided some hours back that I did not know where I was going.

She was not altogether wrong, though the fault was not entirely mine. I knew where I was going the way a man knows a thing he has been told rather than a thing he has seen. There was a hill for sale above a village called Ardenmoor, and a fair breadth of the ground around it, and I had come to look at what I had very nearly already bought — because Penworth had told me to, and because in the matter of what I ought to want I had long since stopped trusting my own judgment over his.

Penworth was my man of affairs, and had been for more years than I mean to reckon up here. He was a verbose and formal and exceedingly precise old gentleman out of Vethmark who managed the whole apparatus of my life that I could not be troubled to manage myself — the letters, the monies, the small steady mountain of paper that any working life throws off whether a man attends to it or not. He had a particular genius, honed over thirty years, for finding me things before I knew I wanted them, and for quietly buying the ground beneath a thing while I was still deciding whether I wanted the thing at all. His letter had reached me three weeks and a good many miles behind, and I had it still, folded twice and gone soft at the creases from a season in my coat. It was a small marvel of its kind: three close pages to tell me that he had come upon a piece of country he believed would suit me — a hill of no agricultural worth and considerable quiet — that he had taken the liberty of provisionally securing it and a good deal of the land about it against my arrival, and that he would count it a personal kindness if I would go and look at what I had nearly purchased before he made me finish the purchasing.

People who have heard the rest of the story like to go back and lend that journey some weight it did not have at the time — some pull of fate, some current running in the blood and drawing me on toward the hill. I understand the wish. It makes a better shape. But the truth is plainer, and I have come to think it the kinder telling: I went because Penworth had an unerring eye and I had learned to trust it above my own; because it was spring; and because there was nowhere else in the wide world I would rather have been, which in those days was less a boast than a small and sorry fact about the size my life had shrunk to. *A hill of no worth and considerable quiet.* When I read that, it sounded like very nearly the only thing left that I wanted.

The last of the road did not so much end as give up on itself — good packed dirt softening to a green lane a cart might trouble twice in a month, until near the end I was riding through grass with only the ghost of a track beneath it. The hedges had come into that first reckless leaf that has no caution left in it. The light lay long and yellow over a country of low hills and older stone, and a stream went about its business off to my left without the least interest in mine. I let Snow set the pace, which was slow, and did not correct her. She had carried me a great many miles and had earned her opinions of the last few, and it is a small courtesy, letting a good animal be right about the road. I have found it repays a man more honestly than most of the larger courtesies do.

The village came into view all at once, the way small places do, as the lane bent round the shoulder of an old oak. Ardenmoor lay below me in the last of the afternoon, and I want to say plainly that it was an ordinary place — a scatter of thatch and grey stone strung along a beck, a mill wheel turning without hurry, smoke standing straight up from a dozen chimneys into windless air. Somewhere a dog was making its position known. Somewhere else a woman called two children in to supper by name, and the second name twice, with the particular patience that means it is very shortly going to run out. I sat at the bend a while and looked at it, and felt the road go out of my shoulders, and was glad in a low and uncomplicated way simply to have arrived somewhere.

There was a hill to the north of the village and a little east of it, rounded and green and crowned with nothing but weather. I marked it the way you mark any rise of ground you have ridden a long way to look at — *that will be the one* — and thought no more of it than that. It was a hill. I had seen a thousand hills. Whatever else it might have had to say for itself, it kept the saying for later, and I have been grateful since that it did. I do not think I could have borne, that first tired evening, to be met by all of it at once.

By the time I came down among the houses the lamps were being lit. Penworth's letter had named the mill and named the man — a fellow called Cael, who held the hill and had done the dealing with Penworth's agent through the winter — and I found the mill easily enough by its wheel and its sound. The miller had the day's flour worked into the creases of him, and he looked me over once and was not surprised; a stranger on a good horse was plainly not the first thing the winter's letters had readied the village for. He sent a boy running up the lane before I had properly finished asking, and I got down and stood and worked the ache from my legs, and had not got far with the work before the old man came down to meet me himself.

He was not the man I had built out of the correspondence. I had made him older and grander in my head; he was only old, in a good plain coat, with a walking stick he seemed to keep more for company than for need, and a face that had done a great deal of weather in its time. He came down the lane at an unhurried pace and stopped a little short of me and looked me over the way you look at a thing you have been expecting for some months and are pleased, on the whole, to find is real.

"You'll be Emlyn," he said.

"I will," I said. "And you'll be Cael."

"Since a good while before you got here." His mouth moved toward a smile and did not quite spend it. "Your man wrote me you'd come the first clear week of spring, and here's the first clear week, and here's you, near enough to the day. Thorough sort, your man. His man was thorougher. Between the pair of them I've signed my name more times this winter than in the ten years before it." He put out his hand and I took it; the grip was dry and steady and in no more hurry than the rest of him. "He said you'd want to see the place with your own eyes before you'd let him finish buying it, and I told his agent that was the first sensible thing anyone in this whole business had said to me. A man ought to look at a hill before he owns it. Come and get something hot in you, and you can walk it in the morning, when there's light to show you the truth of the place. Nothing worth buying ever looks like much at dusk."

I told him that suited me, and was mildly surprised to find how much it did. I had spent three years arranging my life so that no one would offer me a fire and a plate and expect nothing back for it, and I had got good at the arranging, and here was an old man doing exactly that as though it were the plainest thing in the world. I followed him up the lane and did not examine the feeling too closely, for fear of what it might turn out to be.

We ate at his table — a good plain supper, bread and a barley soup and small beer, the food of a man who cooks for himself and has long since stopped apologizing for it — and he talked, easily and without pressing, in the way of someone long used to his own company and glad of a change from it. He told me his grandmother had loved that rise of ground past all sense, had sworn to her last day that on the right sort of still evening you could hear it, though what a body was meant to hear she would never quite say. He said this lightly, the way a man passes on a family foolishness he is fond of and does not believe. I said hills were strange sometimes. He agreed that they were, and said this one was welcome to be as strange as it pleased so long as it went at last to someone who would mind it properly, and he refilled my cup and let the matter be.

He gave me the small room under the eaves, which had a bed and a stool and a window, and wished me a good night, and went heavily up to his own.

I did not sleep at once. I sat on the edge of the strange bed in the dark with my coat still on and, after a while, took from the inside of it the other letter — not the three-page marvel from Penworth that had carried me here, but the one I had carried a great deal longer and had never once had to open, because I knew every word of it already. It had come three weeks late, in another spring than this one, and told me a thing I had been too far from home to prevent. I did not open it now. I only held it, which was a habit I had fallen into and am not proud of, and I looked out the small window to where the hill stood as a darker shape against a dark sky, keeping whatever it kept, and I thought — with no more weight on the thought than that — *I will look at you in the morning, and then I will decide.*

I was half right. I did look at it in the morning.

But I will save the hill for when the light is better, as Cael told me to, because he was right about that as he turned out to be right about a great many things, and because some places should not be walked into in the dark — not on the page, and not in a life. It is enough, for now, to say that I lay down at last in a stranger's house, in a valley I had never meant to stay in, and slept better than I had in a long while, and did not yet know that I had already, in every way that would come to matter, arrived.
