# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Five: The Fork and the Paper

A man who has spent three hours reading a hill with his own hand has one problem he cannot get around, and it is his own hand. Every count I had taken, every finding in my notes, had come up through my palm and my training and my particular way of listening, and the first finding said the hill had been studying exactly that. An instrument cannot check itself. If the hum truly carried my signature, then I was the one tool on that hilltop guaranteed to hear myself in it, and all three findings sat under the same quiet doubt, like columns added by a clerk who owns the shop.

What my trade does with that problem is what any honest trade does: it finds a second pair of hands. And the difficulty was that the second pair of hands could not be just any hands; they wanted sensitivity, which is rare, and they wanted innocence of my notes, which ruled out the one man who had earned a look; and they were attached, the only such pair old enough to be asked within four days' road, to a boy of eighteen whose father had put him into my keeping with conditions we had never once needed to write down.

So I spent most of that week on the one part of it that was properly mine, which was the designing of the check.

A blind verification is easy to describe and very nearly impossible to do well, for the reason that the man doing it stands at both ends of the wire. I wanted the boy's senses without my expectations riding into the room on my voice, and there is no instrument made that will do that for you. There is only a list of rules, written down beforehand by a man who does not yet know what he is going to want.

So I wrote the list, at my own bench, and carried it in my coat pocket on the day. I would ask nothing that named a direction. I would not say north, east, or corner, in the room or on the stair or on the way up. I would say *tell me* and never *do you feel*, because *do you feel* is half the answer handed over free of charge. I would take his words down in his words and not tidy them into mine. And I would fix the order in advance and hold to it whatever came, the room first, the fork second, the paper last, each step giving away a little more than the one before it, so that anything I got early I got cheap.

At the foot of the list I wrote out what a null result would look like, in the plainest terms I could manage: that he would stand in that cellar and find it an ordinary cold room, and say so, and I would write *no finding* under the date and carry my three findings back up the stair with the doubt still sitting on them where it sat now. I made myself read that sentence twice. A man who has not written his own disappointment down beforehand will not recognise it when it arrives. He will negotiate with it.

I thought about it for most of a week. Then I went down to the site and asked Jorin, plainly, whether I might borrow Ren for an afternoon's work in the cellar, of my sort, not his, and Jorin drove the peg he was driving and said, "Ask him," which was both a permission and an entire philosophy of raising sons.

Ren came up on the Saturday. I had put away my notes, cased the instruments whose readings might show, and left the cellar as bare as it honestly got: the stone floor with its one flush crystal, the shelf, the lumen, and the chest in its corner, which I could no more hide than I could hide the hill, and which he had helped build a room around without ever once asking about, because he was his father's son.

I swept it, too, and caught myself sweeping it, and went on sweeping it. I have never settled whether that was hospitality or nerves. The sensitized sheets went into the under-drawer of the shelf in their case, face down and unmentioned. The fork went in my coat. The notebook was a fresh one, because a page with old figures on the back of it is a leading question with a hinge in it.

Above ground it was the best sort of day that country gives, the kind that comes in the first true week of the spring and gets spent almost entirely on people who are indoors. The thorn was out along the way. A warm wind was coming up the valley with the smell of turned ground in it, and the glasshouse was throwing the noon back down the southeast slope, and I stood on my own crown listening to a lark going up over the north wood, thinking about how I meant to spend a fine Saturday afternoon taking a boy down a hole in the ground to ask him careful questions.

I told him the truth, in the shape I could afford: that the hill had a sound in it, which he knew; that I had been measuring the sound a long while; that my measurements had got to the place where the measurer himself was in the way, and I needed senses that were not mine, and his were the best I knew of. I told him he could say no, and that no would cost him nothing with me, now or ever.

He thought about it the way he thought about a cut list.

"You want me to tell you what the room does," he said. "Without you telling me first what it ought to do."

"That's it exactly."

"Aye," he said, and rubbed his palms down his trousers, which was the whole of his ceremony, and went down the stair ahead of me.

The cold met him four treads down, as it meets everybody, and he stopped on the fifth with his hand flat on the wall.

"Forgot how it holds it."

"It holds it all summer."

"Aye. That's the mortar and this ground together. Three days, that lot went off in; you'd not get that in the bottoms in a fortnight." He came down the last of the treads still touching the stone, and stood on the floor and looked up into the vaulting the way a man looks at a face he has not seen in a while. "It's stood well."

"It has."

"It'd want a sump, if ever the hill changed its mind about water." He said it absently, on the road to something else, which was how the boy paid a room a compliment.

I will give what happened down there in the order it happened, because the order is the finding.

He stood in the middle of the floor first, the way he stood in any new-framed room, not looking at anything in particular, taking the whole of it in through whatever door it is that boy takes rooms in through. The lumen burned steady on the shelf. I stood on the stair with my mouth shut and let him have the silence, and it stretched a good long while, and I watched his weight shift, small and slow, the way a man's weight shifts on a boat he has not noticed is turning.

"Level's true," he said at last, to himself as much as to me. "We shot it twice, I remember. Floor's true. Walls are true." A pause, and then, in the mild, personally affronted tone of a craftsman whose numbers will not reconcile: "It doesn't stand true."

"Say more."

"Can't." He frowned at the northeast corner, and it was a frown I knew from his father over a drawing, not a boy's unease but a builder's, the specific irritation of a discrepancy that refuses to show its working. "A room takes its weight down. Every room. Walls to footing, footing to ground; you feel it in your feet, everything in a room agreeing about which way down is." He tipped his chin at the corner, at the black chest sitting quiet against the dry wall. "That corner doesn't agree. It takes from below. Like a post under load, only the load's the wrong way up." He worked his jaw, dissatisfied, and produced, in the end, the most honest sentence of the whole afternoon. "If a wall did that, I'd take it down and lay it again. But there's nothing done wrong. I'd stake my square on it. It's built true and it stands other."

I wrote it down exactly as he said it, standing on the stair with the notebook on my knee, and the scratch of the pencil brought his head round.

"You're writing it."

"Every word, and in your words, not mine. Put into my words it stops being evidence and starts being my opinion of you."

He took that in, and I watched him decide it was fair, and then I watched him do the thing I had not looked for and have been glad of since: he got more exact. Not warier. More exact, the way a man reads a measure twice when he knows it is going on somebody's cut list.

"Then write *other*," he said. "Not *wrong*. I'll not have wrong. I've been through that wall in my head twice since you asked me down here and there's nothing wrong in it."

"What's the difference, to you?"

"Wrong's a fault, and a fault's got a where. You put your finger on it. You take the course out and lay it again." He set his hand flat and high on the wall, the way you test a stud. "Other's got no where. It's the whole room agreeing about something that isn't the ground."

I wrote *other, no where, whole room*, and the hour beside it, and did not ask him to explain it further, because he had plainly given me the whole of what he had and it is bad manners and worse method to press a man for the sentence you would rather he had said.

He turned to me then, and his body did the thing I had been watching for since we came down, the thing he had been doing in small ways the whole time without one sign of knowing it: he turned to face me, and his feet did not finish the turn. Shoulders to me, feet a half-point off, toward the corner, the way iron filings stand a half-point off true on a bench with a magnet in the drawer.

"During the build," I said. "Your father says you felt something. He says you never said what."

The frown deepened a degree. "Felt like the walls were hanging," Ren said. "The whole time. Off nothing. I kept my count and said nothing, because walls don't hang; they bear. I helped cut that floor. It's hill to the middle of the world under that stone." He looked at the corner again. "Still feels hung."

I took out the fork.

It is a plain thing to look at, two tines of drawn steel on a stem, tuned, over a patient month some years ago, to the hill's own baseline, the deep note under the deep note. Struck in an ordinary room it gives an ordinary tone and dies. Struck over the hill it does not die; the ground takes the note up, agrees with it, answers it, the way a great bell will hum back a voice that finds its pitch. It was the first instrument I ever made in that valley, and the least clever, and the one I trust the way you trust an old dog.

I struck it once and set the stem to the stone.

The hill answered. Ren's chin came up at the feel of it, the long agreeing note coming back under our boots out of the ground, and I watched him sort it, place it, accept it, a fact about the site like the water table.

He asked about it, naturally. He had watched a made thing come out of my coat and go to work, and there is no version of that boy who does not want to know how it was got.

"You made it?"

"My first year here. A stem of drawn steel I brought up with me, and a month to tune it. You strike, and you listen, and you take off a filing the width of a hair, and you strike again, and you are wrong, and you do the same tomorrow evening. A month of evenings."

"To get it onto the hill's note."

"To get it near enough that the hill will trouble itself to answer."

"That's a scribe line," he said, pleased, and I told him I did not know the term. "You scribe a board to the wall it goes against. The wall's not straight. Nothing's straight. You don't make the wall true, you make the board fit it." He nodded at the fork in my hand. "You scribed it to the hill."

"That is exactly what I did, and I have been half a lifetime in this trade and had no word for it until this minute."

Then I struck it in three places, the middle of the floor, two paces west, and hard against the south wall, and asked him each time only what came back, and each time he told me in about the same words that it came back everywhere, underfoot, and even. Three plain questions and three plain answers, all of them useless, and that is what a control is for.

Then I struck it again, and this time I said, quiet, "Tell me where the answer stands."

He was still for the better part of a minute, eyes shut, doing with the whole of his body what I do with a trained palm, and the affronted frown came back, deeper than it had been all day.

"That's wrong too," he said. "It comes back everywhere; underfoot, that's right, that's the hill. But it comes back sooner there." His hand rose and pointed, without his eyes opening, without one degree of doubt, at the northeast corner. "Sooner and cleaner. Like the answer lives closer, that way." His eyes opened. "Which is wrong, because that way is a wall, and then it's just hill."

"And then it's just hill," I agreed.

Then I did the thing my own list said I would do, and it was the least pleasant ten minutes of the afternoon.

I asked him to keep his eyes shut, and I took him by the shoulders and turned him about, twice one way and once the other, the way children turn one another at a game, until I was reasonably satisfied he had lost the room. I walked him two paces off from where he had been standing and set him down there. And I struck the fork, and asked him again, in the same words I had used before, to tell me where the answer stood.

He pointed. Not at the place his arm had pointed the first time, which would have proved only that a boy remembers his own arm. He pointed at the corner, from new ground, with his eyes shut, at very near the same bearing off his own shoulder.

I did it a third time from the foot of the stair. He was slower about that one, and got there.

By then the colour had gone out of him. He was not frightened; he was tired the way a man is tired at the end of a day of fine measuring, when the whole of the work has been attention and none of it muscle, and there was a faint quiver in his hands. I sat him down on the second tread with his back against the wall and went up and brought down bread and cold meat and a cup of small beer, and we ate in that cellar with the lumen amber on the shelf and said very little for a quarter of an hour, because he wanted the quarter of an hour, and because I was not going to take a signature off a tired hand.

The last thing I did was the paper. I keep sensitized sheets for the trade, close-made paper that takes a person's arcane signature the way plaster takes a key: you lay a palm flat, hold still, and the paper keeps the shape of what you are. Every signature I have ever taken prints the same general way, a spreading from the palm outward to the sheet's edge, brighter or fainter with the strength of the talent, patterned with its particular grain. Mine spreads like frost on glass. Soren's, though I have never taken it and never will unasked, would spread, I would wager the hill, like sound rings on water.

Ren laid his hand to the sheet for the slow count of thirty, careful as a man setting a pane, and I lifted it and turned it to the lumen, and stood looking at it a good deal longer than the reading of a signature wants.

It had printed strong, stronger than I had guessed. And it had not spread. It ran. From the palm-shape the pattern went in branching lines all one direction, across the sheet and clean off its edge, dividing and dividing and reaching, every last line of it, the way roots go through a bank toward water they have already found. I have taken a fair number of signatures. I had never seen one with a direction in it.

So I did not trust it, and I told him I did not trust it, and I took a second sheet.

The taking is a fussier business than it looks. The sheet wants to lie flat on hard cold stone with nothing whatever beneath it; a fold or a crumb of grit under the paper will print as faithfully as a hand will. It wants the palm dry, the wrist unsupported, and thirty seconds of a man not fidgeting, which is a longer thirty seconds than anybody expects. I had him give me the left hand this time instead of the right, and I turned the sheet a quarter-turn on the floor before he laid it down, so that if the paper or my own way of setting it out were putting the run into the thing, the run would come up turned along with it.

It did not come up turned. It ran as the first had run, a shade fainter for the left hand, dividing as it went, off the edge and gone.

Whether it ran toward anything I did not ask the paper, and I want that plainly down. A sheet lying on a cellar floor is not a survey, and I have watched better men than me take a bearing off a thing that was never built to give one. What I had was a repeat: one subject, two sheets, one hand each, the same afternoon. It ran, and it did not spread. I laid the two of them side by side under the lumen and wrote that sentence into the notebook in the flattest words I owned, and put no second sentence under it.

He looked at it with me. Being Ren, what he said was, "It's run past the edge," in the tone of a man noting a board cut short, and being eighteen, what he said next, after a silence, was, "Is it wrong?"

"No," I said, and I made certain he heard the level in it. "It is not wrong. It is strong, and it is yours, and I have not seen its like, and I don't know yet what it means. That is the plain truth, and you'll always have the plain truth from me in this room, so I'll say it again. I don't know yet what it means.

He looked at the two sheets a while longer.

"Has Da got one?"

"Every soul has one. Your father's would print, and it would be his, and it would spread the way very nearly all of them spread."

"And it'd not be strong."

"No."

He took that with nothing at all in his face, and I have thought about that silence a good many times since, because there is no comfortable place for a boy to put the news that he has more of a thing than his father has, least of all a boy who measures the world in his father's units.

"Does the strong do anything?" he said at last. "Is it for something."

"It can be. It's a capacity, like a good ear or a straight eye, and a capacity is not a trade until a man makes it one. What it's for is a thing you decide, and decide late, and I'll not have you deciding it at eighteen in my cellar on the strength of two sheets of paper."

"That's fair."

"It's more than fair, it's the whole of my thinking on the subject, and I'll say the other part once more because it weighs more than any of it. I don't know yet what it means."

He nodded, slowly, taking that the way he took a load figure, and I told him the other thing, the thing I had been turning over since before he ever came down the stair.

I told him that a talent, seen early, gets reached for; that there are hands in the world that take up a gifted child as a man takes up a dropped tool, and do not ask the child much about it; that I had my reasons, old ones, paid for, to want no letter written and no visit paid on his account; and that until he was older and the choosing could be all his, I wanted what had happened in this cellar to stay between the two of us and the stone. I did not dress it as a lesson or a secret society. I said it as you say a thing to a man you respect, and he heard it that way.

"Da knows I felt something," he said. Scrupulous, even now.

"Your father knows, and that's right, and I'll never ask you to keep a thing from your father. The shape of it, though, the paper, the fork, the corner, that stays here. For now. Not forever. There's a difference, and I'll be the one answering for it."

"Aye," said Ren, and put out his hand, and we shook on it over the sensitized sheet, and that was the second oath ever sworn in that cellar, if you count the one the hill had been saying nightly at the corner this past year, which I had begun to.

Soren came up the way at dusk, as he did most days, and did not knock, as he never did, and when I came out he was already going back down the flags, a small dark shape against the last light, and on my doorstone, under a pebble the size of an egg, lay a paper of his own.

It was a drawing, done in the hard-pressed pencil of a boy who draws the way other boys throw stones, for the accuracy and not the art. It showed my hill. It showed the Study on the crown, and the glasshouse gleaming down the southeast slope in a scribble of light, and the way going down in its neat stitched line, and the storehouse on the east shelf standing finished, which it was not, roofed and doored, which it had neither. And rising off the crown where the Study stood, drawn over and around the little room I lived in like a thing remembered rather than invented, a tower. Round, and tall, and windowed, with a light in the top of it.

Under the whole of it, in the careful letters of a boy who had lately conquered them, was written: **the hill's good place.**

I stood on my doorstone in the failing light with the paper in both hands, and down in the valley the first lamps were coming on, and under my feet, patient as ever, the ground went on saying its one long word toward the corner of my cellar, and I found I had been holding the drawing the way you hold something that has been handed to you to keep.
