# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-Five: The Fork and the Paper

I came up that stair with three findings under my arm and one difficulty, and the difficulty was the hand I had taken them with. Every count in my notes had come up through my own palm, my own training, my own way of listening, and the first finding said the hill had been studying exactly that. An instrument cannot check itself. If the hum carried my signature, I was the one tool on that hilltop certain to hear myself in it, and all three findings sat under the same doubt, like columns added by a clerk who owns the shop.

What my trade does with that is what any honest trade does: it finds a second pair of hands. The trouble was that they could not be just any hands. They wanted sensitivity, which is rare, and they wanted to know nothing of my notes, and that ruled out the one man who had earned a look at them. The only such pair within four days' road belonged to a boy of eighteen whose father had lent him to my hill on terms neither of us had ever needed to write down.

So I spent most of that week on the one part of it that was properly mine, which was the designing of the check.

It went on at the bench in the Study, in the evenings, when the ordinary day was used up and there was nothing left that wanted hands. I would clear the end of the bench, which I do when I am putting off beginning, and set the candle where it would not throw my own arm across the paper, and sit down in front of a sheet with a pencil and no notion whatever of the first line.

The first three drafts were bad, and all three were bad in the same direction. They were clever. I wrote myself instructions about tone. I wrote a note about where I ought to stand and how far off. On the second evening I produced a paragraph of some elegance on how to put a question so that it opened a door without pointing through it, and I read that paragraph over on the third evening and saw that every line of it was me arranging to be understood, and being understood was the one thing in the world I had no right to want in that cellar. The three drafts went into the fire. I began again with rules that said only what I was not to do, and gave no reasons for themselves.

A blind check is easy to describe and very nearly impossible to do well, because the man doing it stands at both ends of the wire. I wanted the boy's senses without my own expectations riding in on my voice, and there is no instrument made that will do that for you. There is only a list of rules, written down beforehand by a man who does not yet know what he is going to want.

So I wrote the list, at my own bench, and carried it in my coat pocket on the day. I would ask nothing that named a direction. I would not say north, east, or corner, in the room or on the stair or on the way up. I would say *tell me* and never *do you feel*, because *do you feel* is half the answer handed over free of charge. I would take his words down in his words and not tidy them into mine. And I would fix the order in advance and hold to it whatever came, the room first, the fork second, the paper last, each step giving away a little more than the one before it, so that anything I got early I got cheap.

Two rules I struck out again, and the striking is as much the design as anything I kept.

The first was a rule about how long to wait before putting a question a second time. I struck it because it was my own impatience wearing a procedure's coat.

The second I kept three evenings before I saw what was in it. I had written that if an answer came back empty I would ask again in different words, to be thorough. It reads as thoroughness. It is a hint with its hat off. Any second wording I could have written would have carried, somewhere down in its grammar, the answer I was hoping for, and I would never afterwards have been able to tell which of us put it there. I ruled it through twice, hard, so that I could not read it under the line on the day, and wrote in its place that I would ask each thing once, in the words already written down, and take back whatever came, including nothing.

The order took the rest of the week, and I did not begin with the order I ended with. I began with the fork first, because the fork was the interesting part. It took me two evenings to admit that I had put the loudest instrument at the front of my own check to please myself, and that anything said about the room afterwards would have had the note of it running underneath. So the fork went second and the room went first, and the room got its silence. I set the reason down in the margin beside the line, so that when the afternoon came and I began to want to hurry, the argument would already be on the paper and I would have nothing to do but read it.

At the foot of the list I wrote out what nothing would look like, in the barest words I could manage: that he would stand in that cellar and find it an ordinary cold room, and say so, and I would write *no finding* under the date and carry my three findings back up the stair with the doubt still sitting on them where it sat now. I made myself read that sentence twice. A man who has not written his own disappointment down beforehand will negotiate with it when it comes.

I thought about it for most of a week. Then I went down to the site and asked Jorin, straight out, whether I might borrow Ren for an afternoon's work in the cellar, of my sort, not his, and Jorin drove the peg he was driving and said, "Ask him."

He did not look up to do it. He was setting out a line along the shelf with a mallet and a bag of pegs, going up the line on his knees, and he sighted, and drove, and answered, and took the next peg out of his teeth, and not one of the four got in the way of the others. I had come down that hill with three or four sentences ready about the boy having his own say, and about a father's rights in the matter, and about how the afternoon would go and what would not be asked in it. I used none of them. I stood there on the shelf with all of them still in me and understood that I had been handed far more than the loan of a Saturday, and that saying so out loud would have made it smaller. So I said I was obliged to him. He said nothing at all to that, and I went back up.

I made the fair copy of the list that evening and read it through against the drafts to be certain the tidying had not lost me anything, and then the drafts went into the fire as well, because a discarded rule left about on a bench is a rule a tired man finds again on the morning and thinks better of. The copy went into the inside pocket of my coat, folded once, and stayed there. Twice on the Friday I took it out for no reason I could give afterwards, and read it, and put it back, and by the second time its corners had already gone soft.

Half a sheet, a dozen lines, the foot of it crossed and crossed again, nothing on it a stranger would look at twice, and nearly all its work done by what it forbade me.

Ren came up on the Saturday. I had put away my notes, cased the instruments whose readings might show, and left the cellar as bare as it honestly got: the stone floor with its one flush crystal, the shelf, the lumen, and the chest in its corner. I could not hide the chest any more than I could hide the hill. He had helped build the room around it and had never once asked, being his father's son.

I went down at eight in the morning to do it, and that was five hours earlier than the work wanted. The working book went up to the Study and into a drawer, and the register after it, and the loose leaves of the reading, and the sheet I had struck the bearing out on, which I did not so much put away as carry up the stair at arm's length. The blanket came up too. A folded blanket on a cellar floor is a sentence about how long somebody has been kneeling there and in what direction, and I was not going to pay that boy the compliment of his own senses and then hand him half the answer in the furniture.

What I could not take out I let alone, and I let it alone on purpose. The roots stayed along the west wall and the apples on their rack and the cheese under its cloth, because a stripped room is a room that has been stripped, and he had eyes in his head.

I swept it, too, and caught myself sweeping it, and went on sweeping it. The sensitized sheets went into the under-drawer of the shelf in their case, face down and unmentioned. The fork went in my coat. The notebook was a fresh one, because a page with old figures on the back of it is a leading question with a hinge in it.

The broom was an afterthought and then it was not. That floor is dressed stone laid close, and it had been swept at the back of the winter, and there was nothing on it worth a broom's attention beyond a little grit off my own boots and the fine pale dust that comes off mortar for years after. I swept it to the middle and took it up on a board and carried the board up the stair and out and shook it off downwind of the door. Then I went back down and swept the floor again, corner to corner, in courses, and the second sweep took longer than the first for the single reason that there was nothing to find and I went slowly to be sure of it.

I did the floor round the chest and did not move the chest, and got the head of the broom in under the front of it as far as it would honestly go, and then stopped, and stood up straight, and looked at what I was at.

I swept it a third time at about half past ten.

Above ground it was the best sort of day that country gives, the kind that comes in the first true week of the spring. The thorn was out along the way. A warm wind was coming up the valley with the smell of turned ground in it, and the glasshouse was throwing the noon back down the southeast slope, and I stood on my own crown and listened to a lark going up over the north wood. Before the afternoon was out I would be eleven steps under it with a notebook.

He came up a little before one, in his working clothes, having been in his father's yard all morning and meaning to be back in it on Monday, and he came up at Jorin's pace, a pace that does not alter for a slope and does not alter for a level either. I went out and met him at the top of the last flags rather than wait for him at my own door. We stood there a while and got his wind back, looking down the stitched line of the way at the flags he had just come up, and I did not begin until he was ready to be begun at.

I told him the truth, in the shape I could afford: that the hill had a sound in it, which he knew; that I had been measuring the sound a long while; that my measurements had got to the place where the measurer himself was in the way, and I needed senses that were not mine, and his were the best I knew of. I told him he could say no, and that no would cost him nothing with me, now or ever.

He took his time over it.

"You want me to tell you what the room does," he said. "Without you telling me first what it ought to do."

"That's it exactly."

"Aye," he said, and rubbed his palms down his trousers, which was the whole of his ceremony, and went down the stair ahead of me.

The cold met him four treads down, as it meets everybody, and he stopped on the fifth with his hand flat on the wall.

"Forgot how it holds it."

"It holds it all summer."

"Aye. That's the mortar and this ground together. Three days, that lot went off in; you'd not get that in the bottoms in a fortnight." He came down the last of the treads still touching the stone, and stood on the floor and looked a long time up into the vaulting. "It's stood well."

"It has."

"It'd want a sump, if ever the hill changed its mind about water." He said it on the road to something else, which was how the boy paid a room a compliment.

I kept to the order I had written down, and the order is the finding.

He stood in the middle of the floor first, as he stood in any new-framed room, not looking at anything in particular, taking the whole of it in through whatever door it is that boy takes rooms in through. The lumen burned steady on the shelf. I stood on the stair with my mouth shut and let him have the silence. It ran on a long time. I watched his weight go over onto one foot and come back, small and slow, and go over again.

"Level's true," he said at last, to himself as much as to me. "We shot it twice, I remember. Floor's true. Walls are true." A pause, and then, in the tone of a craftsman whose numbers will not come out: "It doesn't stand true."

"Say more."

"Can't." He frowned at the northeast corner. It was a frown I knew from his father over a drawing: not a boy in a strange room but a builder over a figure that will not show its working. "A room takes its weight down. Every room. Walls to footing, footing to ground; you feel it in your feet, everything in a room agreeing about which way down is." He tipped his chin at the corner, at the black chest sitting quiet against the dry wall. "That corner doesn't agree. It takes from below. Like a post under load, only the load's the wrong way up." He worked his jaw a moment. Then he gave me the most honest sentence of the whole afternoon. "If a wall did that, I'd take it down and lay it again. But there's nothing done wrong. I'd stake my square on it. It's built true and it stands other."

I wrote it down exactly as he said it, standing on the stair with the notebook on my knee, and the scratch of the pencil brought his head round.

"You're writing it."

"Every word, and in your words, not mine. Put into my words it stops being evidence and starts being my opinion of you."

He took that in, and I watched him decide it was fair, and then I watched him do the thing I had not looked for and have been glad of since: he got more exact. Not warier. More exact, the way a man reads a measure twice when he knows it is going on somebody's cut list.

"Then write *other*," he said. "Not *wrong*. I'll not have wrong. I've been through that wall in my head twice since you asked me down here and there's nothing wrong in it."

"What's the difference, to you?"

"Wrong's a fault, and a fault's got a where. You put your finger on it. You take the course out and lay it again." He set his hand flat and high on the wall. "Other's got no where. It's the whole room agreeing about something that isn't the ground."

I wrote *other, no where, whole room*, and the hour beside it, and did not ask him to explain it further. He had given me the whole of what he had, and I was not going to press him toward a better sentence.

He turned to me then, and his body did the thing I had been watching for since we came down, and had been doing in small ways all afternoon without one sign of knowing it. He turned to face me and his feet did not finish the turn. Shoulders to me. Feet a half-point off, toward the corner.

"During the build," I said. "Your father says you felt something. He says you never said what."

The frown deepened a degree. "Felt like the walls were hanging," Ren said. "The whole time. Off nothing. I kept my count and said nothing, because walls don't hang; they bear. I helped cut that floor. It's hill to the middle of the world under that stone." He looked at the corner again. "Still feels hung."

I took out the fork.

It is a plain thing to look at, two tines of drawn steel on a stem, tuned, over a patient month some years ago, to the hill's own baseline, the deep note under the deep note. Struck in an ordinary room it gives an ordinary tone and dies. Struck over the hill it does not die. The ground takes the note up, and agrees with it, and answers it. It was the first instrument I ever made in that valley, and the least clever, and the one I trust the way you trust an old dog.

I struck it once and set the stem to the stone.

The hill answered. Ren's chin came up at the feel of it, the long agreeing note coming back under our boots out of the ground, and I watched him sort it, place it, accept it, a fact about the site like the water table.

He asked about it. He had watched a made thing come out of my coat and go to work, and there is no version of that boy who does not want to know how it was got.

"You made it?"

"My first year here. A stem of drawn steel I brought up with me, and a month to tune it. You strike, and you listen, and you take off a filing the width of a hair, and you strike again, and you are wrong, and you do the same tomorrow evening. A month of evenings."

"To get it onto the hill's note."

"To get it near enough that the hill will trouble itself to answer."

"That's a scribe line," he said, and I told him I did not know the term. "You scribe a board to the wall it goes against. The wall's not straight. Nothing's straight. You don't make the wall true, you make the board fit it." He nodded at the fork in my hand. "You scribed it to the hill."

"That is exactly what I did, and I have been half a lifetime in this trade and had no word for it until this minute."

Then I struck it in three places, the middle of the floor, two paces west, and hard against the south wall, and asked him each time only what came back, and each time he told me in about the same words that it came back everywhere, underfoot, and even. Three plain questions and three plain answers, all of them useless, and useless was what they were for.

Then I struck it again, and this time I said, quiet, "Tell me where the answer stands."

He was still for the better part of a minute, eyes shut, doing with the whole of his body what I do with a trained palm, and the frown came back, deeper than it had been all day.

"That's wrong too," he said. "It comes back everywhere; underfoot, that's right, that's the hill. But it comes back sooner there." His hand rose and pointed, without his eyes opening, without one degree of doubt, at the northeast corner. "Sooner and cleaner. Like the answer lives closer, that way." His eyes opened. "Which is wrong, because that way is a wall, and then it's just hill."

"And then it's just hill," I agreed.

Then I did the thing my own list said I would do, and it was the least pleasant ten minutes of the afternoon.

I asked him to keep his eyes shut, and I took him by the shoulders and turned him about, twice one way and once the other, the way children turn one another at a game, until I was reasonably satisfied he had lost the room. I walked him two paces off from where he had been standing and set him down there. And I struck the fork, and asked him again, in the same words I had used before, to tell me where the answer stood.

Taking a grown lad by the shoulders and turning him blind in a cold room is a liberty. It was a liberty I had asked for in the open air beforehand and been granted in the open air beforehand, and it was a liberty still. The words came off the paper in my pocket, at the speed I had set them at, and holding to that speed cost me more than the turning did, because everything in me wanted to say them warmer the second time, and warmer is a thumb on the scale.

He pointed. Not at the place his arm had pointed the first time, which would have proved only that a boy remembers his own arm. He pointed at the corner, from new ground, with his eyes shut, at very near the same bearing off his own shoulder.

I did it a third time from the foot of the stair. He was slower about that one, and got there.

By then the colour had gone out of him, and there was a faint quiver in his hands. It was the tiredness of a day of fine measuring, when the whole of the work has been attention and none of it muscle. I sat him down on the second tread with his back against the wall and went up and brought down bread and cold meat and a cup of small beer, and we ate in that cellar with the lumen amber on the shelf and said very little, because he wanted the time, and because I was not going to take a signature off a tired hand.

He ate the bread first and the meat after, in that order, and slowly. Over our heads the afternoon was still going on, the lark and the warm wind and the thorn out along the way, and none of it came down eleven steps. I sat on the bottom tread with my own cup and did not fill the quiet.

The last thing I did was the paper. I keep sensitized sheets for the trade, close-made paper that takes a person's arcane signature the way plaster takes a key: you lay a palm flat, hold still, and the paper keeps the shape of what you are. Every signature I have ever taken prints the same general way, a spreading from the palm outward to the sheet's edge, brighter or fainter with the strength of the talent, patterned with its particular grain. Mine spreads like frost on glass. Soren's I have never taken and never will unasked. It would spread like rings going out on water.

Ren laid his hand to the sheet for the slow count of thirty, careful as a man setting a pane, and I lifted it and turned it to the lumen, and stood looking at it a good deal longer than the reading of a signature wants.

It had printed strong, stronger than I had guessed. And it had not spread. It ran. From the palm-shape the pattern went in branching lines all one direction, across the sheet and clean off its edge, dividing and dividing and reaching, every last line of it, the way roots go through a bank toward water they have already found. I have taken a fair number of signatures. I had never seen one with a direction in it.

So I did not trust it, and I told him I did not trust it, and I took a second sheet.

The taking is a fussier business than it looks. The sheet wants to lie flat on hard cold stone with nothing whatever beneath it; a fold or a crumb of grit under the paper will print as faithfully as a hand will. It wants the palm dry, the wrist unsupported, and thirty seconds of a man not fidgeting, and thirty seconds is longer than anybody expects. I had him give me the left hand this time instead of the right, and I turned the sheet a quarter-turn on the floor before he laid it down, so that if the paper or my own way of setting it out were putting the run into the thing, the run would come up turned along with it.

So I went over that patch of floor with my sleeve first, and then with the flat of my hand, twice, feeling for anything at all, and turned up a crumb of mortar no bigger than a seed and got rid of it, and then went over the same patch a third time, because one crumb is an argument for a second. I laid the sheet down and did not touch it again once it was down. And I counted the thirty aloud and slow, because thirty counted inside a man's head comes out at about twenty-two, and I have been caught by that before.

Then I stood off the sheet with my hands behind me and looked at the wall. Nothing in the trade forbids watching. But a boy holding still for thirty seconds under an interested eye is holding still differently, and I had spent a week of evenings getting as much of myself out of that afternoon as I knew how to get out of it. So I looked at the wall, and counted, and left the paper and the boy the business to themselves.

It did not come up turned. It ran as the first had run, a shade fainter for the left hand, dividing as it went, off the edge and gone.

Whether it ran toward anything I did not ask the paper. A sheet lying on a cellar floor is not a survey, and better men than me have taken a bearing off a thing that was never built to give one. What I had was a repeat: two sheets, a hand each, the same afternoon. It ran, and it did not spread. I laid the two of them side by side under the lumen and wrote that sentence into the notebook in the flattest words I owned, and put no second sentence under it.

He looked at it with me. Being Ren, what he said was, "It's run past the edge," in the tone of a man noting a board cut short, and being eighteen, what he said next, after a silence, was, "Is it wrong?"

"No," I said, and I made certain he heard the level in it. "It is not wrong. It is strong, and it is yours, and I have not seen its like, and I don't know yet what it means. That is the plain truth, and you'll always have the plain truth from me in this room, so I'll say it again. I don't know yet what it means."

He looked at the two sheets a while longer.

"Has Da got one?"

"Every soul has one. Your father's would print, and it would be his, and it would spread the way very nearly all of them spread."

"And it'd not be strong."

"No."

He took that with nothing at all in his face, and I have thought about that silence a good many times since. He measures the world in his father's units.

"Does the strong do anything?" he said at last. "Is it for something."

"It can be. It's a capacity, like a good ear or a straight eye, and a capacity is not a trade until a man makes it one. What it's for is a thing you decide, and decide late, and I'll not have you deciding it at eighteen in my cellar on the strength of two sheets of paper."

"That's fair."

"It's more than fair, it's the whole of my thinking on the subject, and I'll say the other part once more because it weighs more than any of it. I don't know yet what it means."

He nodded slowly, and I told him the other thing, the thing I had been turning over since before he ever came down the stair.

I told him that a talent, seen early, gets reached for; that grown men settle among themselves what a gifted child is for, and are a long way into the settling before anyone thinks to ask the child; that I had my reasons, old ones, paid for, to want no letter written and no visit paid on his account; and that until he was older and the choosing could be all his, I wanted what had happened in this cellar to stay between the two of us and the stone. I did not dress it as a lesson or a secret society. I said it as you say a thing to a man you respect, and he heard it that way.

"Da knows I felt something," he said. Scrupulous, even now.

"Your father knows, and that's right, and I'll never ask you to keep a thing from your father. The shape of it, though, the paper, the fork, the corner, that stays here. For now. Not forever. There's a difference, and I'll be the one answering for it."

"Aye," said Ren, and put out his hand, and we shook on it over the sensitized sheet, and that was the second oath ever sworn in that cellar, if you count the one the hill had been saying nightly at the corner this past year, which I had begun to.

Soren came up the way at dusk, as he did most days, and did not knock, as he never did, and when I came out he was already going back down the flags, a small dark shape against the last light, and on my doorstone, under a pebble the size of an egg, lay a paper of his own.

The pebble had been chosen. Flat on its underside, heavy for its size, off the beck by the colour of it, and set square in the middle of the sheet so that a wind coming up from any quarter would have to argue with all of it at once. I lifted the stone off and put it in my coat pocket without thinking about it at all, and turned the sheet round the right way up in the last of the light.

It was a drawing, done in the hard-pressed pencil of a boy drawing for the accuracy and not the art. It showed my hill. It showed the Study on the crown, and the glasshouse gleaming down the southeast slope in a scribble of light, and the way going down in its neat stitched line, and the storehouse on the east shelf standing finished, which it was not, roofed and doored, which it had neither. And rising off the crown where the Study stood, drawn over it and around it like a thing remembered rather than invented, a tower. Round, and tall, and windowed, with a light in the top of it.

He had drawn it hard. The lead went silver where he bore down, and the sheet had a valley in it you could feel from the back with a thumb, and the lines he was surest of had been gone over twice and three times: the four corners of the Study, the ridge of the glasshouse, the stitches of the way told off one at a time down the slope. He had not counted them right. He had taken great care to get them even.

What was not on it I noticed after. No cabin, though I slept in it and he had gone past its door most days for two years. No barn. No paddock rail. No thorn, and the thorn was the best thing on that hill that week. The north wood got three scribbles and no hesitation in any of them. The beck got one line and the line was correct. The storehouse had taken the same weight of pencil as the Study had, and so had its roof, and so had its door.

At the foot of the sheet the pressure came off. He had gone slowly there, and small, and level, and left himself a clear space above the line before he began it.

Under the whole of it, in the careful letters of a boy who had lately conquered them, was written: **the hill's good place.**

I stood on my doorstone in the failing light with the paper in both hands, and down in the valley the first lamps were coming on, and under my feet, patient as ever, the ground went on saying its one long word toward the corner of my cellar, and I found I had been holding the drawing the way you hold something that has been handed to you to keep.
