# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Fifteen: Steel and Glass

The greenhouse came up the frontier road in pieces, on four wagons, and it was the first thing I built on that hill that the valley could not pretend was ordinary.

A stone house they understood. A stone house is only a cave a man has troubled to square, and every family in Ardenmoor had raised a wall or watched one raised, and the Study, for all it stood on a hill that had thrown off two lifetimes of walls, was in the end a house, and houses they knew. But the greenhouse came as a frame of wrought steel, black and slender and strange, forged in the city to my drawings and warded in the forging against rust and rot and the particular hungry damp of a glasshouse, so that it would stand a hundred years and never weep a red tear down its joints. It came up the road on four wagons behind six straining horses, long black members longer than any tree in the valley grew straight, and the whole village found some reason to be on the road the day it passed, the way they had found reasons to come up and watch the Study not fall.

Steel does not come cheap to a frontier. I want to be honest about what that decision was, because it is easy, at this distance, to tell it as vision, and at the time it was a good deal more like a man putting his last coin on a horse he liked the look of.

I had made one payment. I had made it by scraping, and the scrapings were gone: the lamp money was steady but small, and the city money was spent, and Penworth had sat in my one good chair and told me in plain words that the land had to carry the next one. The prudent course was obvious and I could have recited it. Sit still. Spend nothing. Put every copper by against the spring, get the Wrays' crop in and sold, and let the hill earn its keep a year before asking it for anything else.

Instead I wrote to Penworth in the last week of summer and told him I meant to spend the better part of a year's margin on wrought steel and glass.

His reply came up the road a fortnight later and I have it still. *I note that you propose to build a glasshouse. I note also that you have one payment behind you and no reserve whatever, and that the frontier is not celebrated for its glaziers. You will do as you think best; you always have, and it has served you better than my advice has on at least two memorable occasions. I have therefore confined myself to arranging the shipping, which will cost you nine per cent less than the quotation you were undoubtedly about to accept. Do not write to me about the weather. Write to me when the crop is in.*

That is the whole of Penworth's approval, and it is the most any man ever got from him, and I read it twice and then went and ordered the steel.

I had thought it through the way I thought through everything that year, which was slowly, and against the money. And I had come to a thing I believed: that a hill which answered a lamp would answer a garden, and that a garden under glass, warmed and warded and made to hold a season the valley could not, was the one thing I could build that would earn from the ground itself and not from my own two hands at the bench. Lamps were the winter's bread. The greenhouse was meant to be the years' bread, and Brenna's as much as mine, and that was a thing we settled between us before ever a pane of glass came near it.

It was still a gamble. If the frame had come off the wagons bent, or the glass had come through broken, or the first winter had cracked the lot, I would have had nothing to meet the spring with but a payment I could not make and a very expensive black skeleton on a hill. I knew that going in. I have never been sure whether what I did that autumn was faith or only a man too tired of being careful to be careful any longer, and I have noticed that from the outside the two are indistinguishable, and that only one of them is ever written up afterward.

She came up to plan the soil while the frame was still going up, and stood in the black skeleton of it with the sky showing through where the glass would go, and turned all the way round once, slowly, taking the measure of it against whatever private standard she kept, and said, "Well. You've built a cage for weather."

"I've built a place to keep a season alive out of its season."

"That's what I said." But she said it with the corner of her mouth, and then she went to work, because Brenna at work was Brenna most herself, and we spent the better part of that afternoon on our knees in the steel with a stick of charcoal and a board, drawing beds. I know soil the way a builder knows it, which is to say I know what it will bear and how it drains. She knew it the way her grandmother had known the hill, from the inside, from a lifetime of asking things of it and being answered, and I had the sense that afternoon, not for the first time and not for the last, of being the junior partner in a trade I had thought was mine.

We laid it all out between us, the warm beds and the propagation frames and the long benches, the place for the things that paid and the place for the things that healed, her trade and my craft learning to share a roof. And when we came to the north end of the house, the shaded end, where the hill's own shoulder would keep the low winter sun off it and hold it cool and dim the year round, she stopped, and looked at it a while, and did not draw anything there.

"Leave the north corner," she said.

"For what?"

"For later." She stood up off her knees with the small private grimace she gave her own joints, and looked at the cool shaded end of the glasshouse, and I waited for the rest of it, and the rest of it did not come.

"Brenna."

"Mm."

"It's a quarter of the house." I put the charcoal down. "I've eleven feet of the best-warded ground in this valley standing empty on your say-so and no notion what for. I'm not asking to be difficult. I'm asking because if it wants a cold bed I have to run the ducting round it now, and if it wants damp I have to set the drain now, and I cannot do either of those things for a thing you won't name."

She considered that with her whole attention, the way she considered everything, and I could see her weighing what she owed me against something else, and I could see the something else win.

"Cool," she said. "Shaded. Dry, not damp. No ducting; it's not to be warmed." She met my eye without a flicker of apology. "And no, I'll not tell you. Not because you've not earned it. Because it's not mine to hand over on a Tuesday because a man wants his drawings finished, and if I say it out loud before I'm sure I'll have said it, and it can't be unsaid, and I've been the only one keeping it for thirty years." A pause. "Ask me again at the turn of the year and I may have a different answer for you. Ask me again this afternoon and you'll get this one, louder."

"Cool, shaded, dry," I said. "No ducting."

"That's the whole of it."

"It's not," I said, "but it's enough to build with," and she said, "Aye, it is," with the corner of her mouth, and we got back on our knees and drew.

So I left it. We drew the rest of the beds around the empty north corner and left it bare on the board, and I ran the ducting the long way round to spare it, at a cost of some four days and a quantity of swearing, and I found I did not mind. I have never once been sorry I let a careful person come to a thing by their own road, and I did not start being sorry that autumn. There is a particular comfort in a partner who will hold a line with you standing right on top of it. You know precisely where you are with such a person, which is more than can be said for the agreeable ones.

The glass came a fortnight later and took three weeks to hang, and I will not give you the whole of it pane by pane, though I could. What I will tell you is that on the evening we set the last light in, Brenna and Ren and I standing back in the blue dusk to look at it, the greenhouse caught the last of the day and the first of the rising lumen-glow off the crown together, and stood there full of held light on a hill that grew nothing, and it was, I thought, the most hopeful thing I had made in that place or any other. A glass house is an act of faith. You build it against every frost that is surely coming, and you fill it with the tender things, and you say to the winter, plainly and in advance: not these. Not this year. I had not said that to a winter in a long time.

It was that same autumn that Essa's bedframe came, and it is a smaller thing than a glasshouse, and I set it here beside the greenhouse on purpose, because the two of them were the same act done in two sizes, and only one of them was easy.

I had lived a year and more in the cabin on a rope bed and a straw tick, which is no hardship to a man who has slept in a tent and worse, and I had told myself I kept it so out of thrift, the money being always tight. That was true, and it was not the whole truth. The whole truth was that a proper bed is a thing you build for a life you mean to live and go on living, and I had not been sure, that first year, that I was building any such thing, and a rope bed asks nothing of you and promises nothing back. But the greenhouse had unsettled something in me, some held breath I had not known I was holding. A man who builds a glasshouse against the winters plainly means to be there for them. So I went down to Essa's shop off the square, where the good smell of cut oak and hide glue lived, and I ordered a bed built, a real one, and a chest of drawers to go with it, from a woman who made furniture the way Tomas made iron, more of herself into it than the thing strictly required.

She brought the bed up herself on a borrowed cart when it was done, and she did not come alone, and that is the part of this I did not expect.

Ren came, to carry, having been volunteered by his father in the way fathers volunteer sons. And the cart had rather more on it than a bed. There was a pair of good wool blankets from Lira, sent up with no message at all, which is precisely how Lira sends a thing. There was a bolster from Mira, Cael's daughter, whom I had spoken perhaps forty words to in a year and a half. There was a jar of something from Brenna with instructions tied to it in her hand. There was even, wrapped in sacking, a small mirror on a stand that Kellard had "had by him," and which I later learned to a certainty he had not had by him at all but had ordered up from Wainford in the spring on a whim and never sold.

I stood in my own yard and looked at all this and understood, with the peculiar mixture of gratitude and mortification that is the whole experience of being cared for by people who have not asked your permission, that the village had been discussing my housekeeping. Not this month. For a year. Somewhere below me, in the inn and over the oven and across Kellard's yard, the collective judgment had been quietly forming that the wizard on the hill, who had built a stone room and a timber house and a barn and now a palace of glass, was sleeping on a rope bed under one blanket like a man expecting to be moved on, and that it would not do.

"Don't," Essa said, watching my face. "It's not charity, it's opinion. You'll find there's a deal more of the second in this valley than the first."

We got the bed in and set it up in the small square bedchamber that had held, until that day, a rope bed and a chest of clothes and nothing else, and Essa looked round the bare room while I settled the frame, and said, in the blunt kind way of a woman honest to the grain of a thing, "It's a monk's cell in here. You've a whole good house and you live in it like you're waiting to be told to leave." She was not wrong, and I did not tell her she was. Ren, who had said nothing whatever for half an hour, said, "It's a good bed, though," which was the sort of remark that ended discussions in that family, and Essa said it was, because she had made it, and set the last peg and gathered her tools and went, and took the boy down with her, and left me alone with a bed built for a life and a room that had not yet decided to be one.

I made it up with Lira's blankets. I set Kellard's mirror on the chest, where I would have to see myself in it every morning, which I had contrived to avoid for years. I did not manage the mirror at first. I turned it to the wall the first night and stood it round again the second, and that is the whole of that particular battle, and I record it because it was harder than the greenhouse.

I made the bed up. And that is nearly the whole of the grief in this chapter, that a man made a bed, except for the last of it, which is this. Setting the room to rights, I went to the desk to clear it, and reached to put something away in the lower right drawer, the sticking one, the one that held the two unread pages of the river house now and had held, since before that, since I first set the desk up, a thing I did not take out. A pair of dimensional callipers, fine ones, the finest I ever owned, in a fitted case worn soft at the corners. I need not tell you here how they came to me or whose they had been the making of; that is another book, and not this one, and I was not ready that autumn to open either the drawer or the account. I put my hand to it out of habit and it stuck, the way it always stuck, catching on the swollen wood or on whatever it caught on that I had never troubled to plane down. And I stood there a moment with my hand on the pull, and I could have freed it. It was a stuck drawer and I am a man who has opened harder things. I let it stay stuck. I straightened the rest of the room around it, the new bed and the good chest and the bare clean walls, and I left the one drawer shut, and I went out to see to the lamps. Down the shoulder the greenhouse stood in the last of the light, full of glass and faith against the coming frost, and it was a good thing to have built, and I was glad of it. It was enough, that autumn, to have built the bed. The drawer could keep. The hill was patient, and so, I was learning, slowly, and late, and against my own grain, could I be.
