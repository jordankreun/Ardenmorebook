# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-One: Letters Going Both Ways

*What follows I could not have known that spring. I had it from her much later, in pieces, and I give it here in her order, not mine.*

The inn emptied late, for a spring night. Lira stood at the door while the last of them went, took the goodnights as they were offered and returned them at the same weight, and slid the bar across, and stood a moment in the quiet the way a woman stands in a room she has just got back.

The fire was banked. The girl had done the floors before she went, and done them properly, which had taken two years of teaching and was now worth the two years. The cups were on their shelf. Lira walked the room once, because a room will lie to you from behind the bar, straightened a bench that had wandered, pinched a candle that had no business still burning, and took the day's box to the small table in the snug where the light was good enough and nobody could see her from the lane.

Then she did the books.

There were two of them. The first was the inn's, tall and broad-backed and handsome, bought by her father in a year when the inn could not afford it, which was his whole way of doing business and the reason she had inherited an inn worth having and no money in it. Into this she entered the day: so many dinners, so much ale, the room over the stable let to a wool factor for two nights, a debt of Kellard's brought current, three marks and four to the good against the week's costs. She wrote a fair hand, quick and square, and she did not linger over the work, because it was the kind of work that goes wrong when it is lingered over.

The second book was small, and plain, and lived under the first one, and was not a book of money.

She did not write in it every night. She wrote in it when there was something worth the ink, which in Ardenmoor was not often, and that was precisely as she preferred it. Into the small book, over the years, had gone the weather of the valley: who had married, who had died, what the quarry paid, whether the harvest came in, the small lawful history of a small lawful place. And into it, these two years and more, had gone the wizard.

She turned back through the pages sometimes, on nights like this one, before she wrote. It was a discipline, like counting a till you already trust. The entries ran spare. *Bought the hill. Paid on the day.* That was the first, two springs gone. Then, in their seasons: *Built on the crown. Wall stood.* And: *A lamp that does not burn. Gave the first to the herb-woman, no charge that I can find out.* And: *Winter. Sells light now. The valley likes him better than it says.* And, from last summer, on a line by itself, in a hand very slightly smaller than the hand around it, as though the ink itself had been asked to keep its voice down: *The boy.*

She sat with that page a while, on this particular night, and she did not add anything to it.

What she added, on a fresh line, was this: *Spring. He has laid a stone way down the hill. Alone, by hand, of an evening, the whole of the season. It comes to the foot of the lane and stops there. He asked nobody for anything.* She looked at the line. After a moment she added, because the book was no use to her if it was not exact: *It is a better piece of work than the lane.*

Then she got out the letter paper.

The address she did not have to look up. She had not had to look it up for a long time, and there had been a season of her life, long finished, when the not-having-to had felt like standing in a draught, and that season was of no use to anybody now and she did not visit it. Under the inn's tall ledger, flat at the bottom of the drawer, lay a thin packet of old letters tied in a bootlace, which she likewise did not visit. She took out a fresh sheet, and she wrote the salutation the office preferred, which managed to be both correct and warmless, a trick she had privately admired for years, and she made her quarterly report.

She was good at it. That is a thing worth saying plainly, because she was good at it in the particular way of a woman doing well a job she wished did not exist. The office wanted to know the temper of the far valleys: what moved on the roads, what the quarries and the mills were worth this year, whether anything unusual required anyone's attention. *Nothing unusual requires anyone's attention* was a sentence she had written so many times, in so many seasons, that her hand could make it while her mind stood entirely elsewhere, and it was the most important sentence in every letter she sent, and she built each report around it the way you build a wall around a well.

She wrote the wool prices. She wrote that the road past Wainford was no worse than any spring. She wrote that the quarry had let two men go and taken on a boy, that the mill's wheel had been mended, that the harvest intentions were ordinary. She wrote, because it would have been remarkable to omit him and remarkable was the one thing he must never be, a paragraph on the wizard: that he continued in the lamp trade, in a small commercial way; that he paid his accounts on the day; that he had put up a glass forcing-house and sold early salads, which the neighborhood found more comic than wonderful; that he had lately improved the footpath on his own ground at his own cost. Tradesman's items, every one. A man made entirely out of invoices. She read the paragraph back and was satisfied with it the way a joiner is satisfied with a hidden joint.

Then she sat, with the pen off the paper, for rather a long time.

There was a fact she had been carrying since midsummer past. The whole valley had carried it, in the way a valley carries a thing, which is to say everyone had heard about the well, and everyone had agreed without one word being exchanged that there was nothing to have heard. A bucket had slipped. A woman had caught her boy back from an edge. Such things happen in market squares, and the man who happened to be crossing the square at the time had done nothing that forty witnesses could name, and the boy in question was in any case the old man's grandson and none of the office's affair, and none of hers.

She wrote the line anyway, once, to see it. *There was an incident at the well at midsummer involving the smith's scales and the miller's boy.* It was not even accurate. She had made it inaccurate with some care, the way you offer a coat that will not fit, and it still sat on the page like a lamp in a window. She looked at it and thought, not for the first time, that the whole craft of her position lay in one distinction: the office read what she wrote, but somewhere a person she had never met read what she chose not to write, and of the two documents the second was by a distance the more informative, and only she would ever hold it.

She took a fresh sheet and copied the letter over without the line, in her quick square hand, and burned the first sheet in the banked fire, standing over it with the tongs until it was ash and the ash was broken, which was not dramatics but housekeeping; she had a horror of half-burned paper the way other women have a horror of mice.

The mail came through twice a week now, which the valley owed to the wizard's parcels, not that the valley said so. Wenn the carrier left the bag on the settle by the door and took what she had for him and whatever was warm in a cloth besides, and Thursday morning early she sorted the bag on the bar, before the room filled: the factor's letters, a seed catalogue for the Wrays that would be the talk of their kitchen for a month, something legal for Kellard, the usual paper for the hill.

She sorted the hill's post the way she sorted everyone's, which was a thing she would have said out loud to any face in the valley, and it was true, and like her letters it was built to be true. But her hands knew the weights and papers of his correspondence by now the way they knew her own cups, the thick laid stock from Vethmark that was the lawyer, the mean thin blue that was the freight agents, and so her hands knew before she did when they came, that Thursday, to something they had not held before: cream, heavy, a hand like a fence of black iron, and on the back, in wax the colour of a dried plum, a seal she had not seen pressed fresh in a great many years.

She stood quite still for a moment, in her empty morning room, holding another person's letter.

The seal was the office's. Not the plain daily stamp her own answers came franked with, on the rare occasions anything answered at all. The old proper seal, the tower and the open book, the one that meant the letter had been written upstairs, by somebody whose time was worth something, to be read and kept. She knew its weight in a hand. There had been one under the ledger, in the bootlace packet, for a good many years.

She did not hold it to the light. She would want that understood, if it were ever asked, and it never would be: she did not tilt it, nor weigh it past what sorting required, nor stand there one breath longer than the sorting took. She set it in the middle of the hill's little stack, under the lawyer and over the freight papers, square with both, exactly where the sorting would have set it, and she went and stood behind her bar and looked at the small stack sitting there in the plain grey light, addressed in iron to a man who was at that moment, if the morning was like other mornings, on his knees in a glass house a mile up and over, with dirt on his hands, talking to lettuces.

The room would fill in an hour. The boy would come for the hill's bread and take the post up with it, at a run now the whole way, on the new stones.

Lira took down her good glasses, which no one had asked for, and polished one, and held it to the window, and set it back, and after that she did the thing she did on the mornings her own weather wanted managing, which was work. But twice before the room filled she came back down the bar to where the little stack sat, and squared it, though it had not needed squaring, and the second time she stood by it a moment with her hand flat on the bar beside it, the way you stand at a gate looking at weather coming over a hill that is going to arrive whether you have mentioned it to anyone or not.

*Letters going both ways now,* she wrote in the small book that night, and entered the day's take in the tall one, and banked the fire, and went up.
