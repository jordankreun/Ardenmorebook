# The Tower of Ardenmoor

## Book One

### Chapter Twenty-One: Letters Going Both Ways

*What follows I could not have known that spring. I had it from her much later, in pieces, and I give it here in her order, not mine.*

The inn emptied late, for a spring night. Lira stood at the door while the last of them went, took the goodnights as they were offered and returned them at the same weight, and slid the bar across, and stood a moment in the quiet the way a woman stands in a room she has just got back.

At that hour the room belonged to nobody, and she took a minute of it before she began. The heat had gone out of the far end an hour since. The day's smell had settled into its parts: ale under the boards, woodsmoke, wet wool where men had steamed themselves dry by the fire and gone home to their beds. She put the bolt across the yard door and tried it with her shoulder, out of habit and not out of any doubt about the bolt. The shutters on the beck side had swelled with the spring and wanted lifting an inch before they would come, and she lifted and set them, first the one and then the other, and the room went quieter by just the amount of water it had been letting in all evening. After that the loudest thing in the house was the fire, and the fire was nearly out.

The fire was banked. The girl had done the floors before she went, and done them right, which had taken two years of teaching and was now worth the two years. The cups were on their shelf. Lira walked the room once, because a room will lie to you from behind the bar, straightened a bench that had wandered, pinched a candle that had no business still burning, and took the day's box to the small table in the snug where the light was good enough and nobody could see her from the lane.

The snug held one table, one bench, a shelf with nothing on it, and a window set too high in the wall to be of use to anybody passing; her father had done the night's books at that same table for thirty years and had chosen the room for the same reasons and never once given them. She set the candle at her left hand. She turned the box out on the boards and counted it, copper first, into little towers of ten, then the silver laid in a row and all turned one face up, because money that lies tidy can be counted twice at speed and give the same answer both times. It came out where she had expected within two copper, and the two copper were under the rim of the box. Then she lifted the drawer key off its hook.

Then she did the books.

There were two of them. The first was the inn's, tall and broad-backed and handsome, bought by her father in a year when the inn could not afford it, which was his whole way of doing business and the reason she had inherited an inn worth having and no money in it. Into this she entered the day: so many dinners, so much ale, the room over the stable let to a wool factor for two nights, a debt of Kellard's brought current, three marks and four to the good against the week's costs. She wrote a fair hand, quick and square, and she did not linger over the work, because it was the kind of work that goes wrong when it is lingered over.

The tall book was an honest book and was built to be read by strangers. That was the point of it. Any man with a claim on the inn or an interest in it could have been sat down at that table with a candle and left alone for an evening, and would have found the ale and the beds and the stabling and the oats and the tallow all where they belonged, ruled off at the quarter-day and totalled twice, the second total in a different ink to prove the two halves of the year had been added by a woman in two different moods and agreed anyway. She kept the front twenty pages of it in her father's hand and had never been tempted to copy them out fair. His figures leaned. Hers stood up. Between the two of them the inn had gone on being an inn through a bad decade and a worse one, and the book said so without saying anything.

The second book was small, and plain, and lived under the first one, and was not a book of money.

It had been a limp thing when she got it and the years had not improved it. The spine had gone soft, the corners were rubbed round, the ink of the early pages had browned toward the colour of weak tea while the later pages still ran black, and anyone turning it over on a shelf would have set it down again as somebody's aunt's stock book. She ruled her own faint lines in it with the back of a knife and wrote small.

She did not write in it every night. She wrote in it when there was something worth the ink, which in Ardenmoor was not often, and that was precisely as she preferred it. Into the small book, over the years, had gone the weather of the valley: who had married, who had died, what the quarry paid, whether the harvest came in, the small lawful history of a small lawful place. And into it, these two years and more, had gone the wizard.

The older reaches of the book would have bored a stranger to tears, and she had never written a line of it for a stranger. Whole years ran to four entries. *Wet spring. Beck over the ford stones twice. Hay in late and light.* *Quarry took nobody on.* *A dry summer. Wells down the street gave out and the square's did not.* *Frost at the back end of March and the street out all night with straw round the pumps.* She gave a birth the width of one line and a death the width of one line, having settled early that the book was not to be permitted opinions about which of the two was the larger; and where a family came through a bad year she wrote *a bad year* and left it there, on the reasoning that anybody who wanted the detail had it already and anybody who did not had no business in the book. She was nowhere in it herself. She had gone looking once, years back, to be sure of that, and had found her own name twice, both times set down flat as she would have set down any other keeper of any other house, and had been satisfied.

She turned back through the pages sometimes, on nights like this one, before she wrote. It was a discipline, like counting a till you already trust. The entries ran spare. *Bought the hill. Paid on the day.* That was the first, two springs gone. Then, in their seasons: *Built on the crown. Wall stood.* And: *A lamp that does not burn. Gave the first to the herb-woman, no charge that I can find out.* And: *Bread goes up that hill every morning of the world and nobody arranged it.* And: *Winter. Sells light now. The valley likes him better than it says.* And, from the spring after that: *Timber on the shoulder. A house and a barn, and both of them small.* And: *The mill planed his boards. What was charged for it is not known in this house.* And: *The Wray place has a roof on it again.* And, from last summer, on a line by itself, in a hand very slightly smaller than the hand around it, as though the ink itself had been asked to keep its voice down: *The boy.*

She sat with that page a while, on this particular night, and she did not add anything to it.

Then she turned forward again, through the autumn and the winter and out the other side of them, past entries she could have said off by heart in the dark. *Harvest. Nine days. He was in the bottoms for all nine of them and got the hang of it about the fourth.* *Walked his own rents. Eleven days, on foot, no cart and nobody with him.* *Land changed hands the back end of the autumn. Five holdings. Every one of them stood up out of it.* *Ferris tells the room he got the better of a wizard. Twice a week since October.* *Steel up the hill on eleven wagons and the track no wider than it ever was.* *Builder's men on the hill through a frost that stopped every other job in the valley, and paid of a Saturday.* *Ten fleeces went up out of Pardon's loft and no money came down.* *Glass hung in the cold. Nobody here has seen the like and nobody will say so.* *Green stuff down the street before anyone's peas were in, and sold before ten.* Then the last written line, and under it the ruled feint of the next one, and nothing on it at all.

What she added, on a fresh line, was this: *Spring. He has laid a stone way down the hill. Alone, by hand, of an evening, the whole of the season. It comes to the foot of the lane and stops there. He asked nobody for anything.* She looked at the line. After a moment she added, because the book was no use to her if it was not exact: *It is a better piece of work than the lane.*

She read it over once and left it to dry while she got on, and she did not blot it. It struck her, not for the first time, that a stranger turning the leaves of it would take the thing for a private account of one man's business, kept for no reason but nosiness, and that the stranger would be right about the counting and would never arrive at what the counting was for, and she smoothed the page flat and let the matter alone.

Then she got out the letter paper.

It lived in the same drawer under a board that lifted, and it was better paper than anything else in the house, and it was the one expense of hers that nobody had ever seen a reason for and nobody had ever asked about. She squared a sheet to the edge of the table. She changed the pen, the inn's nib having gone soft on a day of figures and the office being owed a clean line. She trimmed the new nib, tried it on the corner of a spoiled sheet, trimmed it again. She moved the candle two inches to the left. All of that took longer than the first page of the letter would take, and all of it was the work, in the sense that twenty minutes at a sharpening stone is work.

The address she did not have to look up. She had not had to look it up for a long time, and there had been a season of her life, long finished, when the not-having-to had felt like standing in a draught, and that season was of no use to anybody now and she did not visit it. Under the inn's tall ledger, flat at the bottom of the drawer, lay a thin packet of old letters tied in a bootlace, which she likewise did not visit. She took out a fresh sheet, and she wrote the salutation the office preferred, which managed to be both correct and warmless, a trick she had privately admired for years, and she made her quarterly report.

She was good at it. That is a thing worth saying plainly, because she was good at it in the particular way of a woman doing well a job she wished did not exist. The office wanted to know the temper of the far valleys: what moved on the roads, what the quarries and the mills were worth this year, whether anything unusual required anyone's attention. *Nothing unusual requires anyone's attention* was a sentence she had written so many times, in so many seasons, that her hand could make it while her mind stood entirely elsewhere, and it was the most important sentence in every letter she sent, and she built each report around it the way you build a wall around a well.

There was a shape to it besides, and she kept the shape. The office liked its news in an order, roads and trade and prices and persons, and it got that order from her every quarter without variation. It also got, every quarter, a letter within a few lines of the length of the last, because a report gone short is a report with something taken out of it and a report gone long is a report with something put in, and both of them get read twice by somebody who is paid to read things twice. She began with the standing matter, the sentences that would have been true in any year of the last ten, to get her hand going at the right dullness before she came to anything real. Then the quarter's own facts, one at a time, each of them turned over first to see whether it had an edge anywhere on it, and any that had were set down again flatter.

She wrote the wool prices. She wrote that the road past Wainford was no worse than any spring. She wrote that the quarry had let two men go and taken on a boy, that the mill's wheel had been mended, that the harvest intentions were ordinary. Every line of it true. Every line of it of no earthly use to anyone. She read each one back before she went on to the next, listening for anything in it a man at a desk a week's road away might want to ask a second question about, and there was nothing, because she had taken pains that there should be nothing, and the pains were the trade. She wrote, because it would have been remarkable to omit him and remarkable was the one thing he must never be, a paragraph on the wizard: that he continued in the lamp trade, in a small commercial way; that he paid his accounts on the day; that he had put up a glass forcing-house and sold early salads, which the neighborhood found more comic than wonderful; that he had lately improved the footpath on his own ground at his own cost. Tradesman's items, every one. A man made entirely out of invoices.

The labour in that paragraph had all been in the choosing. The glass house went in because a building that size on a bare hill is not a thing that can be left out of a report; but it went in as salad. The footpath went in because half the valley had laughed about it in her own taproom and would go on laughing about it to carters, and a fact already loose on the roads is safer inside her letter than outside it. Everything else about the man that she could have written, she had never once written, and there was no list of those omissions anywhere, and there never would be, a list being the one document she could not have explained to anybody.

She read the paragraph back the way a joiner looks at a hidden joint, and left it as it stood.

Then she sat, with the pen off the paper, for rather a long time.

There was a fact she had been carrying since midsummer past. The whole valley had carried it, in the way a valley carries a thing, which is to say everyone had heard about the well, and everyone had agreed without one word being exchanged that there was nothing to have heard. A bucket had slipped. A woman had caught her boy back from an edge. Such things happen in market squares, and the man who happened to be crossing the square at the time had done nothing that forty witnesses could name, and the boy in question was in any case the old man's grandson and none of the office's affair, and none of hers.

She had watched the valley do it, that summer, and it had been worth watching. For a fortnight afterward the square argued about the well. The ironwork loose in the stone. The rope that had cut its own groove in the sheave and pulled out of true ever since. The coping, in the view of a third man, since before either of the other two was born. There was a father nine years dead who had held opinions on all of it and was quoted at her bar four evenings running. They enjoyed it. They had no intention of settling it, and they had every round of it in front of her, and not one of them in fourteen days said the word bucket.

She wrote the line anyway, once, to see it. *There was an incident at the well at midsummer involving the smith's scales and the miller's boy.* It was not even accurate. She had made it inaccurate with some care, the way you offer a coat that will not fit, and it still sat on the page like a lamp in a window. She looked at it and thought, not for the first time, that the whole craft of her position lay in one distinction: the office read what she wrote, but somewhere a person she had never met read what she chose not to write, and of the two documents the second was by a distance the more informative, and only she would ever hold it.

It had not always been a distinction she was obliged to make. For most of the years of it there had been nothing in this valley to leave out. A report from Ardenmoor was a report about wool and weather, and she had written it the way you sweep a floor that is already clean. She could name the morning that changed, and it had been an entirely ordinary morning: her own square, a horse loose in the shafts, a bucket going over the well coping, and herself in her own doorway with a cloth in her hand, looking the wrong way at the wrong moment and given a thing she had not asked to be given and could not hand back.

It had been market, and warm before seven. She had the trestle out in front of her own door and was wiping it down a third time, because of the dust coming off the carts and because a table put out dirty is a table nobody buys off. The square was as loud as it ever got in the year. Carts drawn wheel to wheel the whole north side and more of them backed into the lane, butter under wet cloths, a stall of early beans, three cheeses on a board with a boy set to watch them who plainly had no notion what he was watching for. She had been half minded to send for the inn's water early and had not sent, and that was the entire size of her part in it, and she had gone over that part since a good many more times than it deserved.

Then there was a horse loose in Kellard's shafts, and every face in that square went with it, and hers did not.

She was the width of the square away. Between her and the well stood the horse and the slewed cart and Kellard at its head, a basket of eggs got up off the cobbles into somebody's arms, and two men leaving the oven wall at a run. Out of all of that, the one place in the square making no sound whatever was the well. She heard nothing from it. She would say so if anyone ever asked her, and nobody ever would: she heard nothing, she was far too distant to have heard anything, and what came to her instead came in through her eyes and took less time than a full bucket takes to fall.

Then it was over, and the square was already putting itself back together the way squares do, with commentary. She found she still had the cloth in her hand. The man from the hill was standing at the well with his hand on the windlass frame, and he was looking at her, and she gave him the nod she would have given a carter and turned back to her table. She finished it, both ends and the near edge, without hurrying at it. Then she carried the cloth inside, and rinsed it, and wrung it out, and hung it on its nail, and opened the room, because there were forty people coming into it and a market day does not care what anybody has seen. It was three days before she got the small book out. When she did, she wrote two words in it, and shut it, and put it back under the other one.

Since then the small book and the letters had been two accounts of the same valley, and what stood between them had begun to have a shape. She thought of it as a shape, and she was exact about the word. A secret is a thing a person holds, and can put down. A shape is a thing a person makes, once a quarter, in ink, and posts.

And there was no leaving off. She had worked that out early and had not troubled to work it out again, because it did not improve with handling. Years of *nothing unusual requires anyone's attention* is a wall. One quarter gone thin, one season in which the wizard's paragraph did not sit in its usual place among the wool prices, and the wall would have a door in it, and somebody whose whole employment was the reading of walls would find the door before she had finished the sentence. She had made herself dull in order to be left alone. The only way to go on being left alone was to go on being dull, exactly, at the same length, four times a year, for as long as he stayed.

She took a fresh sheet and copied the letter over without the line, in her quick square hand, and burned the first sheet in the banked fire, standing over it with the tongs until it was ash and the ash was broken.

A written sheet does not want to burn in a banked fire. It lifts at the corners and browns and lies there being paper, and if it goes up whole it goes up as a black leaf with every line of the writing still on it in grey, legible across a room. A flake of that can ride a chimney and come down a street and be picked off a doorstep by somebody with an idle morning. So she opened the bed of the fire with the poker, laid the sheet flat on the red of it, held it down with the tongs until it took, and stood over it with her arms folded until there was nothing left but the black leaf. Then she broke the leaf. Then she broke the pieces of it, and turned them under, and raked the bed once across, and set the tongs back on their hook, where the girl would find them in the morning. The smell was in the room a minute and then was not. She washed her hands, ash getting into everything, and there was a clean sheet still to fold.

The clean sheet she folded in three and addressed and sealed with a candle-end and no device pressed into it, a seal with a device being a thing people remember having seen. Then she put it on the shelf behind the bar with the inn's own paper, a brewer's account and a wool return and a note about a delivery, where it would sit in the open for two days with nothing to distinguish it, being the dullest letter in the house.

The mail came through twice a week now, which the valley owed to the wizard's parcels, not that the valley said so. Wenn the carrier left the bag on the settle by the door and took what she had for him and whatever was warm in a cloth besides, and Thursday morning early she sorted it on the bar, before the room filled.

The bag came in cold and smelling of the outside of a horse. She emptied it onto the bar in one movement and sorted standing, left to right, households first and trades after, into the small stacks the village had learned where to look for. Two for the mill, both of them thin. One for the forge from a supplier, by the paper. The seed catalogue, thick as a ledger and gaudy on the front, good for a month of reading aloud in the Wrays' kitchen and two months of argument after. Something legal for Kellard, folded square, a wafer on it and a clerk's copperplate across the face. Three for the wool factor upstairs, who would want them before he wanted his breakfast. A letter for a cottage on the lane in a hand she had watched improve over four years, a son away and getting better at writing home. Sorting is not reading. Sorting is weight, and paper, and the direction a fold has been made in, and a woman who has done it long enough gets through a whole bag in the time the fire takes to catch.

She sorted the hill's post the way she sorted everyone's, which was a thing she would have said out loud to any face in the valley, and it was true, and like her letters it was built to be true. But her hands knew the weights and papers of his correspondence by now the way they knew her own cups, the thick laid stock from Vethmark that was the lawyer, the mean thin blue that was the freight agents, and so her hands knew before she did when they came, that Thursday, to something they had not held before: cream, heavy, a hand like a fence of black iron, and on the back, in wax the colour of a dried plum, a seal she had not seen pressed fresh in a great many years.

She stood quite still for a moment, in her empty morning room, holding another person's letter.

The seal was the office's. Not the plain daily stamp her own answers came franked with, on the rare occasions anything answered at all. The old proper seal, the tower and the open book, the one that meant the letter had been written upstairs, by somebody whose time was worth something, to be read and kept. She knew its weight in a hand. There had been one under the ledger, in the bootlace packet, for a good many years.

The paper was cream and heavy and cut clean, the sort a house buys by the ream and never afterward thinks about. The hand had been trained in a room where hands are trained: every letter the same height, no flourish anywhere on it, the address set out in four lines with a rule scored under the last, and the village's name spelled right, a courtesy fewer than one letter in five that came into that bag managed.

She did not hold it to the light. She did not tilt it, nor weigh it past what the sorting required, nor stand there one breath longer than the sorting took, and there was nobody in the room to see her not do any of it. She set it in the middle of the hill's little stack, under the lawyer and over the freight papers, square with both, exactly where the sorting would have set it, and she went and stood behind her bar and looked at the small stack sitting there in the plain grey light, addressed in iron to a man who was at that moment, if the morning was like other mornings, a mile up and over on his knees in a glass house with dirt on his hands.

The room would fill in an hour. The boy would come for the hill's bread and take the post up with it, at a run now the whole way, on the new stones.

He would have it by noon, then. He would read it and file it and get on with his morning, and nothing in his life in this valley had ever happened in such a way as to put it into his head to wonder what went out of it the other way, or in whose hand, or with what left out. She did not hold that against him. It was the whole object of the exercise, and she had built it herself, line by line, out of wool prices. There was nobody to say so to, and she had long since stopped composing the sentence she would have said if there had been.

Lira took down her good glasses, which no one had asked for, and polished one, and held it to the window, and set it back, and after that she did the thing she did on the mornings her own weather wanted managing, which was work. But twice before the room filled she came back down the bar to where the little stack sat, and squared it, though it had not needed squaring, and the second time she stood by it a moment with her hand flat on the bar beside it, the way you stand at a gate looking at weather coming over a hill that is going to arrive whether you have mentioned it to anyone or not.

The work, in its order, was this. The fire built up out of the bank and the ash pail carried out to the yard. Shutters back, all six. Sand down on the boards at the door end where the mud comes in, and swept off again. A barrel changed, and settled, and tapped, and the first half-pint drawn and thrown away. The good glasses down off their shelf and gone over, then the ordinary ones, then the tables wiped and set square to the wall, then the settle cushions turned. The factor came down and was fed and went out with his three letters in his coat. Somebody's dog got into the passage and was got out of it again without any fuss on either side. By nine the room had begun to fill in the slow way of a morning that is not a market morning, two carters and a man waiting on the mill and an old fellow who came in most days to be somewhere with a fire in it, and she drew what was asked for and made the change and said what wanted saying and nothing past it. Her hands did all of that while her mind stood entirely elsewhere. They had been taught the trick at a table in the snug, years back, and had learned it well, and had never since been able to unlearn it.

Once, going for the letter paper, her hand went past the bootlace packet at the bottom of the drawer and stopped there a moment, and did not take it out. It had been years.

*Letters going both ways now,* she wrote in the small book that night, and entered the day's take in the tall one, and banked the fire, and went up.
