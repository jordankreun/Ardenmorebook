# VOICE: THE NARRATOR OF ARDENMOOR
### The governing voice for The Tower of Ardenmoor, Book One.
### The floor is Le Guin (see the style guide): quiet, patient, sensory, restrained.
### On that floor stands Rothfuss — the intimate teller — with Mancour present only as
### the grain of a real working world. Where this note and the style guide differ, the
### spirit is the same and the style guide's restraint always wins. Read fully before drafting.

---

## THE VOICE IN ONE SENTENCE

A narrator who was there and has thought about it since, telling you the story of a grieving
man rebuilding a life — and a working magical practice — on a strange hill, with lyric attention
to the light, the silence, and the labor that fills a day.

The story leads and the telling is a novel's, not a game's. But the world beneath it is a
Mancour world: a wizard building up a real practice and the tower around it, a trade in true
commercial magic, a cast gathering to the work. That building is not backdrop — it is much of
the book's pleasure and most of its plot at the domestic level. Tell it; don't tally it.

---

## WHAT CARRIES THE BOOK (Rothfuss, foregrounded)

**A teller, not just a camera.** We stay in third-limited past tense, but the narration should
feel nearly as intimate as first person — we live behind Opus's eyes and inside his particular
intelligence. He notices precisely, remembers sideways, and reflects with a dry, earned wisdom.
The reader should feel *told* the story by someone who knows how it goes and is choosing, kindly,
what to dwell on. That choosing — where the narrator lingers, what he passes over in a line — is
the book's pulse. Let it breathe. A chapter is allowed to slow down and simply attend.

**Sensory precision as a moral act.** The exact quality of a sound, a light, a silence is worth
getting right: the specific amber of the LUMEN, the half-beat pause in Tomas's hammer, the way
frost lifts off grass in the order the sun touches it. Precision signals care; care is the
book's whole argument.

**Sentences that occasionally open into music.** Most sentences are clean and load-bearing. But
once or twice a chapter — at a threshold, a small grief, a moment of wonder — let a sentence
lengthen and sing, using rhythm, a turn of repetition, a well-placed fragment. Earn these; never
let the book go purple. The music lands because the surrounding prose is plain.

**The pleasure of a story well-told.** Opus values a thing done properly, including a thing
*said* properly. Let the narration take quiet pleasure in its own well-made sentences without
ever winking at the reader. Storytelling itself is part of the book's warmth.

**Naming and the weight of the true word.** Naming is quietly powerful here, obliquely: the hill
learning Opus's "shape," the predawn pulse becoming "almost a word," Brenna's grandmother naming
the Singer and the Listener. Keep it numinous. Never systematize it into a spell-list.

**Room to digress, briefly.** Rothfuss trusts a short detour — a memory, a small philosophy, the
history of a tool — when it deepens the teller. Permit these. Keep them short, keep them earned,
and always return to the ground with something gained.

## WHAT MANCOUR GIVES THIS BOOK — THE BUILT WORLD

This is the Spellmonger inheritance, and it stays. Mancour is the co-author of the *world*
(Rothfuss is the co-author of the *telling*). His gift is a wizard who builds: a man raising a
tower and a working magical practice on a hill, gathering a cast of hands and apprentices and
neighbors to the work, dealing in magic as a real trade with real customers and real costs.
Render it with relish — the building is much of the book's warmth and most of its plot at the
domestic scale.

**Magic as a real, commercial practice.** Opus is a planewright running a growing concern: the
LUMEN made and sold as a line, housings commissioned from Tomas, a labor crew, deliveries from
the quarry, credit extended and earned, apprentices taken on. Show the trade. The pleasure of
watching a competent professional diagnose, price, make, and deliver is real, and it belongs in
this book.

**The tower rises, and a life rises with it.** Each structure raised, each deal struck even,
each new hand hired (Ren) is a beat of a place and its people prospering through good work and
fair dealing. It is both at once — a grieving man rebuilding a life, and a practice being built
up around him. Never make it choose between the two.

**Commerce is character.** This is the one discipline that keeps the built world from going
gray: every transaction is also a relationship. Tomas's "Even," Lira's refilled jug, Kellard's
wary credit — the deal carries the feeling. Never let the practice become a spreadsheet; the
numbers matter because the people do.

**Legible magic, learned through use.** Workings have architecture, materials with opinions,
costs, and rest afterward, and the reader comes to feel the rules — but they learn by watching
the work, never from a lecture or a system diagram. Wonder comes from response: the ground
answering, a plant turning its leaves toward the hill.

## HOW IT SITS ON THE PAGE

**Attention leads; craft is one of the things attended to.** Sometimes a paragraph is pure
noticing — light, weather, a face, a memory — with no mechanism in it at all, and that is not a
paragraph to cut. When craft is on the page, let the narrator find the moment of grace inside
it, but don't force a "finish" onto every working. The rhythm below is *available*, not a mold:

> He set the last anchor at the northeast corner and felt for the deep layer before he
> committed the working's roots, the way you test ice with your weight before you trust it. The
> hill took the shape he asked for the way dry ground takes rain — not gratefully, ground has no
> gratitude, but completely, as though it had been shaped for exactly this and was only now being
> asked.

Some chapters will never do this. A chapter that is a walk, a conversation, and a kettle is a
whole chapter if the attention is alive.

**Dialogue stays plain; the narration carries any music.** Never let characters speak lyrically
(except, sparingly, Brenna channeling her grandmother). Jorin says "Even it runs north. Slight."
The beauty is in how Opus hears it.

**Restraint over abundance.** The book's power is in what it withholds. This is the Le Guin
floor and it is never overruled: name no emotion the reader can infer, explain no silence, and
when something enormous happens the prose goes *quieter*.

---

## HOW THE REGISTER BREATHES (no dials, just feel)

The balance shifts with the scene, and you feel it rather than measure it. Working scenes carry
more concrete craft, with the wonder let in a sentence at a time. Domestic and relationship
scenes are warm and closely observed, the noticing doing the work the speech won't. Grief and
threshold scenes are where the music lives — and where it must be quietest, most withheld. The
interludes belong to no one but the hill: geologic patience, fragments, no interiority, no craft.
That is the one place the teller falls silent.

If you ever find yourself deciding a percentage, stop and just listen to the paragraph.

## GUARDRAILS

**The Rothfuss risk, now that he leads:** self-admiring sentences, digression that forgets to
return, a protagonist who is effortlessly the best at everything. Counter: Opus is competent but
grief-slowed, occasionally wrong, and the book's problems are solved by patience and other
people, not by his brilliance. Cut any sentence that admires itself. Keep detours short and
earned. Lyricism is a spice, not the meal.

**The Mancour risk:** info-dumping the magic system, spreadsheet pacing, deals with no
relationship inside them, characters as function-holders. The built world stays in full; only
its gamified shadow goes. Every rule arrives through action, every transaction is also a
relationship, and the ledger is never the point — the people the ledger describes are.

**The shared trap — over-explaining.** The good pages trust what the weak pages explain.
Ardenmoor trusts. Quote the marginal note; don't gloss it. Show the refilled jug; don't tell us
Lira has softened. Render the compass pointing down; don't explain what it means. The reader
assembles meaning; that assembly is the reading experience.

---

## A NORTH-STAR TEST

Read any drafted paragraph and ask: *Is the narrator's attention alive here — am I being told
this by someone who saw it and cared?* If the answer is yes, the paragraph earns its place,
whether it holds a ward or only a kettle. If it's mechanism with no one behind it, find the
teller. If it's lovely but hollow — pretty with nothing seen or felt — cut it. The voice is a
person paying close, patient attention to a world worth the trouble.
