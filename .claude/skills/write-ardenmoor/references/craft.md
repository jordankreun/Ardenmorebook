# THE CRAFT — how fiction is built

**Purpose.** `editorial-read.md` teaches how to *read* a draft: it asks whether a scene earns its
place, whether a turn is earned, whether a payoff was planted. This file is its generative twin. It
teaches how to *build* the things that file knows how to judge.

The distinction matters because the rest of the engine cannot supply it. `feedback-engine.md` is an
enumerated ledger of this book's accumulated taste; every rule in it carries a `*Source:*` tag
pointing at a specific author note. That file can tell you the author dislikes foreshadow flags. It
cannot tell you *why hindsight narration structurally leaks tension*, which is the thing you need at
two in the morning when the sentence wants to write itself the wrong way.

Read section 1 before any of the others. It is the one that keeps the rest from doing damage.

---

## 1. THE COZY CONTRACT (read this before anything below)

Almost every craft text in existence is built on conflict. Scene = goal + obstacle + disaster.
Escalate each act. Raise the stakes. Give the protagonist agency and make him fight for it. That
advice is not wrong; it is domain-specific, and this book is outside the domain. Imported unexamined,
it would wreck the thing that makes this book work.

### What supplies forward motion when conflict does not

- **Competence displayed.** A reader will follow a skilled person doing skilled work almost
  indefinitely, provided the work is real and the difficulty is honest. This is the engine under the
  storehouse spec, the glass gauge, the reading of a crystal by tallies in fives.
- **Accumulation.** The pleasure is cumulative, not climactic. Each chapter the world is slightly
  more inhabited: one more name, one more building, one more obligation running in one more
  direction. The reader's investment compounds like the ledger does.
- **Reciprocity.** The book's real plot is an economy of favours. A gift laid downhill returns as a
  chain of lanterns coming up it. Track who owes whom; that ledger is the tension.
- **Deferred wonder.** One large strange thing, withheld and measured rather than deployed. The hum
  does not escalate. It is *investigated*, which is a different and slower kind of pull.
- **Return.** Seasons, rituals, the Wednesday bread, the same hill at a different temperature. Return
  with variation is a structural pleasure in its own right, not a filler between events.

### OVERRULED — standard advice, and what this book does instead

| Standard advice | Why it is standard | What this book does |
|---|---|---|
| Every scene needs conflict | Conflict guarantees a scene has a reason to exist | Every scene needs **change**. Name what changed: a fact, a relationship, an understanding, or the temperature. Conflict is one way to change something; it is not the only way. |
| Escalate stakes each act | Rising stakes counteract reader fatigue | Stakes stay **village-sized**. What grows is **implication** — the same small thing meaning more because more is attached to it. A burned barn matters because of who comes up the hill for it. |
| Open with a hook, in medias res | Buys attention before it is earned | Open on **consequence** (see §5). The hook is the narrator's voice and the reader's trust in it. A hook that outruns the register is a promise the book will not keep. |
| Cut anything that doesn't advance plot | Prevents flab | Atmosphere **is** the product. Cut the **empty** chapter — one that neither moves nor deepens anything — never the quiet one. A chapter whose work is texture earns its place if the attention is alive on the page. |
| The protagonist must drive the action | Passive protagonists bore | This book's problems are solved by **patience and other people**. That is the thesis, not a defect. Emlyn's competence is in *asking*; the plot is the valley answering. |
| Increase try/fail cycles | Manufactures tension | Increase **cost and reciprocity**. The failure that matters is not "he tried and failed" but "he got it and it cost someone something." |
| Give the antagonist page time | Externalises the conflict | There is no villain on stage. The pressures are money, weather, distance, and grief. Keep the distant institutions as **weather**, not as enemies. |

**The test that replaces the conflict test:** at the end of a chapter, can you say in one sentence
what is different now that was not different before, and does at least one person feel it? If yes, the
chapter is doing its job however quiet it is.

## 2. THE SCENE

**A scene is a continuous span of time, in one place, in which something changes.** Break any of the
three and you have something else: a jump in time is a new scene, a move to a new place is usually a
new scene, and no change at all is texture wearing a scene's clothes.

The change has four legitimate kinds, and only the first is what most craft texts mean by "plot":

1. **A fact changes.** Something is now true that was not: an order is placed, a bargain struck, a
   reading taken.
2. **A relationship changes.** The distance between two people is different at the end.
3. **An understanding changes.** Nobody's circumstances moved, but the narrator or the reader now
   knows something they did not.
4. **A temperature changes.** The emotional weather shifts. This is the most easily abused, because
   it is the easiest to *claim* and the hardest to demonstrate. Demand evidence on the page.

### Scene versus summary

**Summary compresses repetition and covers transit.** It is the right tool for the fourth identical
day of hauling stone, for a season passing, for a journey whose events do not matter.

**Scene is where the book actually happens.** A load-bearing beat rendered as summary is the single
most common structural failure in this manuscript's history, and it has a diagnostic signature:
long paragraphs, few or no quotation marks, and past-habitual verbs ("would go," "every morning,"
"in those days"). `tools/craft-check.sh` measures exactly this.

**The rule:** if a beat is a turn, a deal, a discovery, a first meeting, a confession, or a refusal,
it is played in real time. Summary may deliver you to its door and may carry you away afterward. It
may not stand in for the thing itself.

**The honest exception:** a beat may be summarized when the *narrator's distance from it* is the
point. A memoir narrator compressing something he does not want to look at directly is doing
characterization, not dodging. But you must be able to say which one you are doing.

### Entering late and leaving early

Come into a scene at the last possible moment before the change begins, and leave on the beat the
change lands. Everything before is throat-clearing; everything after is the author reluctant to stop.

The commonest form of entering early is furniture: arrivals, greetings, sitting down, weather
inventory. The commonest form of leaving late is the explanatory tail — the paragraph after the good
line that tells the reader what the good line meant. Both are cuttable almost without exception.

The one licensed late entry in this register: the deliberate approach, where the walking-toward *is*
the scene (the climb up the hill with a man who has never been asked up). That is not throat-clearing
because the approach is where the change happens.

## 3. THE TURN

Every scene worth playing has a turn: the moment the change actually occurs. It has four parts, and
skipping the third is how a turn comes to feel unearned.

1. **Setup.** The situation as it stands, stated economically. The reader must know what is at risk
   of changing before it changes.
2. **Pressure.** Something presses on it. A question asked, an offer made, a fact produced, a silence
   held.
3. **The weighing.** The character does not answer immediately. *This is the part that gets cut and
   must not be.* A decision arrived at instantly reads as authorial convenience, however correct the
   decision is. Give the deliberation its beat: the silence, the physical tell, the cup turned a
   quarter-turn on the table, the man who looks at the door on its trestles instead of at his son.
4. **The settle.** The new state, landed in an image or a short line, not explained.

A turn that feels snapped is almost always missing step 3. A turn that feels soggy usually has too
much of step 4.

## 4. DIALOGUE AS ACTION

Dialogue is not people exchanging information. It is people *doing things to each other* with words:
conceding, refusing, deflecting, testing, protecting, forgiving. If a line of dialogue is only
transferring facts, it is exposition wearing quotation marks, and it should be narration instead.

### How subtext is actually constructed

Subtext is not vagueness. It is produced by a specific and repeatable move: **the character answers a
different question than the one asked**, and the gap between the two carries the meaning.

- Asked whether he minds, a man says the wall is still true.
- Asked how long the vote took, an elder says the clerk had the ink stopper off and back on again.
- Asked whether the boy is ready, a father says the price of a fair count.

The reader performs the subtraction. That performance is the reading experience; do not do it for
them by adding the sentence that explains.

**Corollaries.** People understate what costs them most. People deflect to the concrete when the
abstract is unbearable. People say the small true thing instead of the large true thing. And people
who have known each other twenty years do not explain themselves.

### Diction domains — how to make voices distinct without dialect

Give each recurring character a **domain of metaphor** drawn from their work, and let them reach for
it under pressure. This is the cheapest and most durable characterization device available, and it
never reads as an accent.

- **Jorin** measures. Load, bearing, plumb, square, what a thing will carry. His verdicts are
  single words because a measurement does not need a sentence.
- **Ren** thinks in frames and loads, but he is learning a second vocabulary; his best lines are the
  moment his old domain reaches something new ("it's built true and it stands other").
- **Tomas** works in heat and tolerance. He answers in three words or fewer, and his real answers are
  physical: a hammer that pauses half a beat, an arm out straight.
- **Brenna** grows things. She measures in seasons and in what a living thing will tolerate. Flat,
  exact, no ceremony.
- **Penworth** accounts. Columns, dates, liabilities, the sentence eleven lines long. His warmth
  arrives disguised as scolding.
- **Cael** governs. He speaks in precedent, procedure, and the management of other people's opinions.
- **Lira** weighs. She gives nothing unpaid-for, and her diction is the ledger's: what a thing costs,
  what it is worth, what is owed.
- **Soren** counts. He is a child who reports observations and withholds inference.

### Two further tests

- **Does the line do two jobs?** The best dialogue advances the moment *and* characterizes the
  speaker *and* ideally carries information the reader needs. One job is acceptable; zero is cut.
- **Speaker trackability.** In any exchange with three or more voices, or a long two-hander, can the
  reader always tell who is speaking? The fix is one small gesture or tag, never a tag on every line.

## 5. CAUSALITY AND MOMENTUM

The engine already tests causality as **continuity**: does this chapter contradict the reason an
earlier chapter gave? That is necessary and insufficient. There is a second test it has never
applied.

**Continuity-causality asks: does this contradict?
Momentum-causality asks: does this compel?**

A book can be perfectly consistent and still feel like a list. The cure is that each chapter should
exist *because of* a named prior event, not merely *after* it.

### The therefore/but test

Between any two consecutive chapters, the honest connective should be **therefore** or **but** —
almost never **and then**. Say the joint aloud:

- *Ch 17 → Ch 18:* the census found no money, **therefore** he must pay in land and time. Strong.
- *Ch 15 → Ch 16:* Penworth demanded the crop come in and sold, **therefore** the harvest is an
  installment to be cut by hand. Strong (this joint was repaired; it read as "and then autumn came").

### The three entry moves

Every chapter opens in one of three ways. Classify yours before you draft it.

- **CONSEQUENCE.** The opening names or clearly implies the prior event that caused this chapter.
  Strongest, and should be the default.
- **FRAME.** The narrator steps back to reorder, reflect, or hand over to another point of view.
  Legitimate and valuable, but it must produce its cause within a paragraph or two, or it becomes a
  calendar opening with better manners.
- **CALENDAR.** The chapter opens because time passed: the thaw came, the harvest came, a night at
  the front of that winter. **This is the weak one.** It is not forbidden — a season genuinely can be
  the cause, and a fire genuinely has no cause the narrator will claim — but every calendar opening
  should be interrogated, because the cause is usually available two chapters back and simply was not
  claimed at the door.

**The repair is almost always cheap.** In every case found in this manuscript, the causing event
already existed on the page a chapter or two earlier; the opening merely failed to point at it. You
do not invent plot to fix a calendar opening. You point at what is already there.

### Declaring the parent

At draft time, write the line: *this chapter happens because of ____.* If the honest answer is "time
passed," you have found the chapter that needs work. `tools/chapter-check.sh` asserts this line
exists in the engine report; that assertion exists to force the sentence to be written, because
writing it is the whole of the discipline.

## 6. SETUP AND PAYOFF

A setup is any object, promise, question, or capability introduced with enough weight that a reader
will remember it. Weight is the operative word: a mentioned chair is not a setup; a chair somebody
refuses to sit in is.

Every setup ends in exactly one of three states. Two are legitimate.

- **PAID.** The setup is discharged on the page. The way is laid; the village runs up it in the dark.
- **BANKED.** The setup is explicitly acknowledged as still open. *"I don't know yet what it means,"*
  said twice and meant. Banking is a craft act, not an evasion: it tells the reader the author is
  holding the thread deliberately, which converts an itch into anticipation.
- **ORPHANED.** Planted with weight, then never referenced again by anything. **This is the only
  failure**, and it is invisible to every per-chapter check, because the chapter that plants it is
  fine and the chapters that fail to pay it do not know it exists.

### Watering

A long-running setup needs occasional contact or it dies quietly. **Water it rarely, and never the
same way twice.** Between waterings a seed rests; resting is the default. The failure mode is not
under-watering but *repetitive* watering, which telegraphs.

A useful form of watering that costs nothing: the **refusal**. A narrator declining to look at a
thing keeps it alive better than a narrator checking on it, because it also characterizes the
refusal.

### Blindness at plant time

A seed must not change the narration's temperature. If a first-time reader can tell a paragraph is
load-bearing, replant it. This is why hindsight flags are banned at a seed: they are a spotlight
mounted on the thing you are trying to hide.

Tracking lives in `state/thread-ledger.md`. Update it in post-flight, every chapter, or it rots.

## 7. INFORMATION MANAGEMENT

At any moment three parties know different things: **the reader**, **the narrator**, and **the
characters**. Most structural pleasure comes from managing the gaps deliberately rather than
accidentally.

- **Reader behind narrator** — the default in memoir. The narrator knows how it turned out; the
  reader does not. The tension is the reader's curiosity about a settled past.
- **Reader ahead of a character** — dramatic irony. The reader watching Lira write a report Emlyn
  will not learn of for a long time. This is the strongest tool available in a low-conflict book,
  because it generates apprehension without any threat appearing on the page.
- **Reader ahead of the narrator** — rare and powerful. The reader assembles the twist from the
  bearing, the leaves, and the corner while the narrator is still auditing. Earned only if every clue
  was fairly placed.

**Withholding versus cheating.** Withholding is declining to state something the narrator has no
reason to state yet. Cheating is a narrator who *would* have thought about a thing at that moment and
conspicuously does not, or who describes his own knowledge in terms designed to mislead. The test: on
reread, does the withheld passage still read honestly? If it only worked because the reader was
missing something, it cheated.

## 8. POINT OF VIEW AS AN INSTRUMENT

POV is not a configuration setting. It is a lens with specific optical properties, and this book uses
an unusual one.

**Retrospective first person (memoir) buys you:**

- Judgment. The narrator may assess his younger self, which is where most of the book's warmth and
  all of its comedy live.
- Compression licence. He may move over four seasons in a sentence because he is telling, not
  dramatizing.
- Intimacy through address. The reader is positioned as a listener, which the coda eventually
  literalizes.

**And it costs you exactly one thing, constantly: tension.** The teller survived; you know it from
the first page. The form leaks suspense by construction.

**Therefore the discipline.** Because the frame already gives away that everything came out
survivable, the narration must not give away *anything else*. This is the craft reason behind the
foreshadow ban, which elsewhere in the engine reads as a taste rule:

- "I did not know then," "that came later," "it was coming" — each one spends a little of the only
  tension the form still has, and buys nothing. The reader already knows there is a later. Telling
  them a specific thing lives in it converts a discovery into an appointment.
- The exception that proves it: a *general* admission of fallibility ("go easy on him") costs nothing,
  because it points at character rather than at plot.

**The knowledge rule.** The narrator may not use knowledge before he acquired it. Not "his grandson"
before anyone says so; "the young boy." This is not pedantry — it is the whole basis on which the
reader trusts the narrator's account of what he *did* know.

**Third-person chapters** are a reserved instrument. Use one only when the reader must see something
the narrator cannot, and never one that knows enough to spend a mystery early.

## 9. TEXTURE AND RHYTHM

Individual chapter quality does not guarantee book quality. Three excellent chapters of the same
shape in a row read worse than three good chapters of different shapes, and no per-chapter review can
see it. This is the engine's documented number-one blind spot; `tools/span-check.sh` exists for it.

### Chapter shapes

Classify each chapter's dominant mode: BUILD, COMMERCE, WONDER, DOMESTIC, SOCIAL, CRISIS, FRICTION.
Then look at the previous two or three. **Three consecutive chapters of the same shape is the flag** —
not a prohibition, but a demand for a reason.

The repair is rarely "add conflict." It is usually to promote an existing beat: a visitor, a small
crisis of craft, a piece of commerce, a scene with a person who has been offstage.

### Temperature

Track warm / cool / tense alongside shape. A long run of unopposed warmth slackens even when every
individual chapter is charming — the reader stops believing anything can cost anything. Book One's
Ch 5 through Ch 9 is the case study: nothing resists the protagonist for five units, and the fix was
a single clause of financial pressure in Ch 8.

### Cadence depreciation

A closing move loses value each time it repeats. Book One closed on dusk, amber light, and
contentment-noticed in five of its first seven units. The move is the register's birthright and
should not be banned — but the *shape* of the close should vary: time of day, who is present, whether
anyone speaks. Check the previous two closes before writing a third.

## 10. THE AUTHORITY OF THE SPECIFIC

The reason this book can carry an aware hill is that it can carry a glass gauge.

A reader extends trust earned on verifiable ground to unverifiable ground. When the mortar cures in
three days, the fall on a cold-store floor is one in sixty, and a lamp costs a half-gold, the reader
concludes that this narrator knows things — and then accepts the hum on the same credit. Specificity
is not decoration; it is the collateral on which the fantasy borrows.

**Practical rules.**

- Prefer the exact to the evocative. "Eleven fleeces" beats "a quantity of wool."
- The specific must be *load-bearing*, not merely present. A number nobody acts on is set-dressing.
- Errors are expensive in both directions: a wrong specific costs more trust than a vague statement
  would have, because it was offered as a credential.
- Logistics are real. Wagons do not climb roadless hills; goods unload somewhere; somebody paid the
  carriage.
- When inventing a process, invent its *failures* too. The eleven housings before the one that held
  do more for credibility than any description of the one that held.
