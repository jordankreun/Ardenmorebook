## 1. THE VOICE IN ONE BREATH

Le Guin's floor, Rothfuss's teller, Mancour's world — and, deliberately, a measured draw
of Mancour's humor.

Plain water that carries weight, not thin water that avoids it. The teller was there, has
thought about it since, and once in a while his attention opens into music. The world
beneath him is a working one — a magical practice built deal by deal, board by board —
and he is allowed an attitude toward that work: a dry, ledger-born, tradesman's humor,
aimed at himself and his trade, never at the hill.

The humor is not decoration. It is the voice's proof of competence and its defense
against sweetness. A cozy book with no dry edge goes saccharine; a dry edge with no
restraint goes glib. Holding both is the whole craft of this book.

The three layers stack; they do not blend into mush. **A Mancour joke, delivered in
Le Guin syntax, by a Rothfuss teller.** That is the sound.

## 2. POV, TENSE, AND THE TELLER FRAME

- POV as locked in §0. Past tense. Never head-hop.
- **Interludes** (four, between Parts) are the hill's wordless POV: no dialogue, no named
  emotion, no human interiority. Perception without a person. These carry zero humor.
- **The retrospective frame** shows itself **once per chapter, twice at most.** More and
  the frame upstages the scene. It is a small admission that the telling comes from
  later, and it must attach to something concrete in the scene:
  - *First person:* "I know now what the warmth in the stone meant. That morning I
    thought it was the sun."
  - *Third limited:* "It would be a year before he understood what the warmth in the
    stone meant. That morning he thought it was the sun."
- The frame never foreshadows plot ("had I known what was coming"). It notices meaning,
  not events. Never a cliffhanger, never a promise to the reader.

## 3. DICTION

- **Anglo-Saxon by default; Latinate saved for lift.** Stone, haul, mend, dusk — not
  construct, transport, repair, twilight, unless the Latinate word is doing deliberate
  tonal work inside a lifted sentence.
- **Verbs carry sentences.** If an adverb props up a verb, replace the verb. Target zero
  `-ly` adverbs; allow one only when no verb in English does the job.
- **Metaphors come from inside the valley.** Rope, tide, grain, mortar, frost, sheep, the
  forge. If Emlyn could not have seen it, he cannot compare to it.
- **Concrete anchor for every abstraction.** Grief is a sticking drawer. Trust is a key
  left on a nail. Abstraction in one sentence, object in the next.
- **Name no emotion the reader can infer from behavior.** Absolute. "He was relieved" is
  a defect; show the shoulders come down.
- **No modern idiom, no techspeak, no fake-archaic.** Anachronism dial at 2 (§7.4).

## 4. SENTENCE AND PARAGRAPH MECHANICS

**Home rhythm.** Two to four cumulative sentences gathering detail, closed by a short
declarative that lands the weight. Not every paragraph — a page of identical shapes is
its own failure — but this is the rhythm the prose returns to.

> The lime came up the hill in the last light, four loads of it, and the carter's boy
> walked at the wheel with a stone ready for the rut he knew was coming. They got the
> last load into the dry before the rain did not come after all. Emlyn paid them in the
> doorway. The evening smelled of wet that never fell.

**Landing sentences are sacred.** The last sentence of a paragraph, a scene, and above
all a chapter carries the most weight with the fewest words. Never land on a lyric lift.
Never land on a joke and a lift in the same paragraph. **Chapters end on an image or a
single line of dialogue — never a question to the reader, never a cliffhanger.** The one
exception is the book's final chapter, which ends on the needle.

**Open in continuity.** The first image of a chapter answers the last image of the one
before it — the same weather, the same hour moved on, the same object seen again.

---

## 5. THE QUIET RULE AND THE BUDGETS

**Quiet at the peak.** At the biggest moment of a scene, sentences get shorter, diction
plainer, figuration drops to zero. If a passage feels important and the prose is working
hard, the prose is wrong. **The book gets quieter at its biggest moments, never louder.**

**Music budget.** At most one lifted passage per scene, two or three per chapter. A lift
is one to three sentences where the language opens, and it must attach to labor the
chapter has already done concretely. A lift with no earned work under it is decoration.

**Aphorism budget.** Two per Part, maximum, each paid for by the scene in front of it.
Le Guin earns a maxim twice a book; the rest is accurate seeing. In doubt, cut the maxim
and keep the seeing.

**Teller budget.** One retrospective noticing per chapter, two at most (§2).

**Humor budget.** Per §7.3.

## 6. DIALOGUE AND THE FIXED VOICES

"Said" is the tag. Characters withhold as much as they reveal. No dialogue that exists to
explain the world to the reader. **Never explain a silence.**

The speech rules below are non-negotiable. Humor bends around them; it never bends them.

| Character | Fixed rule | Humor calibration |
|---|---|---|
| **Jorin** | Single freighted words | The driest comedy in the book, and he never knows he's making it. One word can be a verdict. Never give him two sentences to be funny in. |
| **Tomas** | Stillness and one load-bearing question | Never funny, never a target. His stillness can make the sentence *before* him funnier by contrast. Humor stops at his threshold. |
| **Brenna** | Considers, then speaks completely | Comedy of thoroughness — she lays out a folly in full, in order, without malice, and the completeness is the joke. Never wrong inside her own speech. |
| **Lira** | Neutral ledger-facts and service, never stated warmth | The deadpan goldmine. A neutral entry carries a scene's comedy without her expressing anything. *In the book she had written: wards, one. Goat, one. Balance: even.* The ledger jokes; Lira does not. |
| **Soren** | Solemn, structural | Never jokes, never gets the joke. The narration never laughs at him — the teller's protectiveness is canon. His solemnity may be quietly funny to the reader; the prose never frames it that way. |
| **Ren** | Honest to the grain | The comedy of plain honesty — a literal answer that cuts. No wit, no timing. The humor is that none was intended. |

## 7. THE HUMOR SYSTEM

### 7.1 The four sources (only these)

**Bathos through logistics.** Something numinous or grave, and then someone has to pay
for it, haul it, or explain it to a client who won't understand.

> The ward took the better part of the night, and near dawn the stones under it went
> warm, all together, the way a hearth does when the fire catches at last. In the
> morning the farmer asked if it would keep foxes out too, or was that extra.

**Professional cynicism.** The patience of a man who has dealt with guilds, notables,
paperwork, and clients who want a miracle at cost. Weary and exact — never bitter.

> The reeve's letter ran to three pages. One of them was titles.

**Self-deprecation as competence signal.** Emlyn undercuts himself precisely because he
is good and doesn't need to posture. He may be vain, wrong, cheap, or petty about money
on the page, and the telling never rescues him from it.

> He had told Jorin the hinge design was sound. He had told him twice, which was the
> problem.

**Domestic friction.** The small weather of a working household: apprentices, meals,
tools misplaced, the politics of who mends what. Warm, never cute.

### 7.2 The five placement rules

1. **Joke in the noun, not the adjective.** A precisely absurd *thing* lands; a clever
   modifier is the author nudging, and the floor won't hold it. If the comedy lives in an
   adjective or adverb, rebuild until it lives in a noun, a verb, or a fact.
2. **Deadpan, always.** State it flat and walk on. No signposting, no "wryly," no
   sentence explaining the sentence before it. The narrator never laughs at his own line.
3. **Anachronism dial at 2.** Borrow Mancour's *attitude*, never his vocabulary (§7.4).
4. **Either side of the weight, never on it.** Comedy before a peak sharpens it; after,
   releases it; on it, kills it.
5. **Aimed at Emlyn, not the world.** He can be the fool of the scene; neighbors and
   clients can be gently absurd the way real people are. The hill, the hum, the grief
   objects, and the dead are never funny. The book laughs *inside* its world, never at it.

**And structurally: no stacking.** Never two beats in consecutive sentences, never a joke
inside a lift.

### 7.3 Budgets by thread

- **Building** — the humor's home. 2–4 beats in a building-heavy chapter.
- **Apprentices** — 1–2 beats, affectionate friction only. Emlyn can be outmatched by a
  child's logic; the child is never the punchline.
- **Grief** — effectively zero. None in any scene whose live thread is grief, and none
  within sight of the chest, the letter, the drawer, or the bedroom. Sole exception: the
  involuntary comedy of ordinary life *continuing* (a pot boiling over during a hard
  hour), rendered without a smile.
- **Undercurrent** — zero. The hum, the readings, the correspondents, the Conclave. One
  permitted shape only: Emlyn attempts a deflecting lightness and the scene shows it
  failing — the joke lands on silence, and the silence is the point.
- **Interludes** — zero, absolutely.

Typical chapter: 1–3 beats. Broad building chapter: up to 4. Grief or undercurrent
chapter: 0. **When unsure, under-spend.** Le Guin's gravity is the asset the entire
system exists to protect.

### 7.4 The anachronism dial

- **0 — fake-archaic.** "Prithee," "'twas," inverted syntax for flavor. Banned.
- **2 — the target.** Timeless plain English. Modern in *attitude* (irony, professional
  weariness, an eye for absurdity), pre-industrial in *vocabulary and rhythm*. Nothing a
  reader can date.
- **7 — Mancour's own register.** Contemporary casual diction, managerial vocabulary,
  audible 21st-century speech. Banned for this book.

**Banned (non-exhaustive; extend by analogy):** basically; honestly / frankly as
sentence-openers; okay; fine; "sure enough" as a discourse marker; "at the end of the
day"; "in terms of"; "the bottom line"; worst-case; manage / management; priorities;
logistics; efficient / efficiency; process (as a noun of work); situation; percent and
numeral-percentages; "not great"; "to be fair"; options; schedule (prefer "the days we
had"); budget (prefer "the purse," "what the work could bear").

Replacements come from the trade: the hauling, the purse, the count, the ledger, the
season's worth, what the stone would bear. If a banned word survives because "there's no
other way to say it," there is — find it in the valley.

## 8. COMMERCE IS CHARACTER

Every transaction answers two questions: **what does it cost besides money,** and **what
does it reveal about someone.** A deal with no feeling is a spreadsheet; a feeling with
no object under it is fog. Humor rides on top of this rule and never replaces it — a
funny deal is still a character beat first.

> Before: *Emlyn negotiated with the carter for a lower rate on the lime, eventually
> agreeing on three silver per load, a meaningful saving over the season.*
>
> After: *The carter wanted four silver the load and swore lime hauled heavier this
> year. They settled at three, and the man drove off pleased with himself, which was
> worth the silver. Lime does not change its weight. Carters do.*

Price, feeling, a small vanity indulged on purpose, and a deadpan landing in the nouns.

**Magic on the page:** silent craft. No incantations, no system exposition, no rules
explained to the reader. Magic is rendered as skilled labor with cost and apprenticeship,
described from the hands outward — what it takes out of him, what it costs to buy, who
wants it cheaper.

## 9. TONAL REGISTER BY THREAD

The four threads are currents, not quests on a schedule. A chapter follows whichever the
story has made live. Each has a fixed register:

- **Building** — warm, dry, hands-on, rendered with relish. Highest humor. Every
  structure is also a relationship, never a milestone on a build-order.
- **Apprentices** — patient and observational. Gentle humor. The teller's protectiveness
  toward Soren governs every line about him.
- **Grief** — quiet, object-bound, unnamed. Measured only in domestic thresholds. **No
  flashbacks; the dead exist in objects only.** Zero humor.
- **Undercurrent** — weather, not plot, until the book's last movement. Restrained, cool,
  never ominous-for-effect. Zero humor. Never reveal the mechanism early.

## 10. PACING AND MONTAGE

Time moves honestly. Errands take hours; a quarry round-trip is a day; construction
compresses into montage **anchored by day-labels and physical progress, never "time
passed."** Years may live in a subordinate clause; an afternoon may take four pages —
the narrator's attention sets the exchange rate.

> Before: *Over the following weeks, construction proceeded efficiently. Materials
> arrived on schedule and the walls rose steadily.*
>
> After: *By the feast of the first cutting the walls stood waist-high. Two days lost to
> rain, one to the quarry's cart, and one to an argument about the door that Emlyn won
> by paying for it. By midsummer a man could stand in the study and know it would be a
> room.*

Stakes never inflate to epic. This is village-sized: implications grow, volume does not.
Let early chapters run wide and unhurried — earn the reader's love of the world before
asking anything of them.

## 11. WORKED EXAMPLES

**A. Self-admiring lyric → floor.**

> Before: *Dawn spilled molten gold across the frost-jeweled hill, gilding every blade of
> grass with the promise of the day to come.*
>
> After: *Frost had come in the night. The sun took it off the east slope first, and the
> grass lay dark where it had been, as if the hill had turned over in its sleep.*

Cut the jewelry; keep one figure, sourced from inside the world.

**B. Named emotion → behavior.**

> Before: *A wave of grief moved through him as he passed the bedroom door.*
>
> After: *He passed the bedroom door without slowing. He had gotten good at that, the
> not-slowing.*

**C. Joke in the adjective, dial too high → joke in the noun, dial at 2.**

> Before: *The letter was hilariously pompous — basically a masterclass in
> self-importance.*
>
> After: *The reeve's letter ran to three pages. One of them was titles.*

Signposting, banned diction, comedy in modifiers — all three defects gone. The after
version is a fact, stated flat.

**D. Humor on the peak → humor moved off it.**

> Before: *Tomas set down his cup. "And if it isn't the wind?" he said — which was like
> him, always saving his one arrow for your softest spot.*
>
> After: *Tomas set down his cup. "And if it isn't the wind?"*
> *Emlyn watched the fire for a while.*

The wry aside dies. If the scene wants a comic beat, it goes earlier — the day's failed
mortar, the walk over — and the peak is left in silence.

## 12. REWRITE PROCEDURE (PER CHAPTER)

Run the skill's startup sequence first (this file, story bible, manuscript log, target
chapter and both neighbors). Then rewrite in ordered passes. Do not merge them; each has
one job.

1. **Fence the weight.** Mark every weight-peak: grief thresholds, undercurrent
   surfacings, the turn the chapter exists for. Inside a peak and the ~3 sentences
   entering and leaving it: zero humor, and the quiet rule (§5) applies.
2. **Ledger pass.** Every transaction, logistics stretch, and montage. Enforce §8. Place
   or adjust humor beats per §7. Remove any existing joke that fails the flat test or
   lives in a modifier.
3. **Floor pass.** Diction sweep against §3 and the banned list. Adverb sweep. Metaphor
   provenance check. Quiet the fenced peaks. Rebuild paragraph landings.
4. **Teller pass.** Music, aphorism, and teller budgets (§5). Confirm the chapter opens
   in continuity with the previous closing image and ends on image or line of dialogue.
5. **Continuity.** The rewrite changes voice, not facts. Any new fact introduced by a
   beat — even a goat, a feast day, a price — gets cut or logged in
   `state/story-bible.md`. Then run `references/continuity-checklist.md` as usual, plus
   the normal recap line in `state/manuscript-log.md`.
6. **Read aloud.** Every changed paragraph at reading pace. Anything that stumbles, sings
   too hard, or winks gets one more pass.

## 13. VOICE-CHECK FLAG LIST

Flag any line that:

1. Signposts or explains its own joke.
2. Places humor inside a fenced peak or its 3-sentence approach.
3. Uses banned diction (§7.4) or any dateable modern idiom.
4. Puts the comedy in an adjective or adverb.
5. Aims a joke at the world, the hill, the dead, or a grief object.
6. Stacks two beats in consecutive sentences, or puts a joke inside a lift.
7. Renders a deal with no feeling, or a feeling with no object.
8. Names an emotion or explains a silence.
9. Admires itself — a lift with no earned labor under it.
10. Spends an unpaid aphorism, or exceeds the Part budget.
11. Breaks a fixed speech rule (§6) in pursuit of a laugh.
12. Gets louder at a big moment.
13. Info-dumps magic, or lets the practice harden into a spreadsheet.
14. Flashes back to Arielle, or names her feeling instead of showing her objects.
15. Ends a chapter on a question or cliffhanger.
16. Reveals the undercurrent's mechanism early.

**Fix the prose, not the checklist.**

## 14. LITMUS TESTS

- **Flat test** — read it with no tone. Still lands → keep. Needs a wink → cut.
- **Cut test** — remove the adjective. Nothing lost → it stays gone.
- **Provenance test** — could this metaphor occur to a man who has never left the valley?
- **Ledger test** — what does this deal cost him besides money?
- **Quiet test** — at the peak, can the sentence be shorter? Then it isn't quiet yet.
- **Target test** — who is this joke at the expense of? If not Emlyn or the ordinary
  absurdity of people, cut it.

When two tests disagree, **restraint wins.** This book survives a missing joke. It does
not survive a cracked floor.

---

## 15. FAILURE MODES

- Drafting or revising before reading the previous chapter.
- Forgetting to update the story bible, so later chapters contradict this one.
- Stakes inflating to epic.
- The practice hardening into a spreadsheet.
- Forcing a beat into a chapter that wanted to be quiet, or padding to a word count.
- A sentence that admires itself; a digression that forgets to come back.
- Explaining a silence or naming an emotion.
- Chasing a laugh past the floor.

## §19. POINT OF VIEW DISCIPLINE (Spellmonger hybrid — updated 2026-07-11)

The book follows Emlyn but is **not wholly in his voice.** Two modes, one per chapter, never mixed
within a chapter:

- **Emlyn's chapters (the default, most of the book): FIRST PERSON, past tense.** His voice: dry,
  precise, self-aware, warm underneath; he observes his own grief the way he'd observe a ward with a
  flaw he isn't ready to repair. He tells it at a **near remove** — close to the living of it, with
  ordinary hindsight ("I didn't know it yet") — **not** as an ancient chronicler decades later. No
  deep-time framing, no "this account," no correcting the legends about himself. Inside an Emlyn
  chapter we still never enter another head: we see others only through what is physically observable
  (Lira folding a letter, Jorin's hammer pausing a half-beat).
- **Other-character chapters + interludes: THIRD-PERSON LIMITED**, past tense, on one character (or
  the hill, for interludes), in the same blended voice. Used when the reader needs a scene away from
  Emlyn (Lira sealing a Conclave letter; Tomas alone with the hum before his confession — but only
  *after* the mystery can bear it; the hill between parts). Stay in that one head for the whole
  chapter; no head-hopping. Reserve it — most chapters are Emlyn's.

Never spend a mystery early by choosing a POV that knows too much: no inside-Tomas chapter before
his confession beat, no hill-interior that states the twist.

## §20. DO / DO NOT

**Do:** trust silence; let readers assemble meaning; keep stakes village-sized while implications grow; render children with dignity; keep the Conclave and the correspondents as distant weather until Part IV; let the twist recontextualize rather than shock.

**Do not:** use modern idiom or techspeak ("okay," "process his feelings," "energy signature" — say *imprint* or *signature* only as the instruments' craft-terms already established); psychoanalyze characters in narration; write flashbacks of Arielle (she exists in objects only); inflate to epic stakes; give villains page-time (there are none on stage in Book One); use numbered lists, headers, or any structural furniture inside the narrative; break POV; explain the marginal note — quote it and let it work.

## §21. SCENE CRAFT DIALS (locked 2026-07-14 — the author's four standing pushes; write toward these from the first line)

> **SCOPE: DRAFTING AND DEVELOPMENTAL WORK ONLY** (scoped 2026-07-31; the lock's content is
> unchanged, only where it applies is now stated). **Dials 1, 2 and 4 instruct additions** — more
> dialogue, summary converted to played scene, a comic beat — and `modes/revise-moderate.md` and
> `modes/revise-light.md` forbid exactly those. This file is a revision mode's primary prose
> authority, so without this banner the two documents give opposite orders on the same page.
> **Dial 3 (vary the bookends) stays live in every mode**: it is diagnostic, not additive, and a
> revision can act on it by varying what is already there.

1. **More dialogue.** The book's most alive passages are its talkiest (Cael at the inn, Jorin's
   "Good"). Let load-bearing exchanges PLAY OUT IN VOICES: negotiations, confessions, teases,
   bargains happen as talk on the page, not as the narrator's recounting of talk. Every chapter
   wants at least one or two sustained dialogue scenes. NPC speech rules still govern every line.
2. **Scene over summary.** Montage/summary is a BRIDGE between beats, never the beat itself. If a
   chapter's turn, deal, discovery, or first-meeting happens inside a summarizing paragraph, stop
   and dramatize it in real time. (The montage habit is the identified drift: several early
   chapters compress lived scenes into recounted report.)
3. **Vary the bookends (openings AND closes).** The default open (weather/season/state-of-me) has
   become a pattern; so has the evening-alone-reflective close. Rotate OPENINGS deliberately: open
   mid-dialogue, mid-task, on another person arriving, on an object, in a room the reader hasn't
   seen. And glance at the previous 2–3 CLOSES: if a third straight quiet-evening-coda is forming,
   vary the *shape* of the close (time of day, subject, who is present), never its calm. This does
   NOT ban the cozy close (the register's birthright) or the deliberate callback-closers (the
   lamplight, "for company," "see what the morning would bring"); it just prevents an accidental
   third in a row. Check the previous chapter's opening and the last two closes before drafting
   (pre-draft brief item).
4. **More humor.** Wry asides alone don't carry the Mancour warmth. Aim for at least one genuine
   comic beat per chapter: a running bit (Snow's grudges, Penworth's letters, the village's
   standing opinions), a deadpan exchange, a small domestic absurdity. Humor is a craft beat, not
   a garnish; it earns the quiet moments. Running bits MAY recur across chapters (they are
   callbacks, not echoes — note deliberate ones in the phrase registry's comments, don't register
   them as one-use).

## §22. WRITE-TIME TIC BUDGET

Aim at this *while* drafting, not only when fixing afterwards. The teller's signature
constructions are voice at low frequency and mannerism at high. Roughly per chapter: **", which
is/was …" tails ~5** (each must land a judgment or a joke — "which was Jorin knighting him");
**"a good/great deal / a great many" ~3** (elsewhere: *much, far, many, most*, or no measure at
all); **"the way you/a …" similes ~5** (only the vivid); **"I will not pretend / I will not tell
you" ≤1** (usually say the thing directly: "The first ones did not work."); **"in the end" ≤2;
"which is to say" ≤2; "which by then" ≤1.** Never reuse another chapter's distinctive coinage
(`tools/phrase-registry.txt`); deliberate callbacks are a plotted exception, not a habit. Prefer
the period, the colon and the plain declarative to the appositive reflex.

---

**Relationship to §5 and §7.** §5's music, aphorism and teller budgets and §7's humor budgets
govern *how often the voice reaches for an effect*. This section governs *which constructions the
teller over-uses*. They are different dials and `tools/prose-lint.sh` enforces this one.

## §23. GUARDRAILS

**The lyric risk:** self-admiring sentences, digression that forgets to return, a protagonist
effortlessly the best at everything. Counter: Emlyn is competent but grief-slowed, occasionally
wrong, and the book's problems are solved by patience and other people, not by his brilliance.
Cut any sentence that admires itself. Keep detours short and earned. Lyricism is a spice, not
the meal.

**The practical risk:** info-dumping the magic system, spreadsheet pacing, deals with no
relationship inside them, characters as function-holders. The built world stays in full; only its
gamified shadow goes. Every rule arrives through action, every transaction is also a
relationship, and the ledger is never the point — the people the ledger describes are.

**The shared trap, over-explaining.** The good pages trust what the weak pages explain. Ardenmoor
trusts. Quote the marginal note; do not gloss it. Show the refilled jug; do not tell us Lira has
softened. Render the compass pointing down; do not explain what it means. The reader assembles
the meaning, and that assembly is the reading experience.

## §24. A NORTH-STAR TEST

Read any drafted paragraph and ask: *is the narrator's attention alive here — am I being told
this by someone who saw it and cared?* If yes, the paragraph earns its place, whether it holds a
ward or only a kettle. If it is mechanism with no one behind it, find the teller. If it is lovely
but hollow, pretty with nothing seen or felt, cut it. The voice is a person paying close, patient
attention to a world worth the trouble.
