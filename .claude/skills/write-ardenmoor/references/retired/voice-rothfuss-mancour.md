# VOICE: THE NARRATOR OF ARDENMOOR

### The governing voice for The Tower of Ardenmoor, Book One.
### Where this note and the style guide differ, the style guide's restraint wins.
###
### Rewritten 2026-07-31. The previous edition opened by telling you to read it in full and then
### apply a re-weight that superseded most of it, so every drafting session read ~1,500 words of
### a balance it was simultaneously instructed to correct for. Only the live balance is stated
### here. Nothing below is superseded by anything.

---

## THE VOICE

**One blended voice, fused in every paragraph. Not two registers taken in turns.**

- **Le Guin is the floor**, and it is never overruled: name no emotion the reader can infer,
  explain no silence, and when something enormous happens the prose goes *quieter*.
- **Mancour is the surface, everywhere.** The teller is a wry, plainspoken, competent
  professional recounting how it actually went: direct, dry-humoured, a little self-deprecating,
  comfortable in the genre. He tells you what a thing cost, how it was done, and what he thought
  of it, in plain words. The nuts and bolts of the trade, the logistics, the money, the building
  are rendered with relish. A paragraph about how something was done is welcome and needs no
  poetic finish.
- **Rothfuss is the undertone, everywhere** — present in every paragraph, not a grace note saved
  for the big moments. The intimate teller, one well-made concrete image, a quiet music beneath
  the plainness. Everyday narration is therefore never flat mechanism.

What gets cut is *self-admiring* ornament, never the quiet warmth and never the concrete image.
Trim "a sky the pale scrubbed blue of something just laundered" to a cleaner line, but keep the
image ("a pale sky the scrubbed blue of fresh-washed linen"). Do not strip it to a bare adjective.
**A flat, undertone-less paragraph is as wrong as a purple one.** If you ever find yourself
deciding a percentage, stop and listen to the paragraph instead.

**POV** is session-lock #1 (`references/session-locks.md`): Emlyn's chapters first person, past
tense, close to the events with ordinary hindsight, never an ancient chronicler. Other
characters' chapters and the interludes are third-person limited, same blended voice.

**Sentence length.** The wry-plain register runs on shorter, cleaner sentences. Do not chain four
or five independent clauses with "and, and, and". No comma splices. A long cumulative sentence is
allowed on purpose, for rhythm, as the exception rather than the reflex.

**Calibration.** Chapters 1–3 are the reference, read as a single continuous voice: plain, wry and
practical on the surface, warm and closely observed underneath, the same in the kettle-and-
paperwork paragraphs as in the hum scene. At a real threshold the **Ch 3 hum scene is the model** —
the same voice simply *deepens*, the undertone rising nearer the surface, never switching register.

## HOW IT SITS ON THE PAGE

**Attention leads; craft is one of the things attended to.** Sometimes a paragraph is pure
noticing — light, weather, a face, a memory — with no mechanism in it at all, and that is not a
paragraph to cut. When craft is on the page, let the narrator find the moment of grace inside it,
but do not force a "finish" onto every working. This rhythm is *available*, not a mould:

> He set the last anchor at the northeast corner and felt for the deep layer before he committed
> the working's roots, the way you test ice with your weight before you trust it. The hill took
> the shape he asked for the way dry ground takes rain — not gratefully, ground has no gratitude,
> but completely, as though it had been shaped for exactly this and was only now being asked.

Some chapters never do this. A chapter that is a walk, a conversation and a kettle is a whole
chapter if the attention is alive.

**Dialogue stays plain; the narration carries any music.** Never let characters speak lyrically
(except, sparingly, Brenna channelling her grandmother). Jorin says "Even it runs north. Slight."
The beauty is in how Emlyn hears it.

**The register breathes by scene, and you feel it rather than measure it.** Working scenes carry
more concrete craft, with the wonder let in a sentence at a time. Domestic and relationship scenes
are warm and closely observed, the noticing doing the work the speech will not. Grief and
threshold scenes are where the music lives, and where it must be quietest and most withheld. An
other-character chapter carries the same blend tuned to that person's intelligence rather than
Emlyn's: what Lira notices, weighs, and will not say. The interludes belong to no one but the
hill — geologic patience, fragments, no interiority, no craft. That is the one place the teller
falls silent.

## WRITE-TIME TIC BUDGET

Aim at this *while* drafting, not only when fixing afterwards. The teller's signature
constructions are voice at low frequency and mannerism at high. Roughly per chapter: **", which
is/was …" tails ~5** (each must land a judgment or a joke — "which was Jorin knighting him");
**"a good/great deal / a great many" ~3** (elsewhere: *much, far, many, most*, or no measure at
all); **"the way you/a …" similes ~5** (only the vivid); **"I will not pretend / I will not tell
you" ≤1** (usually say the thing directly: "The first ones did not work."); **"in the end" ≤2;
"which is to say" ≤2; "which by then" ≤1.** Never reuse another chapter's distinctive coinage
(`tools/phrase-registry.txt`); deliberate callbacks are a plotted exception, not a habit. Prefer
the period, the colon and the plain declarative to the appositive reflex.

## GUARDRAILS

**The lyric risk:** self-admiring sentences, digression that forgets to return, a protagonist
effortlessly the best at everything. Counter: Emlyn is competent but grief-slowed, occasionally
wrong, and the book's problems are solved by patience and other people, not by his brilliance.
Cut any sentence that admires itself. Keep detours short and earned. Lyricism is a spice, not
the meal.

**The practical risk:** info-dumping the magic system, spreadsheet pacing, deals with no
relationship inside them, characters as function-holders. The built world stays in full; only its
gamified shadow goes. Every rule arrives through action, every transaction is also a
relationship, and the ledger is never the point — the people the ledger describes are.

**The shared trap, over-explaining.** The good pages trust what the weak pages explain. Ardenmoor
trusts. Quote the marginal note; do not gloss it. Show the refilled jug; do not tell us Lira has
softened. Render the compass pointing down; do not explain what it means. The reader assembles
the meaning, and that assembly is the reading experience.

---

## A NORTH-STAR TEST

Read any drafted paragraph and ask: *is the narrator's attention alive here — am I being told
this by someone who saw it and cared?* If yes, the paragraph earns its place, whether it holds a
ward or only a kettle. If it is mechanism with no one behind it, find the teller. If it is lovely
but hollow, pretty with nothing seen or felt, cut it. The voice is a person paying close, patient
attention to a world worth the trouble.
