# THE ECONOMY OF ARDENMOOR — the frame, the model, the calendar

Rewritten 2026-07-27 on the author's reframe. Purpose: keep money, land, rent, and wages
consistent across chapters and *felt* rather than invented.

> **⚠️ THE ONE SENTENCE THIS DOCUMENT EXISTS TO PROTECT:**
> **Emlyn does not have a wealth problem. He has a cashflow problem.**
> He is not a poor man. He owns something genuinely valuable. He simply cannot turn any part of
> it into money on any timescale that helps him. Every economic beat in the book must serve that
> distinction, and no beat may quietly convert it back into poverty.

> **SOURCING CAVEAT.** Research was gathered under a network policy that blocked direct page
> fetches, so figures come from search-result snippets quoting primary/secondary sources, not
> full-text reading. Trend-level figures are well corroborated; specific per-acre figures are
> flagged SOFT. Where a number is my arithmetic rather than a source's, it says so.

## 1. THE FRAME: GOOD LAND, UNDEVELOPED AND REMOTE

**⚠️ NOTHING ON THE ESTATE IS BAD LAND.** No wasteland, no barren ground, no useless bog. Delete
that idea wherever it survives. The soil in the bottoms is *as good as any Emlyn has stood on
since*. There is water everywhere, timber on the slopes, grazing enough on the high ground to keep
a valley in wool, and the wet reach north and west **wants nothing in this world but draining and
has never once been offered it.**

**So why is it nearly free? Because land is worth what it can EARN, and this land cannot earn yet.**

The chain, which is real economics and not a contrivance:

```
land value  =  capitalised net earnings
net earnings =  (yield × price AT MARKET)  −  (cost of production + cost of CARRIAGE)
```

Ardenmoor is **four days on horseback past the railhead at Wainford**, and a loaded wagon needs a
fortnight for what the mails do in a week. For bulk produce, **carriage eats the whole margin.**
Net earnings round to nothing. Therefore the land prices at nothing — *no matter how good the soil
is.* Soil quality is not the variable. **Access is the entire variable.**

This is why frontier land is cheap in every era, and it is well evidenced: the analogous US study
(Donaldson & Hornbeck) found that removing the 1890 rail network would have cut aggregate American
farmland value by **64%**. Access, not fertility.

**Penworth's line, now the thesis of the purchase (Ch 3):** *"Ground is not worth what it will
grow. Ground is worth what it can carry to a market, and every mile between the two comes off the
price."*

**Cael's version, in his own idiom (Ch 2):** *"Land wants two things, and it's the same two things
everywhere I've ever heard of. It wants hands and it wants a road. You've bought yourself a deal
of land with neither."*

→ **Hands and a road.** That is the book's whole economic problem, stated by a farmer in one
breath, and both halves are solvable. That is what makes this a cozy novel about building rather
than a tragedy about ruin.

### What Emlyn actually bought

> *"A man who buys such a place is not buying a farm. He is buying the makings of one, and the
> making is all still in front of him, and it is the making and not the buying that costs."* (Ch 3)

**The purchase price was never the hard part. The DEVELOPMENT is the hard part.** That inversion
is the engine of Book One and it should never be softened.

## 2. ⚠️ THE DEVELOPMENT TRAP (why a rich man has no money)

This is the mechanism that makes "asset-rich, cash-poor" *bite* instead of merely being stated.

**To earn, the land needs:** drainage · fencing and hedging · barns, byres, cottages · a road out ·
and **hands** — families who do not exist in the valley in the numbers required.

**What that costs** (researched, see §5): thorough drainage ~£3/acre · a pair of labourers'
cottages £350 (1892) · a complete farm homestead, house plus cottages, ~£3,900 (1860s). Improving
even a few hundred acres properly is a multi-thousand-pound proposition spread over years.

**And here is the trap:**

| | |
|---|---|
| To get capital | you need **collateral** |
| Collateral means | **improved** property with income and comparable sales |
| Undeveloped remote land is | **not collateral** — no income, no market, no comparables, no lender interest |
| To improve the land | you need **capital** |

**A lender in the metropole will advance money against the hill and the buildings on it, because
those are a thing he can picture and sell. He will not advance a shilling against five thousand
acres of country he has never seen, that earns nothing, four days past the end of the railway.**

→ **This is precisely why Ch 17's doors are shut:** *"The hill was charged twice over and could not
be charged a third time… There was nothing in the bottoms to sell that anybody in that valley had
the money to buy."* Both halves are now rigorous. The bottoms are genuinely valuable and genuinely
unsaleable, because **the only buyers who could pay are four days away and the only people nearby
have no money.** An asset with no local market and no lender interest is not money. It is a
promise.

→ And it is why **Ch 19 must buy labour with CRAFT.** Not because he is poor. Because he is
*illiquid*, and craft is the one asset he can spend the same week he earns it.

## 3. THE PERIOD ANCHOR

Ardenmoor is a secondary world, but the texture should read as **late-Victorian**, with the
**frontier** economics of land beyond a railhead rather than the settled economics of the English
shires. The **Great Depression of British Agriculture (1873–96)** is a useful backdrop for *why
nobody else is buying* and why old holdings were let go, but ⚠️ **do not lean on it to make the
land bad.** The land is not bad. It is unreached.

Useful period facts:
- Cereal acreage fell **22%** (9.43M → 7.4M acres) as land went out of cultivation. Ground going
  back to thistle is the era's signature, and it is **abandonment, not infertility.**
- Over 600 estate sales per year in the 1880s; new commercial wealth buying land off indebted old
  families is the documented signature transaction. Emlyn is a recognisable *type*.
- Rents fell ~30% nationally (£1.705/acre 1870–74 → £1.198 1895–99).

## 4. LAND VALUES UNDER THIS FRAME

| Land | Price/acre | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Good arable **with access** | £20–60 | SOFT. What Ardenmoor's bottoms would fetch *if the rail came* |
| Poor/marginal arable, settled districts | £2–10 | SOFT |
| **Ardenmoor's developed core** | **~£4** | Good soil, derelict, no access, tangled title |
| **Ardenmoor's undeveloped ground** | **8s–13s** | Good in kind, never improved, bought at nominal parish/township price |
| Hard anchor | £6/acre | Bamburgh Castle **plus** 10,000 Northumberland acres, £60,000, 1894 |

**Years' purchase** (price = annual rent × multiple): ~28–33× pre-depression, compressing to ~19×
by the 1890s.

**⚠️ THE VALUATION GAP IS THE STORY.** At ~18s/acre blended, ~5,500 acres cost about £5,000. The
same ground with a road and hands and drains is worth *many times* that. **Emlyn is sitting on the
gap.** He cannot eat it, borrow against it, or sell it locally. He can only close it by building,
slowly, out of income he does not yet have.

## 5. COSTS, WAGES, AND WHAT MONEY FEELS LIKE

| | |
|---|---|
| Agricultural labourer | 10s/wk (1850) → 15–18s/wk (1880s), highly seasonal |
| Mason | ~29s/wk · Carpenter ~25s/wk · Bricklayer ~£2/wk summer |
| Cottage rent | 1s–2s 6d/wk (£4–6/yr) |
| Maid-of-all-work £6–9/yr · Butler £40–100/yr | |
| Loaf of bread | 1–2d |
| "Comfortable" middle-class income | ~£300/yr |
| Thorough drainage | ~£3/acre |
| Pair of labourers' cottages | £350 (1892) |
| Complete farm homestead (house + cottages) | ~£3,900 (1860s) |
| Glasshouse anchors | Kew Palm House £30,000 (362 ft, monumental); Torquay Winter Gardens £12,783 |

**Derived (my arithmetic): the winter crew.** 8–10 men over ~20 weeks ≈ **£280–350 in wages
alone**, before Jorin's fee or materials. *This is the exact size of the hole in Ch 19.*

### Rents and tenancy
| Quality | c.1850–70 | c.1895 |
|---|---|---|
| Good arable | 35–45s/acre | 20–25s |
| Poor upland | 15–20s | 10–15s |
| Rough grazing | a few s to 10s | little changed |

- Historical norm was **fixed cash rent, half-yearly, tenancy-at-will**; English sharecropping was
  so rare that Adam Smith used the French *métayage*. ⚠️ **BUT ARDENMOOR'S CROP-SHARE IS CANON AND
  STAYS** — Ch 16 establishes it as known local practice (*"They had come off a hard sharecropping
  on Maret's sister's place, from a man who counted"*). On a frontier past the railhead, where coin
  is scarce and produce cannot be sold anyway, **share rent is the rational instrument.** It is a
  deliberate frontier divergence, not an error. Emlyn's *fairness* is the contrast, not his format.
- **Improving leases are historically real** and well attested: rent-free or reduced years in
  exchange for reclaiming ground, stepping up once the land is "in heart." **The Wrays' free first
  year is correct as drafted.** Keep.
- No compensation for tenant improvements until the Agricultural Holdings Acts 1875/1883. A tenant
  who improved land could lose it. This is why Bess Wray is wary and why **putting a holding in
  Tam's own name on his own paper is a real gift.**

## 6. THE ACQUISITION (author's two-phase plan)

### Phase 1 — the stealth purchase, financed by a BRIDGE LOAN

**£5,000 short-term loan** from a metropolitan bank, secured on the river house. Period-correct:
private lending of the era really was short-term, interest-only, principal callable on sale. **The
bridge IS the book's grace year** — nothing falls due until the place has had a full turn of the
seasons.

| | Acres | £ | £/acre | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Anchor purchase** | ~2,500 | 3,000 | ~£1 4s | Hill and valley. *Parts* already improved (a few working tenant farms, sound outbuildings), so it reads as a respectable gentleman's retirement buy. Contains essentially **all** the workable ground. |
| **Parish border purchase** | ~3,000 | 2,000 | ~13s 4d | Good rough country never developed: fell, scrub-wood, old grazing gone to bracken, and the undrained wet reach north and west. Bought quietly through the parish registry. **Paperworked**, not bought at market. |
| **Total** | **~5,500** | **£5,000** | **~18s 2d** | |

Anchor internals: ~400 workable-but-derelict @ £4 = £1,600 · ~200 woodland @ £3 = £600 · ~1,900
hill, slopes, rough valley @ 8s = £760. ≈ £2,960.

**⚠️ THE SECRECY POINTS OUTWARD.** Cael negotiated for the families in the open (Ch 1) and
Penworth's agent sat at his table three evenings with a map (Ch 2). **The valley knew.** The
disguise is aimed at rival speculators and the railway company. Do NOT retcon Ch 1–2 into a covert
operation.

### Phase 2 — liquidating the house

Grand, a rich merchant's house, freehold-equivalent **~£12,000** — but on a **ground lease** and
carrying **the bridge plus an older debt**, so it **recovers about half.**

| | £ |
|---|---|
| Net realised | 6,500 |
| Less bridge principal | (5,000) |
| Less fees, solicitors, lease assignment | (300) |
| **Surplus — "a bit of runway"** | **~1,200** |

→ Emlyn emerges **outright, unencumbered owner of ~5,500 acres.**

**⚠️ THE SIX-MONTH CLEARING GAP IS THE LOAD-BEARING DEVICE.** The money does not arrive at once.
Two tranches, per existing canon: **exchange ~£4,000, spring Yr 2 (Ch 12)**; **completion balance
~£2,500, autumn Yr 2 (Ch 16)**. Through that window he is committed to money he does not hold.

## 7. ⚠️ THE CASHFLOW CALENDAR (the real spine)

Money in is **lumpy and seasonal**. Money out is **continuous**. That mismatch, not poverty, is the
book's economic drama. Read down this table before writing any money beat.

| When | IN | OUT | State |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Yr 0 spring** | Bridge £5,000 drawn | Purchase £5,000 | Land owned, nothing developed |
| **Yr 0 sum–aut** | — | Establishment: stone, roof, well, tools, Lira's room | Grace year running |
| **Yr 1** | Lamp trade begins (Ch 8) | Building, living, seed advanced to tenants | Wrays' **free first year** — no rent by design |
| **Yr 1 aut** | — | — | Nothing from the land yet, *as agreed* |
| **Yr 2 spring** | House **exchange ~£4,000** | Straight at the bridge principal | First real relief |
| **Yr 2 sum** | Lamps | **First note payment** | Survivable *because* of the exchange money |
| **Yr 2 aut** | Harvest (meagre: newly broken ground) + customary rents + **completion ~£2,500** | **Second note payment in full** · then glasshouse **materials** | **ELEVEN SHILLINGS.** Nothing toward labour |
| **Yr 2 aut** | *Expected: arrears* | — | ⚠️ **The arrears were the labour plan. They collapse (Ch 17).** |
| **Yr 2 winter** | — | Glasshouse **labour ~£300** | No instrument left → **buy labour with CRAFT (Ch 19)** |

### Why the £1,200 surplus does not rescue him — all four must hold
1. **Earmarked and spent before the glasshouse exists as a plan** — house, timber outbuildings,
   well, stock, tools, seed, living. Canon already has the tower rising *one careful room at a time*.
2. **It is not one pot.** Two tranches six months apart; the first is consumed by the bridge. The
   £1,200 only exists *after* the completion balance lands, by which point materials are committed.
3. **Materials came from the harvest, not the house** (canon).
4. **Every credit door is shut** (Ch 17): hill charged twice, patents leant on, lamps are the
   winter's bread not a wage bill, crop and house sold and spent, and per §2 **no lender will take
   undeveloped remote land**.

→ **The hole is LABOUR and only labour.** ~£300 of winter wages, with every instrument spent.

### The arrears collapse (Ch 17) — keep the two numbers
- **On paper:** 18 months × 5 inherited tenancies at old customary rates ≈ **£280–320.** "A real
  amount of money. Not a fortune. Enough." Enough, exactly, for the winter crew.
- **Realisable:** Pardon pays in kind; the widow has nothing; Ferris pays "slowly, partly, and with
  a grievance"; Bewick's coin he refuses; Hask he forgives. **≈£20–25.**
- **The gap between those two numbers IS the chapter.** Match the paper figure to the wage bill.

### The glasshouse
160 ft × 5 bays: **materials £800–1,200, labour £300–400, total ~£1,200–1,600.** Against £60–100/yr
of land income and eleven shillings in the box, correctly *ruinous to attempt* — though note the
distinction the book must hold: **it would not bankrupt a man with his assets; it would strand
him.** He can lose everything liquid and still own five thousand acres. That is worse in a
different way, and more interesting.

## 8. ⚠️ WHAT ACTUALLY PAYS IN BOOK ONE (value density, not acreage)

**⚠️ NO ACCESS ARRIVES IN BOOK ONE. NO ROAD TO MARKET GETS BUILT. THE LAND NEVER STARTS PAYING.**
A man who cannot fund £300 of winter wages cannot build a road to a railhead four days off, and any
beat implying otherwise is false to the constraint the whole book runs on.

So how does he survive? **He stops trying to move bulk and starts moving value.**

If carriage eats the margin on anything heavy (§1), then the only goods that can cross four days of
bad road profitably are the ones that are **small, light, and worth a great deal** — or that never
travel at all. Every income stream in Book One is one of those, and this is not a coincidence:

| Stream | Why it works past the railhead |
|---|---|
| **Lumens / the lamp trade** (Ch 8) | Crystalline goods. Enormous value per pound. A saddlebag is a season's income |
| **Patents and licences** | **Weightless.** Pure information. Crosses the four days for the price of postage and pays quarterly forever |
| **Wardings, the practice, craft** | Performed on site, paid on site, frequently **in kind**. Carriage never enters into it |
| **Glasshouse output** | Early and out-of-season produce sold **locally** at a premium; and propagated stock, seed and cuttings, which are **low bulk and high value** |
| **Timber, grain, wool, livestock** | ⚠️ **These cannot pay.** Too heavy. This is the whole problem |

**⚠️ THIS MAKES THE GLASSHOUSE RATIONAL, NOT RECKLESS — and that is a load-bearing reversal.**
The valley thinks he is mad to raise a palace of glass in a district that cannot sell its grain.
He is in fact building **the only large productive asset in that valley whose output can either
command a premium without leaving, or leave in a saddlebag.** Canon already supports both markets:
Kellard takes haulage against **first refusal on the early crop** (local), and Jorin's crew defer
half their wage against **the glasshouse's first two selling seasons** (it sells, and reliably
enough to be collateral between neighbours).

→ Penworth is right about the **risk** (*"you will lose Ardenmoor. Not embarrassment. The hill."*).
Emlyn is right about the **strategy**. Both can be true, and the book is better if they are.

### Ch 20 "Good Roads" — ⚠️ NOT an economic unlock
The cart-road runs **from the hill through Emlyn's OWN woodland to the existing main road**, with a
branch into the village. It is short, local, entirely on ground he already owns, built with
**quarry stone and bartered labour** on the same craft economy as the glasshouse. **Wainford is
still four days away afterward and nothing about the estate's economics changes.**

Its two real justifications:
1. **It is the last mile of the build he already committed to** — glasshouse output has to come
   down and materials have to go up.
2. **The branch to the village has no economic justification whatever.** That is precisely why
   Lira notices it: *"That's not a path to a wizard's tower. That's a road. To the village."*
   **It is a gift, and it must cost him something to be one.**

## 9. LATENT ASSETS — ⚠️ BOOKS TWO AND THREE, NOT BOOK ONE

Real value Emlyn already owns and **cannot reach within this book.** Naming them is the payoff
structure of the series; *reaching* any of them in Book One would break the premise.

1. **The bottoms.** Good soil. Needs hands, drains, fences.
2. **The undrained reach north and west.** Wants draining and nothing else (~£3/acre historically).
3. **The woodland.** Mature oak, ash, a planted larch stand. Real money **the day a road can carry
   it out** — and that day is not in Book One.
4. **The high ground / sporting value.** Grouse let for serious money by the 1880s. Blocked because
   sporting tenants are city men and the valley is four days past the railhead.
5. **⚠️ THE RAILWAY CHOKE-HOLD.** As assembled, the holding commands the ground any rail extension
   past Wainford must cross. **The master key: it converts every item above at once** — which is
   exactly why it cannot turn in Book One.

### ⚠️ OPEN AUTHOR DECISION — who knew about the railway?
- **(A) EMERGENT (recommended).** Nobody planned it. Penworth buys elbow room because that is what
  Penworth does — his own canon line is *"a wise man buys his neighbors' patience before he has
  done anything to spend it."* It turns out to be a choke-hold. **No retcon; Ch 1–3 stand.**
- **(B) PENWORTH KNEW.** He quietly bought his grieving client a fortune and said nothing. Moving
  as a late reveal, but makes him a solicitor trading on undisclosed information.
- **(C) EMLYN KNEW.** ⚠️ Contradicts Ch 1–3 outright and converts a cozy opening into a speculation
  thriller. Not recommended.

Until settled, the choke-hold is **latent fact, UNSTATED on the page.** It costs nothing to leave
unsaid and cannot be un-said.

## 10. CONTINUITY RULES (check every money beat against these)

1. **Never write Emlyn as poor.** He is *illiquid*. "No money" always means **no money right now**,
   never "no worth." Ch 19 has the model line: *"I had no money and I had a great deal else."*
2. **Never write the land as bad.** Undeveloped, unreached, unimproved, gone back, wanting drains
   or hands or a road. Never barren, useless, worthless, waste. (The **hill** alone is genuinely
   rocky, and that is one hill and a running joke.)
3. **Poor yields must come from newly broken ground or weather, never from bad soil.**
4. **Every closed door must close for a stated, rigorous reason** — no lender interest in
   undeveloped remote land; no local buyer with cash; collateral already charged.
5. **Value realised must always cost time or craft, never luck.**
6. **Money in is lumpy and seasonal; money out is continuous.** Any beat that forgets this reads
   false.
7. **⚠️ NONE OF THESE FIGURES GO ON THE PAGE.** The book's money discipline works because it is
   rendered in *felt* units: *eleven shillings and some copper*, *a cheese and a fleece*, *a few
   pounds a quarter*, *a tenth*, *a fifth*. Characters may name small sums they would actually say
   aloud. **The narration must never total the estate.** This document exists so the small sums are
   right.
