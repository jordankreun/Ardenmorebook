## 1. THE PERIOD ANCHOR

Ardenmoor is a secondary world, but its economy should read as **late-Victorian Britain in the
Great Depression of British Agriculture, c. 1873–1896.** This is the single most useful decision
in this document, because that depression makes almost everything the book already does
historically *normal* instead of contrived:

| What the book needs | What the depression supplies |
|---|---|
| Land absurdly cheap | Rents fell ~30% nationally; capital values fell 40–50%+; worst districts unsellable |
| Fields gone back to thistle | Cereal acreage fell **22%** (9.43M → 7.4M acres). Land going out of cultivation IS the story of the era |
| Owners who stopped troubling | Over 600 estate sales/year in the 1880s; "widespread ruin and bankruptcy" among arable farmers |
| A stranger buying up a valley | New professional/commercial wealth buying cheapened estates off indebted gentry is the documented signature phenomenon of the 1880s–90s |
| Tenants holding on with both hands | Landlords granted abatements rather than lose tenants; tenant-right agitation was live politics |

**Corollary:** Emlyn is not an eccentric. He is a recognisable *type* — new money buying land at
the bottom of the market. The valley would know that type. Cael's wariness in Ch 1 is exactly
what such a man would meet.

## 2. LAND PRICES (purchase)

| Quality | Pre-depression (1850s–70s) | Post-depression (1890s) | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Good arable / lowland | £40–60/acre | £20–35 | SOFT |
| Medium mixed | £25–40 | £12–20 | SOFT |
| Poor / marginal arable | £10–20 | **£2–10** (some literally unsellable) | SOFT |
| Rough moor, fell, heather, waste | £1–5/acre, sometimes under £1 | little changed | SOFT |

**The one hard anchor found:** **Bamburgh Castle plus a 10,000-acre Northumberland estate sold in
1894 for £60,000 — £6/acre blended, castle included.** Use this as the reality check on any
whole-estate price.

**Years' purchase** (the period's valuation metric — price = annual rent × multiple):
~28–33× pre-depression, compressing to **~19×** by 1894–1900. Because rent AND the multiple both
fell, capital values fell much further than rents alone. This is why "land values fell by
two-thirds 1870–1914" and "rents fell 30%" are both true.

**⚠️ CORRECTION TO AN EARLIER ASSUMPTION:** rough upland is NOT free. At £1–5/acre, 1,800 acres
is £1,800–9,000 — potentially more than a small developed core. **The mechanism that makes it
cheap is not price but TENURE.** Crown and parish waste was disposed of at *nominal* price; the
buyer pays **fees, not price**. In a documented enclosure award the physical works (fencing,
roads, drains) were ~42% of total cost — so regularising title on open moor you do not intend to
fence skips nearly half the expense. Survey, Act, and commissioners' costs only.

> **This is exactly what Ch 2 already says:** Penworth "sat down with the parish." Keep it.
> The developed land is *bought*. The moor is *paperworked*.

**Remoteness discount:** no UK figure found. The analogous US study (Donaldson & Hornbeck) found
removing the 1890 rail network would have cut aggregate farmland value **64%**. For fiction,
a 20–40% discount for land a day's cart beyond a railhead is defensible. MY INFERENCE, not sourced.

**Legal climate note:** after the Commons Preservation Society (1865) and the Commons Act 1876,
enclosing common waste got *harder* and required proof of public benefit. A man regularising
1,800 acres of common in the 1880s is doing something contestable. Penworth's thoroughness —
three evenings, a map, a sharpening pencil — is not fussiness. It is the only way it works.

## 3. RENTS (annual, per acre)

| Quality | c.1850–70 | c.1895 |
|---|---|---|
| Good arable | 35–45s | 20–25s |
| Mixed | 25–35s | 18–25s |
| Poor upland | 15–20s | 10–15s |
| Rough grazing / moor | a few shillings to ~10s | little changed |

National average: **£1.705/acre (1870–74) → £1.198/acre (1895–99)**, −29.7% (Gregory Clark's
charity-land series; independently corroborated by F.M.L. Thompson at −30%). Individual estates
did far worse: Everleigh, Wiltshire **−48%**; Box estate **−52%**.

**The ratio that carries the whole premise: good arable rents ~10× what moor rents.**

### Tenancy practice — ⚠️ ONE REAL CONFLICT WITH THE BOOK
- **Norm was fixed CASH rent, paid half-yearly, on annual tenancy-at-will.** Not leases.
- **Sharecropping was essentially unknown in England.** Adam Smith used the French *métayage*
  because share tenancy had no English practice to name. A marginal variant ("farming to halves")
  existed but historians call it hidden and rare.
  → **Ardenmoor's "a tenth, then a fifth" would read as archaic or foreign.** THIS IS A CHOICE,
  NOT AN ERROR. Ardenmoor is four days past the railhead under "the old loose title of the far
  country." A frontier running on customary share-rent while the metropole runs on cash is
  characterful and consistent with Ned Pardon paying a cheese and a fleece to nobody for eleven
  years. **Decision pending with the author.** Option: inherited occupiers pay in customary kind
  (archaic — which is the point of a 20-year-stale rent book) while Emlyn's NEW agreements are
  cash, showing him modernising.
- **Improving leases ARE real and well attested.** Rent-free or reduced years granted in exchange
  for reclaiming waste, rent stepping up once the ground is "in heart." **The Wrays' free first
  year is historically correct.** Keep it exactly as drafted.
- No compensation for tenant improvements until the Agricultural Holdings Acts 1875 (toothless)
  and 1883 (mandatory). A tenant who improved land could lose it. This is why Bess Wray is wary,
  and why putting a holding in Tam's *own name on his own paper* is a genuine gift.

## 5. ESTATE SCALE AND STATUS

Bateman's classification (the standard reference, from the 1873 *Return of Owners of Land*):

| Class | Acres | Income |
|---|---|---|
| Great landowner | 10,000+ | £10,000+ |
| **Squire** | **1,000–3,000** (avg ~1,700) | £1,000–3,000 |
| Greater yeoman / minor gentry | 300–1,000 | £300–1,000 |
| Lesser yeoman | 100–300 | — |
| Small proprietor | under 100 | — |

**⚠️ AT ~2,000 ACRES EMLYN IS SQUIRE-TIER BY ACREAGE.** Not near-gentry: gentry, on paper. 4,217
owners of 1,000+ acres held over half of England and Wales. This is a *status* fact the valley
would register instantly, and it sharpens the book's central irony: he is, on any document, one
of the larger landowners in the district, and he has eleven shillings.
→ Ch 2's drafted line "more land than the squire down at Wainford" is right in letter but should
carry that weight deliberately.

**Upland estate composition:** typical Pennine/Welsh/Highland estate ran **30–40% enclosed
in-bye land, 60–70% moor and rough grazing.** Ardenmoor at ~10% workable is at the extreme low
end — *which the depression explains*: this is not virgin moor, it is **abandoned farmland**.
Ground that was worked once and went back. That is historically exact for the period.

**Farm sizes:** ~70% of English and Welsh farms were under 50 acres (Caird's analysis of the 1851
census). Typical lowland tenant family farm 50–150 acres; upland smallholdings well under 50.
→ Wrays at 80 acres = a solid upland family farm. Tam at 24 = a smallholding a young man can
just about start on. Bewick at 10 = a cottager's plot. **All three are correctly sized.**

**Grouse moors — the latent asset.** By the 1880s moorland let for shooting paid better than
sheep; Highland deer forests grew from 73 to 110 (≈2M acres) in a decade precisely because
sporting rents beat agriculture on marginal ground. Guisachan: 22,000 acres whose *sixteen
agricultural tenants* paid £692/year total.
→ **Ardenmoor's moor has real latent sporting value that cannot be realised because sporting
tenants are city men and the valley is four days past the railhead.** It unlocks the moment
access improves. **Ch 20 is titled "Good Roads."** This is an economic engine already sitting
in the outline.

## 6. THE CITY HOUSE

| | Price | Date |
|---|---|---|
| Belgravia, freehold | ~£25,000 | 1858 |
| Queen's Gate, South Kensington (avg sale) | ~£5,050 | 1860 |
| Leighton House, Holland Park (purpose-built) | £4,500 | 1866 |

**So yes — a grand metropolitan house is worth a great deal.** And the documented pattern is
precisely that a London house sold for £15,000–25,000 could buy several thousand acres of
depressed land by the 1890s. **The author's instinct was right.**

**But two mechanics stop this from making Emlyn rich:**

1. **⚠️ LEASEHOLD.** Roughly **two-thirds of London residential property was leasehold**, on
   **99-year** ground leases from the great estates (Grosvenor, Cadogan, Portman, Bedford). A
   leaseholder does not own a house; he owns **the unexpired residue of a term**, pays ground
   rent throughout, and watches the asset waste. Value holds above ~80 years remaining, then
   depreciates at an accelerating rate; below ~20 years it collapses toward nil. Leases also
   carried **repairing covenants** enforceable by the estate's agents.
   → **This is the answer.** A grand river house on a lease with ~30 years left, run down through
   three years of neglect and subject to a repairing covenant, is worth a *fraction* of its
   freehold equivalent, and is *hard to assign*. The house can be magnificent and the proceeds
   modest, with no contrivance whatever. The leasehold system was so resented it produced an
   organised enfranchisement movement, an 1889 Bill, and a Royal Commission.
   → It also explains the existing canon line that the house is **"eating money he can't stop
   spending"**: ground rent falls due whether or not he crosses the threshold, and the repairing
   covenant means neglect is a *liability*, not a saving. (Note: Inhabited House Duty taxed
   *occupied* dwellings, so an empty house escaped that one — the drain is ground rent and
   covenant, not tax.)
2. **Owner-occupation was rare.** ~90% of households RENTED by 1900; only ~10% owned. A
   professional man who owns **two** houses is genuinely unusual — worth knowing for how the
   valley reads him, and how Penworth thinks about liquidating.

## 7. THE ARDENMOOR MODEL (recommended)

All figures are for the author's and the engine's consistency. **NONE OF THEM GO ON THE PAGE.**

### The purchase, ~2,000 acres

| Component | Acres | Basis | ~Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Workable but derelict poor upland arable | ~200 | £8/acre, depression + remoteness | £1,600 |
| Woodland (oak, ash, planted larch) | ~100 | standing timber has real value | £800 |
| Rough moor, fell, common, waste | ~1,700 | parish/Crown at nominal price + survey, Act, commissioners | £700 |
| Title clearing, agent, the elbow-room parcels | — | Penworth's three evenings | £400 |
| **Total** | **~2,000** | **≈£1 15s/acre blended** | **~£3,500** |

Sanity check: Bamburgh went at £6/acre *with a castle*. Ardenmoor at ~£1.75/acre — derelict,
tangled, four days past the railhead, at the trough — is aggressive but defensible.

### The funding

| Source | ~Amount |
|---|---|
| Appreciated holdings (cash down) | £1,200 |
| Loan secured on already-licensed patents | £2,600 |
| River house — *promise* of sale set against the price | (lands mid-book) |
| **Raised** | **~£3,800** vs ~£3,500 purchase + moving and first works |

→ "Cheap, and it still took very nearly everything I could raise" becomes literally true.

**River house:** grand, leasehold, ~30 years unexpired, neglected three years, repairing covenant.
Freehold-equivalent £12,000–18,000; **realisable ~£1,800–2,500.** Completes spring Yr 2, goes
straight at principal — which is why the second note payment is survivable.

### The clock

| | ~£/year |
|---|---|
| Note (interest + amortisation on ~£2,600) | **£150–200** |
| Land income Yr 2 (≈200 acres, mostly derelict, free-first-year tenants, customary rents in kind) | **£30–60** |
| Patent royalties ("a few pounds a quarter") | £15–20 |
| **The gap the trade must close** | **~£80–150** |

**This is the engine of the book.** The land never pays for itself and was never going to. Lamps,
patents, and the practice close the gap. It is why the tower rises one room at a time.

### The arrears collapse (Ch 17)

- **On paper:** 18 months × 5 inherited tenancies at old customary rates ≈ **£140–160.** "A real
  amount of money. Not a fortune. Enough." Enough, specifically, to put Jorin's crew on the steel
  for a winter (§4: ~£125–150 in wages).
- **Realisable:** Pardon pays in kind (a cheese, a fleece); the widow has nothing; Ferris pays
  "slowly, partly, and with a grievance"; Bewick's coin he refuses; Hask he forgives.
  **≈£10–15** — which does not pay a crew for a fortnight.
- **The two numbers are the chapter.** Keep them the same size as the labour bill.

### The glasshouse

Reference points: Kew Palm House £30,000 (monumental, 362 ft); Torquay Winter Gardens £12,783.
No mid-size commercial figure could be sourced. For a 160 ft × 5-bay range: **materials
£300–500, labour £125–200, total ~£500–700.** Against £30–60/year of land income and eleven
shillings in the box, that is correctly *bankrupting*.

The 1845 repeal of the glass excise (and 1851 window tax) is well attested as transformative:
larger panes became affordable and glasshouses came within reach of the middle classes. **The
world needs an equivalent event** — it is why Emlyn can even contemplate this, and why the
valley thinks he is mad rather than impossible.

## 8. THE DIALS

1. **How derelict?** 200 workable of 2,000 is extreme (historical upland norm is 30–40%
   improved). Justified by "abandoned, not virgin." Raising it toward 300–400 makes Ardenmoor
   more ordinary and Emlyn less desperate.
2. **Share-rent or cash?** See §3. Currently share (ahistorical, characterful). Alternatives:
   convert to cash, or split old/new.
3. **Leasehold or mortgage** on the river house. Leasehold is more period-accurate, more
   specific, and better explains the existing "eating money" canon. Mortgage also works and is
   simpler. They can coexist.
4. **Note size.** £150 (survivable, land covers a third) vs £200+ (strangling, land covers a
   fifth). Strangling makes Ch 17 hit harder.
5. **When the moor's sporting value surfaces.** Latent now; "Good Roads" is the natural unlock.

## 9. WHAT NEVER GOES ON THE PAGE

The book's money discipline works because it is rendered in **felt** units: *eleven shillings and
some copper*, *a cheese and a fleece*, *a few pounds a quarter*, *a tenth*, *a fifth*. A reader
told "£3,500" stops feeling and starts auditing.

**Rule: characters may name small sums they would actually say aloud. The narration must never
total the estate.** This document is scaffolding. It exists so the small sums are *right*.
